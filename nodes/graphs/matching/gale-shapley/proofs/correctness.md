---
title: Gale-Shapley returns the proposer-optimal stable matching
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

Fix an input: proposers $P$, receivers $R$, and strict lists without repeats. A pair $pr$ is *acceptable* if $r$ is on the list of $p$ and $p$ is on the list of $r$. Write $r >_p r'$ if $p$ ranks $r$ above $r'$, and $p >_r p'$ similarly. Say that $p$ *proposes to* $r$ when line L7 runs with these values, and that $r$ *rejects* $p$ when line L8 or L11 pushes $p$ after its proposal to $r$, or when line L10 pushes $p$ as the old holder of $r$.

**Claim 1 (states).** At the start of each pass of line L4, every proposer is in exactly one of three states: on the stack $F$ once, held by exactly one receiver, or *done*, popped in line L6 with its list exhausted. Every holder relation $\text{holder}[r] = p$ is an acceptable pair. *Proof.* Initially all proposers are on $F$ and nothing is held. A pass pops $p$ from $F$; line L6 makes it done; line L8 or L11 puts it back on $F$; line L9 makes it held by $r$, which held nobody; line L10 makes it held by $r$ and moves the old holder of $r$ from held to $F$. Lines L9 and L10 run only after line L8 has checked that $p$ is on the list of $r$, and $r$ is on the list of $p$ by line L7. $\square$

**Claim 2 (receivers trade up).** Once $\text{holder}[r] \ne \text{NIL}$, it stays so, and each new value is ranked higher by $r$ than the previous one. *Proof.* Only lines L9 and L10 change $\text{holder}[r]$; line L9 needs it to be NIL, and line L10 needs $\text{rank}_r(p) < \text{rank}_r(\text{holder}[r])$. $\square$

**Claim 3 (proposals are remembered).** If $p$ has proposed to $r$ and $pr$ is acceptable, then from that moment on $r$ holds $p$ or a proposer $p'$ with $p' >_r p$. *Proof.* Right after the proposal, $r$ holds $p$ (lines L9, L10) or a proposer it prefers to $p$ (line L11). By Claim 2, the holder of $r$ only improves later. $\square$

**Claim 4 (proposals in order).** Each proposer proposes to the receivers on its list in the order of the list, each at most once. *Proof.* Line L7 takes the entry at position $\text{next}[p]$ and increments it, and nothing else changes $\text{next}[p]$. $\square$

**Termination.** Each pass of the loop pops one entry of $F$. Entries are pushed once initially for each proposer, and afterwards only in lines L8, L10 and L11, at most once per proposal. By Claim 4 there are at most $m$ proposals, where $m$ is the total length of the lists of $P$. So there are at most $|P| + m$ passes.

**The output is a stable matching.** At the end $F$ is empty, so by Claim 1 every proposer is held by exactly one receiver or is done, and each receiver holds at most one proposer. So the pairs returned in line L12 form a matching $M$ of acceptable pairs. Let $pr$ be an acceptable pair not in $M$, and suppose $p$ is single in $M$ or $r >_p M(p)$. If $p$ is single, it is done and has proposed to every receiver on its list, including $r$. If $p$ is matched, $M(p)$ is the receiver of its last proposal, which comes after $r$ on its list, so by Claim 4 $p$ proposed to $r$ before. In both cases Claim 3 says that $r$ holds at the end a proposer $p' \ne p$ with $p' >_r p$. So $r$ is not single and prefers its partner, and $pr$ does not block $M$.

**Claim 5 (no stable partner rejects).** Call $r$ a *stable partner* of $p$ if $pr$ belongs to some stable matching. In every run, no proposer is rejected by one of its stable partners. *Proof.* Suppose not, and consider the first rejection in the run of some $p$ by a stable partner $r$; let $S$ be a stable matching with $pr \in S$. The pair is acceptable, so the rejection happens in line L10 or L11, and at that moment $r$ holds or takes a proposer $p'$ with $p' >_r p$, and $p'$ has proposed to $r$. By Claim 4, $p'$ has proposed to, and been rejected by, every receiver it ranks above $r$, and all these rejections happened earlier. By the choice of the first rejection, none of these receivers is a stable partner of $p'$, so $S(p')$ is not one of them. Also $S(p') \ne r$ because $S(r) = p \ne p'$. So in $S$ the proposer $p'$ is single or has a partner it ranks below $r$; and $r$ has partner $p$, which it ranks below $p'$. The pair $p'r$ is acceptable, because $p'$ proposed to $r$ and $r$ preferred $p'$ to an acceptable proposer, so $p'$ is on its list. Hence $p'r$ blocks $S$, a contradiction. $\square$

**Proposer-optimality.** For each $p$, let $\text{best}(p)$ be the stable partner of $p$ that $p$ ranks highest, if one exists. By Claim 5 and Claim 4, $p$ is never rejected by $\text{best}(p)$, so it never proposes beyond $\text{best}(p)$, and its final partner, if any, is $\text{best}(p)$ or ranked higher. The output $M$ is stable, so $M(p)$, if it exists, is a stable partner of $p$, hence ranked at most as high as $\text{best}(p)$. So $M(p) = \text{best}(p)$. If $p$ has no stable partner, then $M(p)$ cannot exist, and $p$ is single. So $M$ is determined by the input alone, whatever the order of the stack.

**Receiver-pessimality.** Let $S$ be stable and $r$ a receiver with $M(r) = p$. If $r$ is single in $S$ or $p >_r S(r)$, then since $S(p) \ne r$ and $r = \text{best}(p)$, the proposer $p$ is single in $S$ or ranks $r$ above $S(p)$, so the acceptable pair $pr$ blocks $S$, a contradiction. Hence every receiver matched in $M$ has in $S$ a partner it ranks at least as high as $M(r)$. $\blacksquare$
