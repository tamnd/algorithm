The clique percolation method (CPM) finds overlapping communities in an undirected graph. Fix an integer $k \ge 2$. Two $k$-[cliques](/clique/) are *adjacent* when they share $k - 1$ vertices, and a $k$-clique community is the union of all $k$-cliques that can reach each other through a chain of adjacent $k$-cliques. A vertex can lie in several communities, and a vertex in no $k$-clique lies in none. Palla, Derényi, Farkas and Vicsek proposed it in 2005, and it was one of the first methods for [community detection](/community-detection/) with overlaps. The method is exact, with no objective to optimize, but the number of cliques can grow exponentially with $k$.

## In plain words

A clique is a group in which everyone knows everyone. Pick a size, say three. Now imagine a triangle of three friends rolling over the network: it may move by swapping one person for another, as long as the new three people still all know each other. The two people who stay are its pivot.

Everyone the rolling triangle can reach forms one community. The triangle cannot cross a single bridge person, because a move must keep two people fixed. So two dense groups that meet at only one person stay apart, and that person belongs to both. This is how the method allows overlap. People in no triangle at all are left out.

A larger size gives fewer, tighter communities.

## The problem

The input is a simple undirected graph $G = (V, E)$ without weights, with $V = \{0, \ldots, n-1\}$ given by adjacency lists, and an integer $k \ge 2$. The graph need not be connected.

Let $\mathcal{K}_k$ be the set of $k$-cliques of $G$, the vertex sets of size $k$ that are pairwise adjacent. Two $k$-cliques $A, B$ are adjacent when $|A \cap B| = k - 1$. Let $\sim$ be the equivalence relation on $\mathcal{K}_k$ generated by adjacency: $A \sim B$ when there is a chain $A = C_0, C_1, \ldots, C_r = B$ with $C_{i-1}$ and $C_i$ adjacent. The output is the family of sets

$$\Bigl\{\ \textstyle\bigcup_{C \in \mathcal{Q}} C \ : \ \mathcal{Q} \text{ a class of } \sim \Bigr\},$$

the $k$-clique communities, one for each class. Two communities can share vertices, even $k - 1$ or more of them, but no $(k-1)$-set that is a face of a $k$-clique of each, since those two cliques would be adjacent.

**Ties.** There are none: the output is a fixed family of sets. **Disconnected graphs.** A chain never leaves a connected component, so each community lies in one component.

## The algorithm

```pseudo id=main
CPM(G, k)
L1  C ← the list of all k-cliques of G
L2  make a union-find structure with one set {c} for each index c of C
L3  H ← an empty hash map from (k − 1)-sets of vertices to indices of C
L4  for each index c of C do
L5      for each vertex x ∈ C[c] do
L6          F ← C[c] \ {x}                       ▷ a face of the clique
L7          if F ∈ H then UNION(c, H[F]) else H[F] ← c
L8  for each class Q of the union-find do
L9      output the union of the cliques C[c] with c ∈ Q
```

Two $k$-cliques are adjacent exactly when they have a common face, a $(k-1)$-subset. Line L7 joins each clique with the first clique seen with the same face, so all cliques with that face end in one class. This replaces a test of all pairs of cliques by one pass over the $k N$ faces, with a [hash table](/hash-table/) and a [union-find](/disjoint-set/) structure.

Line L1 can use any method that lists $k$-cliques. A simple one extends each clique by a common neighbour with a larger number, kept on an explicit stack. Chiba and Nishizeki list all $k$-cliques in $O(k\, a(G)^{k-2}\, m)$ time, where $a(G)$ is the [arboricity](/arboricity/).

## Example

The graph has the vertices 0 to 7 and the 12 edges of the clique on $\{0, 1, 2, 3\}$, plus $\{3,4\}$, $\{3,5\}$, $\{4,5\}$, $\{4,6\}$, $\{5,6\}$ and $\{6,7\}$. Take $k = 3$. Line L1 lists six triangles, here in the order of the stack-based search.

| $c$ | $C[c]$ | its faces | line L7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | $\{0, 2, 3\}$ | 23, 03, 02 | all new |
| 1 | $\{0, 1, 3\}$ | 13, 03, 01 | 03 is the face of clique 0, so join 1 with 0 |
| 2 | $\{0, 1, 2\}$ | 12, 02, 01 | 02 and 01 are known, join 2 with 0 |
| 3 | $\{1, 2, 3\}$ | 23, 13, 12 | all known, already joined |
| 4 | $\{3, 4, 5\}$ | 45, 35, 34 | all new |
| 5 | $\{4, 5, 6\}$ | 56, 46, 45 | 45 is the face of clique 4, so join 5 with 4 |

The classes are $\{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ and $\{4, 5\}$, and the communities are $\{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ and $\{3, 4, 5, 6\}$. Vertex 3 is in both: the triangles $\{0, 1, 3\}$ and $\{3, 4, 5\}$ share only vertex 3, so no chain joins them. Vertex 7 is in no triangle and in no community.

With $k = 4$, the only 4-clique is $\{0, 1, 2, 3\}$, which is the only community. With $k = 2$, the cliques are the edges and the communities are the connected components that have an edge, here the whole set $\{0, \ldots, 7\}$. With $k = 5$ there are none.

## Correctness

**Theorem.** CPM$(G, k)$ stops and outputs exactly the $k$-clique communities of $G$, each once.

Two $k$-cliques that are adjacent share a face, and the first clique with that face is a common partner of both in line L7, so they end in the same class. Conversely, every UNION joins two cliques with a common face, which are adjacent. So the classes of the union-find are the classes of $\sim$, and line L9 outputs their unions, one for each class. ## Complexity

The cost is in the [word RAM](/word-ram/) model, with hashing in $O(k)$ expected time per $(k-1)$-set.

- **Listing.** Line L1 takes $O(k\, a(G)^{k-2}\, m)$ time with the method of Chiba and Nishizeki. The number $N$ of $k$-cliques is at most $\binom{n}{k}$, and at most $O(a(G)^{k-2}\, m)$.
- **Faces and unions.** Lines L4 to L7 look at $kN$ faces, each built and hashed in $O(k)$, so $O(k^2 N)$ expected time, plus $kN$ union-find operations, almost $O(1)$ each.
- **Output.** Line L9 writes at most $kN$ vertices.

The time is $O(k\, a(G)^{k-2}\, m + k^2 N)$ in expectation. On a graph without $k$-cliques, it is the cost of the search, at least $\Theta(n + m)$. The space is $O(n + m + kN)$ for the cliques and the hash map. In the worst case, such as a dense graph, $N$ is exponential in $k$, and so is the cost. For $k = 3$ the method takes $O(m^{3/2})$ time, the cost of listing triangles with the [compact-forward algorithm](/compact-forward-algorithm/).

## Properties

- **Undirected and without weights.** Directions and weights are ignored; the variants below add them.
- **Deterministic.** The communities depend only on $G$ and $k$, not on any order or seed. Only the order of the output changes.
- **Not recursive.** The clique search uses an explicit stack, and the union-find works with loops.
- **Overlapping, with unassigned vertices.** A vertex can be in several communities, or in none. Vertices of degree less than $k - 1$ are never in a community.
- **Monotone in $k$.** Each $(k+1)$-clique community lies inside one $k$-clique community, since the $k$-subsets of adjacent $(k+1)$-cliques are joined by chains.

## Variants and improvements

- **Through maximal cliques.** The original program CFinder lists the [maximal cliques](/maximal-clique-enumeration/) of size at least $k$ and joins two of them when they share at least $k - 1$ vertices; the components give the same communities. Every $k$-clique lies in a maximal clique, the $k$-cliques inside one maximal clique are joined by chains (swap one vertex at a time), and two maximal cliques that share $k - 1$ vertices $S$ contain the adjacent $k$-cliques $S \cup \{x\}$ and $S \cup \{y\}$. This is faster when the maximal cliques are few and large.
- **Weighted graphs.** Farkas and co-authors keep only the $k$-cliques whose geometric mean of edge weights passes a threshold.
- **Directed graphs.** Palla and co-authors define directed $k$-cliques, which must be acyclic with a fixed order, and percolate them in the same way.
- **Choice of $k$.** Palla and co-authors choose $k$ and, for weighted networks, an edge weight threshold, so that the network stays just below the point where one giant community appears. In $G(n, p)$, Derényi, Palla and Vicsek show that a giant $k$-clique community appears at $p_c(k) = [(k-1)n]^{-1/(k-1)}$, which for $k = 2$ is the threshold $1/n$ of the [giant component](/erdos-renyi-giant-component-theorem/) of the [Erdős-Rényi model](/erdos-renyi-model/).
- **Faster methods.** Later methods find the communities for all $k$ at once from the maximal cliques, or avoid listing all cliques on large sparse graphs.

## When to use it

Use it when communities may overlap, as in social networks where a person belongs to family, work and sport groups, and when the communities are dense enough to be made of cliques. It also leaves out vertices that belong to no dense group, which is useful when many vertices are noise. Do not use it on sparse graphs with few cliques, where most vertices end in no community, or on dense graphs, where the number of cliques explodes and the communities merge into one. For a partition of all vertices, use the [Louvain method](/louvain-method/) or [label propagation](/label-propagation-algorithm/).

## Pitfalls

- **$k = 2$.** The communities are just the components with an edge; there is nothing to learn. Use $k \ge 3$.
- **$k$ too small on dense graphs.** A few cliques can chain everything into one giant community.
- **Pairwise tests of cliques.** Testing all pairs of $k$-cliques for a shared $(k-1)$-set costs $\Theta(N^2)$; the faces with a hash map avoid this.
- **Recursion.** A recursive clique search can be deep on large cliques; Python has a small recursion limit. Use an explicit stack.
- **Reading the overlap as a partition.** The output does not cover all vertices and is not disjoint, so measures for partitions, such as [modularity](/modularity/), do not apply directly.

## History

Derényi, Palla and Vicsek studied the percolation of $k$-cliques in random graphs, and Palla, Derényi, Farkas and Vicsek used it in 2005 to find overlapping communities in networks of co-authors, of word associations and of proteins, with the program CFinder. Extensions to weighted and directed graphs followed in 2007. Fortunato's survey of 2010 treats it as the main early method for overlapping communities.
