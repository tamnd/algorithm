A lattice is a set of points in $\mathbb{R}^m$ of the form $\{x_1 b_1 + \cdots + x_n b_n : x_i \in \mathbb{Z}\}$, where $b_1, \ldots, b_n$ are [linearly independent](/linear-independence/) vectors. Equivalently, it is a discrete [subgroup](/subgroup/) of $(\mathbb{R}^m, +)$: closed under addition and negation, with no two points closer than some fixed positive distance. Lattices are the objects of the geometry of numbers, and the [shortest vector](/shortest-vector-problem/) and [closest vector](/closest-vector-problem/) problems on them are the base of [lattice-based cryptography](/lattice-based-cryptography/).

## Definition

Let $b_1, \ldots, b_n \in \mathbb{R}^m$ be linearly independent, so $n \le m$. On these pages they are the **rows** of the $n \times m$ matrix $B$. The **lattice generated by $B$** is

$$L(B) = \Big\{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i b_i \;:\; x_1, \ldots, x_n \in \mathbb{Z} \Big\} = \{ x B : x \in \mathbb{Z}^n \}.$$

The matrix $B$ is a **[basis](/lattice-basis/)** of the lattice, $n$ is its **rank** (or dimension), and the lattice is **full rank** when $n = m$.

**Abstract form.** A subset $L \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ is a lattice if and only if it is a **discrete subgroup**: $0 \in L$, $u - v \in L$ for all $u, v \in L$, and there is $\varepsilon > 0$ with $\|v\| \ge \varepsilon$ for every nonzero $v \in L$.

- *A lattice is a discrete subgroup.* $L(B)$ is closed under subtraction because $\mathbb{Z}^n$ is. For discreteness, let $v = \sum x_i b_i \ne 0$ and let $k$ be the largest index with $x_k \ne 0$. With the [Gram-Schmidt vectors](/gram-schmidt-orthogonalization/) $b_i^*$, the component of $v$ along $b_k^*$ is $x_k b_k^*$, so $\|v\| \ge |x_k| \|b_k^*\| \ge \min_i \|b_i^*\| > 0$. Take $\varepsilon = \min_i \|b_i^*\|$.
- *A discrete subgroup has a basis.* This is the converse, proved by induction on the dimension of the span: pick a shortest nonzero vector, project the group along it, and lift a basis of the projection. A full proof is in Micciancio and Goldwasser, Chapter 1. So the two definitions describe the same sets.

**Change of basis.** Two bases $B$ and $B'$ (both $n \times m$) generate the same lattice if and only if $B' = U B$ for an integer $n \times n$ matrix $U$ with $\det U = \pm 1$, a [unimodular matrix](/unimodular-matrix/). Proof: if $L(B) = L(B')$, each row of $B'$ is an integer combination of the rows of $B$ and the converse, so $B' = U B$ and $B = V B'$ with integer $U, V$; then $B = V U B$, and independence of the rows gives $V U = I$, so $\det U \det V = 1$ with both integers, hence $\det U = \pm 1$. Conversely, if $U$ is integer with $\det U = \pm 1$, then $U^{-1}$ is integer by the adjugate formula, so each lattice contains the other.

**Invariants.** The quantities that do not depend on the basis are the lattice invariants. The main ones are the [determinant](/lattice-determinant/) $\det(L) = \det(B B^T)^{1/2}$, the volume of a fundamental parallelepiped; the first minimum $\lambda_1(L) = \min\{\|v\| : v \in L, v \ne 0\}$, which exists by discreteness; the [successive minima](/successive-minima/) $\lambda_1 \le \cdots \le \lambda_n$; and the [covering radius](/covering-radius/).

## Examples and non-examples

**Examples.**

- $\mathbb{Z}^m$, with the standard basis. Here $\det = 1$ and $\lambda_1 = 1$.
- The lattice with rows $b_1 = (2, 0)$ and $b_2 = (1, 2)$. It has $\det = 4$, and it is a sublattice of $\mathbb{Z}^2$ of index 4. The rows $(1, 2), (3, 2)$ give the same lattice, since $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $-1$ and maps the first basis to the second.
- The **hexagonal lattice** $A_2$, with rows $(1, 0)$ and $(1/2, \sqrt 3/2)$. Each point has six nearest neighbours at distance 1. It gives the densest packing of equal discs in the plane.
- The **checkerboard lattice** $D_3 = \{x \in \mathbb{Z}^3 : x_1 + x_2 + x_3 \text{ even}\}$, with basis rows $(1, -1, 0), (0, 1, -1), (1, 1, 0)$. Its determinant is 2 and $\lambda_1 = \sqrt 2$, reached for example by $(-1, 0, 1)$.
- A **q-ary lattice**: for an integer matrix $A \in \mathbb{Z}^{k \times m}$ and an integer $q \ge 2$, the set $\Lambda_q^\perp(A) = \{x \in \mathbb{Z}^m : A x \equiv 0 \pmod q\}$ is a full-rank lattice that contains $q \mathbb{Z}^m$. The [short integer solution](/short-integer-solution/) problem asks for a short nonzero vector in it.
- A **rank-deficient** lattice: $L((1, 1, 1)) = \{(k, k, k) : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a lattice of rank 1 in $\mathbb{R}^3$, with determinant $\sqrt 3$.
- Integer combinations of **dependent rational** vectors also form a lattice: $(2)$ and $(3)$ in $\mathbb{R}^1$ generate $\mathbb{Z}$, with basis $(1)$. The generating set is not a basis, but the group is discrete.

**Non-examples.**

- $\mathbb{Z} + \sqrt 2\, \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{R}$ is a subgroup, but not discrete: $41 - 29\sqrt 2 \approx -0.0122$ and the powers $(\sqrt 2 - 1)^k$ give nonzero elements as small as one likes. The vectors $(1)$ and $(\sqrt 2)$ are dependent over $\mathbb{R}$, so they are not a basis.
- The set $\{x \in \mathbb{Z}^2 : x_1 \ge 0\}$ is discrete but not a group, since it is not closed under negation.
- $\mathbb{Q}^m$ is a group but not discrete.
- A finite set of points other than $\{0\}$ is never a lattice: if $v \ne 0$ is in it, all multiples $k v$ must be in it.
- The points of $\mathbb{Z}^2$ on the line $x_2 = 1$ are a translate of a lattice (a coset), not a lattice, because they do not contain $0$. The targets of the [closest vector problem](/closest-vector-problem/) often live in such cosets.

## Related definitions

- **[Lattice basis](/lattice-basis/).** A matrix $B$ with $L = L(B)$; unique up to the unimodular change of basis above. Finding a good basis is [lattice basis reduction](/lattice-basis-reduction/).
- **[Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization](/gram-schmidt-orthogonalization/).** The orthogonal vectors $b_i^*$ and coefficients $\mu_{ij}$ of a basis, used by all reduction notions.
- **[Lattice determinant](/lattice-determinant/).** $\det(L) = \prod_i \|b_i^*\|$, the volume of $\operatorname{span}(L) / L$.
- **[Dual lattice](/dual-lattice/).** $L^\times = \{y \in \operatorname{span}(L) : \langle y, v \rangle \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ for all } v \in L\}$.
- **[Successive minima](/successive-minima/)** and **[covering radius](/covering-radius/).** The radii at which balls around $0$ contain $k$ independent lattice vectors, and the radius at which balls around all lattice points cover the span.
- **Sublattice and index.** A lattice $L' \subseteq L$ of the same rank; the index $[L : L'] = \det(L') / \det(L)$ is a positive integer.
- **Integer lattice.** A lattice contained in $\mathbb{Z}^m$. Its basis can be put in [Hermite normal form](/hermite-normal-form/), which gives a canonical basis and a test of equality.
- **Computational problems.** The [shortest vector problem](/shortest-vector-problem/), the [closest vector problem](/closest-vector-problem/) and their [approximate](/approximate-svp/) and [gap](/gap-svp/) versions.
