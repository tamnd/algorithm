The randomized NP-hardness of SVP says that the [shortest vector problem](/shortest-vector-problem/) in the Euclidean norm, and even its approximation within any constant factor, cannot be solved in polynomial time unless every problem in NP has a randomized polynomial-time algorithm with one-sided error, that is $\mathrm{NP} \subseteq \mathrm{RP}$. Ajtai proved the exact case in 1998, answering a question van Emde Boas had asked in 1981. Micciancio (2001) extended it to every factor below $\sqrt 2$, Khot (2005) to every constant, and Haviv and Regev (2012) to almost polynomial factors under quasi-polynomial reductions. No NP-hardness of SVP in the Euclidean norm is known under deterministic reductions without extra assumptions, in contrast with the [closest vector problem](/cvp-np-hardness/).

## Statement

**Notation.** As on all pages of this area: lattices are generated by the rows $b_1, \ldots, b_n$ of a basis, with first minimum $\lambda_1$, Gram-Schmidt vectors $b_i^*$, determinant $\det(L)$ and the LLL parameter $\delta$. The norm is Euclidean. For $\gamma \ge 1$, [GapSVP$_\gamma$](/gap-svp/) is the promise problem: given a basis $B$ and $t > 0$, answer YES if $\lambda_1(L) \le t$ and NO if $\lambda_1(L) > \gamma t$.

**Reductions.** A *reverse unfaithful random (RUR) reduction* from a language $A$ to a promise problem $\Pi$ is a randomized polynomial-time map $f$ such that every NO instance of $A$ is mapped to a NO instance of $\Pi$ with probability 1, and every YES instance of $A$ is mapped to a YES instance of $\Pi$ with probability at least $1 / q(\text{size})$ for a polynomial $q$.

**Theorem (Ajtai 1998; Micciancio 2001).** For every constant $\gamma$ with $1 \le \gamma < \sqrt 2$, there is an RUR reduction from an NP-complete language to GapSVP$_\gamma$ in the Euclidean norm. Consequently, if GapSVP$_\gamma$ (or SVP, or $\gamma$-approximate SVP) can be solved in randomized polynomial time with one-sided error, then $\mathrm{NP} \subseteq \mathrm{RP}$.

**Stronger forms.** Khot (2005) and Haviv and Regev (2012) proved the same for every constant $\gamma \ge 1$, and Haviv and Regev proved that GapSVP$_\gamma$ with $\gamma = 2^{(\log n)^{1 - \varepsilon}}$ is not in randomized quasi-polynomial time unless NP has randomized algorithms of [quasi-polynomial](/quasi-polynomial-time/) time, for every $\varepsilon > 0$.

## In plain words

Finding the point of a grid nearest to a given target point is a classic hard problem: puzzles that are believed to have no fast solution can be rewritten as such searches. The question with the origin as the target, the shortest vector question, resisted the same treatment for almost twenty years, because the origin carries no information about the puzzle.

The way around it is to build, with the help of random choices, a special grid that has a huge number of points all at about the same distance from one spot, while its own points stay far apart. Gluing this grid to the target question turns the target into a short grid vector exactly when the puzzle has a solution. The random choices can fail, but only in one direction, so a fast method for shortest vectors would give a fast randomized method for every such puzzle.

## Proof

We prove the theorem for every $\gamma < \sqrt 2$ from two lemmas, following Micciancio and Goldwasser, Chapter 4. Lemma 1 is the source problem, and Lemma 2 is the probabilistic construction; both are cited, and the reduction that combines them is proved in full. Lattice vectors are written as $z L$ for integer row vectors $z$ and generator matrices $L$.

**Lemma 1 (a CVP variant; Arora, Babai, Stern and Sweedyk).** For every constant $c \ge 1$ the following promise problem GapCVP$'_c$ is NP-hard under deterministic polynomial-time reductions. *Instance:* an integer matrix $C$ with $k$ rows, a target $y$ and a number $d > 0$. *YES:* there is $x \in \{0, 1\}^k$ with $\|x C - y\| \le d$. *NO:* for every $x \in \mathbb{Z}^k$ and every integer $w \ne 0$, $\|x C - w y\| > c d$.

The NO condition excludes all integer multiples of the target, not only $y$ itself; it is what the proof below needs, and the PCP-based reductions for approximate [CVP](/cvp-np-hardness/) provide it.

**Lemma 2 (locally dense lattices; Micciancio).** For every constant $\rho < \sqrt 2$ there is a randomized algorithm that, given $k$ in unary, outputs in time polynomial in $k$ a basis $L$ (rows $L_1, \ldots, L_h$), a vector $s$, a number $r > 0$ and an integer matrix $T$ with $h$ rows and $k$ columns, such that

1. $\lambda_1(L(L)) \ge \rho r$, always;
2. with probability at least $1/2$: for every $x \in \{0, 1\}^k$ there is $z \in \mathbb{Z}^h$ with $z T = x$ and $\|z L - s\| \le r$.

*Idea of the construction.* $L$ is a prime number lattice of Schnorr and Adleman. For the first $h$ primes $p_i$, the row $L_i$ has the entry $\sqrt{\ln p_i}$ in position $i$ and a large multiple of $\ln p_i$ in a last position, and $s$ has a large multiple of $\ln b$ in the last position for a random integer $b$. A vector $z \in \{0, 1\}^h$ is within about $\sqrt{\ln b}$ of $s$ exactly when the square-free number $\prod p_i^{z_i}$ is very close to $b$, while a nonzero lattice vector $z L$ corresponds to two distinct coprime integers $g = \prod_{z_i > 0} p_i^{z_i}$ and $g' = \prod_{z_i < 0} p_i^{-z_i}$. Its first coordinates give $\|z L\|^2 \ge \ln g + \ln g'$, and its last coordinate is small only if $g$ and $g'$ are very close, which with the right weight forces both to be about $b$ or larger. So $\lambda_1^2$ is about $2 \ln b$ while $r^2$ is about $\ln b$, which is where $\sqrt 2$ comes from. A counting argument on smooth numbers shows that for a random $b$ there are exponentially many close points, and a random $T$ then maps them onto all of $\{0, 1\}^k$ with probability at least $1/2$, by a combinatorial lemma in the spirit of Sauer's lemma. Real entries are replaced by rational approximations, which only moves the constants by factors close to 1. $\square$

**The reduction.** Fix $\gamma < \sqrt 2$ and choose constants $\rho$ and $c$ with $\gamma < \rho < \sqrt 2$ and

$$\frac{\rho^2}{1 + \rho^2 / c^2} > \gamma^2,$$

which holds for $c$ large enough. Given an instance $(C, y, d)$ of GapCVP$'_c$ with $k$ rows, run the algorithm of Lemma 2 with this $k$ and $\rho$, and set $a = 1/r$ and $\beta = \rho / (c d)$. Output the lattice generated by the $h + 1$ rows

$$\big( a L_i, \ \beta \, T_i C \big) \ (1 \le i \le h), \qquad \big( -a s, \ -\beta y \big),$$

where $T_i$ is row $i$ of $T$, together with the threshold $t = \sqrt{1 + \rho^2 / c^2}$. The integer combination with coefficients $(z, w) \in \mathbb{Z}^h \times \mathbb{Z}$ is

$$v(z, w) = \big( a (z L - w s), \ \beta ((z T) C - w y) \big).$$

If the rows are linearly dependent, a basis of the lattice they generate is computed in polynomial time (for example by MLLL), which does not change the lattice.

*YES instances.* Suppose some $x \in \{0, 1\}^k$ has $\|x C - y\| \le d$, and suppose property 2 of Lemma 2 holds. Take $z$ with $z T = x$ and $\|z L - s\| \le r$. Then

$$\|v(z, 1)\|^2 = a^2 \|z L - s\|^2 + \beta^2 \|x C - y\|^2 \le 1 + \frac{\rho^2}{c^2} = t^2.$$

The vector $v(z, 1)$ is not 0, because its first block $a (z L - s)$ is not 0: if $s$ were a lattice point of $L(L)$, property 2 applied to $x = 0$ and $x = e_1$ would give two distinct lattice points $z L \ne z' L$ (since $z T \ne z' T$) within distance $r$ of $s$, and one of them differs from $s$, which gives a nonzero lattice vector of length at most $r < \rho r \le \lambda_1$, a contradiction (here $\rho > \gamma \ge 1$). So $\lambda_1 \le t$, and the output is a YES instance whenever property 2 holds, that is with probability at least $1/2$.

*NO instances.* Suppose that for every $x \in \mathbb{Z}^k$ and every $w \ne 0$, $\|x C - w y\| > c d$. Let $(z, w) \ne (0, 0)$.

- If $w \ne 0$, then $x = z T$ is an integer vector, so $\|v(z, w)\|^2 \ge \beta^2 \|x C - w y\|^2 > \beta^2 c^2 d^2 = \rho^2$.
- If $w = 0$, then $z \ne 0$, so $z L$ is a nonzero vector of $L(L)$ and $\|v(z, 0)\|^2 \ge a^2 \|z L\|^2 \ge a^2 \rho^2 r^2 = \rho^2$ by property 1.

So every nonzero vector of the output lattice has length at least $\rho$. Since $\rho > \gamma t$ by the choice of $c$, the output is a NO instance of GapSVP$_\gamma$. This uses only property 1, which always holds, so NO instances are mapped to NO instances with probability 1.

*Conclusion.* The map is an RUR reduction from GapCVP$'_c$, which is NP-hard by Lemma 1, to GapSVP$_\gamma$. If an algorithm $M$ decides GapSVP$_\gamma$ in randomized polynomial time with one-sided error, run the reduction and $M$ independently a polynomial number of times on a source instance and accept if any run accepts. A NO instance is never accepted; a YES instance is accepted with probability at least $1/2$ after enough runs. So the NP-complete language is in RP, and $\mathrm{NP} \subseteq \mathrm{RP}$. $\blacksquare$

## Consequences

- **SVP and its approximations are hard.** Exact SVP, the optimization version, and $\gamma$-SVP for $\gamma < \sqrt 2$ (every constant, by Khot and Haviv-Regev) have no polynomial-time algorithms unless $\mathrm{NP} \subseteq \mathrm{RP}$, which would also put NP in $\mathrm{BPP}$ and collapse the polynomial hierarchy.
- **Other problems inherit it.** [Unique SVP](/unique-svp/) for factors close to 1 (Kumar and Sivakumar, using an isolation step like [Valiant-Vazirani](/valiant-vazirani-theorem/)) and SVP in every $\ell_p$ norm within factors below $2^{1/p}$ (Micciancio) are hard in the same randomized sense.
- **Exponential algorithms are expected.** The reductions blow up the dimension only polynomially, so under the randomized exponential time hypothesis SVP needs time $2^{n^{\Omega(1)}}$, and finer reductions give lower bounds of the form $2^{\Omega(n)}$ for $\ell_p$ norms under stronger assumptions. This is consistent with the $2^{\Theta(n)}$ algorithms of [lattice sieving](/lattice-sieving/).
- **Cryptography is not affected directly.** Lattice cryptography assumes the hardness of SVP-type problems within polynomial factors, which are far above the factors covered by the theorem and, by the limits below, are not NP-hard unless the polynomial hierarchy collapses.

## Tightness

- **Why $\sqrt 2$ for this method.** Property 1 and property 2 of Lemma 2 together need many lattice points in a ball of radius $r$ whose pairwise distances are at least $\rho r$. If $\rho \ge \sqrt 2$, the vectors $u_j = p_j - s$ from the center to such points satisfy $\|u_i - u_j\|^2 \ge 2 r^2 \ge \|u_i\|^2 + \|u_j\|^2$, so $\langle u_i, u_j \rangle \le 0$ for all $i \ne j$. A set of nonzero vectors in $\mathbb{R}^h$ with pairwise nonpositive inner products has at most $2h$ elements, reached by $\pm e_1, \ldots, \pm e_h$. So there are at most $2h + 1$ such points, far too few to cover $\{0, 1\}^k$, and the method of locally dense lattices stops at $\sqrt 2$. Khot went beyond it with a different construction and tensor products, which amplify a constant gap.
- **Factors that are probably not NP-hard.** GapSVP$_\gamma$ is in $\mathrm{NP} \cap \mathrm{coAM}$ for $\gamma = \sqrt{n / \log n}$ (Goldreich and Goldwasser) and in $\mathrm{NP} \cap \mathrm{coNP}$ for $\gamma = \sqrt n$ (Aharonov and Regev). So it is not NP-hard for these factors unless the polynomial hierarchy collapses ([Arthur-Merlin](/arthur-merlin/) protocols). The gap between $2^{(\log n)^{1 - \varepsilon}}$ and $\sqrt{n / \log n}$ is open.
- **Randomness.** All the reductions above are randomized. Micciancio showed that his reduction becomes deterministic under a conjecture on the distribution of square-free smooth numbers, and in 2012 gave a version whose only randomized step is a combinatorial construction based on BCH codes. For the max norm, van Emde Boas proved NP-hardness with a deterministic reduction already in 1981.

## History

Van Emde Boas proved in 1981 that CVP is NP-hard in every $\ell_p$ norm and SVP is NP-hard in the max norm, and conjectured that SVP is NP-hard in the Euclidean norm. The question stayed open while the LLL algorithm of 1982 made SVP approximation central to cryptanalysis. Ajtai settled it in 1998 with a randomized reduction for exact SVP and factors $1 + 2^{-n^c}$; Cai and Nerurkar improved the factor to $1 + n^{-\varepsilon}$. Micciancio, in his 1998 thesis and the 2001 journal paper, introduced locally dense lattices and reached every factor below $\sqrt 2$. Khot (2004 conference, 2005 journal) reached every constant and almost polynomial factors under quasi-polynomial reductions, and Haviv and Regev (2007 conference, 2012 journal) simplified the tensor argument and improved the factor to $2^{(\log n)^{1 - \varepsilon}}$.
