The *Solovay-Kitaev theorem* says that a finite set of one-qubit gates that can approximate every gate at all can do so cheaply: a gate within distance $\varepsilon$ of any target needs only $O(\log^c(1/\varepsilon))$ gates from the set, and a classical computer finds the sequence in polylogarithmic time. It is the reason that the choice of [universal gate set](/universal-gate-set/) does not matter for complexity: a circuit written with arbitrary one-qubit gates is rewritten in Clifford plus $T$ with only a polylogarithmic factor more gates. Robert Solovay found the result in 1995 and Alexei Kitaev found it independently and published it in 1997. The version that most people implement, with $c \approx 3.97$, is the recursive algorithm of Dawson and Nielsen.

## Statement

Write $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ for the one-qubit [unitaries](/unitary-operator/) of determinant 1; every one-qubit gate equals one of them up to a global phase. Measure errors with the phase-invariant operator norm distance

$$d(U, V) = \min_{\phi} \lVert U - e^{i\phi} V \rVert, \qquad d(U, V) = \sqrt{2 - |\operatorname{tr}(U^\dagger V)|} \text{ for } U, V \in \mathrm{SU}(2).$$

**Theorem (Solovay-Kitaev).** Let $G \subset \mathrm{SU}(2)$ be a finite set of gates with these properties:

1. $G$ is closed under inverses: $g \in G$ implies $g^\dagger \in G$;
2. the group generated by $G$ is dense in $\mathrm{SU}(2)$: every gate is a limit of products of elements of $G$.

Then there is a constant $c$ such that for every $U \in \mathrm{SU}(2)$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a product $g_1 g_2 \cdots g_L$ of elements of $G$ with $d(U, g_1 \cdots g_L) \le \varepsilon$ and $L = O(\log^c(1/\varepsilon))$. The constant in the $O$ depends on $G$, and the product is found by a classical algorithm in time $O(\log^{c'}(1/\varepsilon))$. The proof below gives $c = \log 5 / \log(3/2) \approx 3.97$ and $c' = \log 3 / \log(3/2) \approx 2.71$.

The same statement holds in $\mathrm{SU}(d)$ for every fixed $d$, so for gates on a fixed number of qubits, with constants that depend on $d$. For a [circuit](/quantum-circuit-model/) of $m$ gates, approximate each gate to $\varepsilon/m$: errors add at most linearly, so the whole circuit is within $\varepsilon$ with $O(m \log^c(m/\varepsilon))$ gates from $G$.

## In plain words

Suppose a small toolbox of gates can get near any gate if you use enough of them. The question is how many you need. Trying all short sequences is fast only for a rough answer. The trick is to fix the error, not the gate. After a rough answer, the remaining error is a small turn. A small turn can be made by doing two medium turns, then undoing them in the other order. The leftover of the medium turns is much smaller than the turns themselves, so rough answers for the medium turns are good enough. Repeat this a few times and the error falls very fast, while the sequence grows only five times longer at each step. So the number of gates grows only like a fixed power of the number of correct digits.

## Proof

The proof follows Dawson and Nielsen. Distances have two properties that the proof uses all the time. They are invariant: $d(WU, WV) = d(UW, VW) = d(U, V)$ for unitary $W$. And they add along products: $d(U_1 U_2, V_1 V_2) \le d(U_1, V_1) + d(U_2, V_2)$, by the triangle inequality and invariance.

**Step 1: a net of fixed size.** The group generated by $G$ is dense and $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ is compact, so for every $\varepsilon_0 > 0$ there is a length $\ell_0$ such that the products of at most $\ell_0$ elements of $G$ are an $\varepsilon_0$-net: every $U$ is within $\varepsilon_0$ of one of them. The value of $\varepsilon_0$ is a constant fixed below, so $\ell_0$ and the size of the net are constants that depend only on $G$. The net is built once, for example by [breadth-first search](/breadth-first-search/) over words, and the step $U \mapsto U_0$ (the nearest net element) is a [nearest-neighbour search](/nearest-neighbor-search/).

**Step 2: a small gate is a commutator of two larger ones.** Let $\Delta$ be a rotation by a small angle $\theta$ on the [Bloch sphere](/bloch-sphere/). Choose $\varphi$ with $\sin(\theta/2) = 2 \sin^2(\varphi/2) \sqrt{1 - \sin^4(\varphi/2)}$. Let $V_0$ and $W_0$ be rotations by $\varphi$ about the $x$ and $y$ axes. The group commutator $V_0 W_0 V_0^\dagger W_0^\dagger$ is a rotation by exactly $\theta$, about some axis; conjugating $V_0$ and $W_0$ by a rotation $S$ that maps this axis to the axis of $\Delta$ gives $V = S V_0 S^\dagger$ and $W = S W_0 S^\dagger$ with

$$V W V^\dagger W^\dagger = \Delta, \qquad d(V, I) = d(W, I) \le c_1 \sqrt{d(\Delta, I)}$$

for a constant $c_1$. This is the *balanced group commutator*: for small $\theta$, $\varphi \approx \sqrt\theta$.

**Step 3: commutators forgive errors.** Suppose $d(V, I), d(W, I) \le \delta$, and $\tilde V, \tilde W$ satisfy $d(\tilde V, V), d(\tilde W, W) \le \eta$ with $\eta \le \delta$. Write $V = I + A$ and $\tilde V = V + E$, and the same for $W$, and expand the commutator. Every term that is first order in the errors $E$ alone cancels against its inverse, so the leftover is of order $\delta \eta$:

$$d(\tilde V \tilde W \tilde V^\dagger \tilde W^\dagger, \; V W V^\dagger W^\dagger) \le c_2 \, \delta \eta.$$

**Step 4: the recursion.** Define $\mathrm{SK}(U, 0) = U_0$, the nearest net element, with error $\varepsilon_0$. For $n \ge 1$:

1. let $U_{n-1} = \mathrm{SK}(U, n-1)$, with $d(U, U_{n-1}) \le \varepsilon_{n-1}$;
2. let $\Delta = U U_{n-1}^\dagger$, so $d(\Delta, I) \le \varepsilon_{n-1}$, and split $\Delta = V W V^\dagger W^\dagger$ by Step 2, with $d(V, I), d(W, I) \le c_1 \sqrt{\varepsilon_{n-1}}$;
3. let $\tilde V = \mathrm{SK}(V, n-1)$ and $\tilde W = \mathrm{SK}(W, n-1)$;
4. return $U_n = \tilde V \tilde W \tilde V^\dagger \tilde W^\dagger U_{n-1}$.

By invariance $d(U, U_n) = d(\Delta, \tilde V \tilde W \tilde V^\dagger \tilde W^\dagger)$, and Step 3 with $\delta = c_1 \sqrt{\varepsilon_{n-1}}$ and $\eta = \varepsilon_{n-1}$ gives

$$\varepsilon_n \le c \, \varepsilon_{n-1}^{3/2}, \qquad c = c_1 c_2.$$

Put $x_n = c^2 \varepsilon_n$. Then $x_n \le x_{n-1}^{3/2}$, so $x_n \le x_0^{(3/2)^n}$. Fix $\varepsilon_0 < 1/c^2$, so that $x_0 < 1$ and the error falls doubly exponentially in $n$. Here $\tilde V^\dagger$ is the word of $\tilde V$ reversed with each gate inverted, which is where hypothesis 1 is used.

**Step 5: the counts.** The word of $U_n$ is five words of level $n - 1$, so its length is $\ell_n = 5^n \ell_0$. Each call makes three calls of level $n - 1$ plus a constant amount of work, so the time is $t_n = O(3^n)$. To reach $\varepsilon_n \le \varepsilon$ it is enough that $(3/2)^n \ge \log(1/(c^2 \varepsilon)) / \log(1/x_0)$, so $n = \log_{3/2} \log(1/\varepsilon) + O(1)$ levels are enough. Then

$$\ell_n = O\big(5^{\log_{3/2} \log(1/\varepsilon)}\big) = O\big(\log^{\log 5/\log(3/2)}(1/\varepsilon)\big), \qquad t_n = O\big(\log^{\log 3/\log(3/2)}(1/\varepsilon)\big),$$

with $\log 5 / \log(3/2) \approx 3.97$ and $\log 3/\log(3/2) \approx 2.71$. $\blacksquare$

The constants $c_1$, $c_2$ and $\ell_0$ are not given here; Dawson and Nielsen compute them. In practice they mean that the recursion helps only once the net is fine enough, which the measured numbers below show.

## Consequences

- **Gate sets are interchangeable.** Any two finite universal gate sets simulate each other with a polylogarithmic overhead. So the class [BQP](/bqp/) does not depend on the gate set, as long as the gates have entries that can be computed efficiently. This is why proofs such as [factoring in BQP](/factoring-in-bqp/) may use exact rotations by $2\pi/2^k$ and still count gates from a finite set.
- **Clifford plus T is enough.** The gates $H$ and $T$ (with $T^\dagger$) generate a dense subgroup of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, so the theorem applies to them (see [universal gate set](/universal-gate-set/) and [phase and T gates](/phase-and-t-gates/)). Together with CNOT they compile every circuit. Fault-tolerant schemes make exactly these gates (see [Clifford group](/clifford-group/) and [quantum threshold theorem](/quantum-threshold-theorem/)), so the theorem connects the circuit model with real error-corrected machines.
- **The error budget is shared.** A circuit of $m$ gates needs each gate to $\varepsilon/m$, so the total count is $m \cdot \mathrm{polylog}(m/\varepsilon)$, and the cost of a gate model bound after compilation has a $\mathrm{polylog}(1/\varepsilon)$ factor.

## Tightness

- **A logarithm is needed.** A set of $g$ gates gives at most $g^{L+1}$ words of length at most $L$. $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ modulo phase is three-dimensional, so covering it with balls of radius $\varepsilon$ needs about $\varepsilon^{-3}$ balls, up to a constant. So some gate needs $L \ge 3 \log_g(1/\varepsilon) - O(1)$: every method needs $\Omega(\log(1/\varepsilon))$ gates, by a [counting argument](/counting-argument/) (Harrow, Recht and Chuang). They also show that for suitable gate sets words of length $O(\log(1/\varepsilon))$ exist, but their proof does not give a fast algorithm to find them.
- **The exponent 3.97 is not the best known.** Refinements of the commutator step reach exponent $3 + \delta$ for every $\delta > 0$ (as Kuperberg's abstract summarizes the earlier work), and Kuperberg (2023) reaches $1.44042 + \delta$ for any finite inverse-closed set, so the general gap to the lower bound is a power below 1.45.
- **Special gate sets do much better.** For Clifford plus $T$, the matrices have entries in the ring $\mathbb{Z}[1/\sqrt 2, i]$, and number-theoretic synthesis finds a $z$-rotation within $\varepsilon$ with $3 \log_2(1/\varepsilon) + O(\log \log(1/\varepsilon))$ $T$ gates in the typical case, with no ancillas (Ross and Selinger). This is linear in $\log(1/\varepsilon)$, so it meets the counting bound up to a constant factor. This uses the structure of $H$ and $T$; the Solovay-Kitaev theorem uses only density and inverses.
- **The hypotheses.** Density is necessary: a set inside a finite group, such as the one-qubit [Clifford group](/clifford-group/) with 24 elements modulo phase, approximates nothing else. The proof above uses inverses in Step 4, to write $\tilde V^\dagger$ as a word.

**A small example to check by hand.** Two rotations by $\varphi$ about $x$ and $y$ have the commutator $R_x(\varphi) R_y(\varphi) R_x(\varphi)^\dagger R_y(\varphi)^\dagger$, a rotation by $\theta$ with $\sin(\theta/2) = 2s\sqrt{1 - s^2}$ and $s = \sin^2(\varphi/2)$. For $\varphi = 0.1$, $s = 0.0024979$ and $\theta = 0.0099917$, very near $\varphi^2 = 0.01$. Two turns of size $0.1$ give a net turn of size $0.01$, which is Step 2: an error of $0.1$ in $V$ and $W$ costs only about $0.1 \cdot 0.1$ in the commutator.

### Implementation

The file `impl/python/solovay_kitaev_theorem.py` implements the algorithm for one qubit over the base set $\{H, T, T^\dagger\}$. A gate is stored as a unit quaternion $(a, b, c, d)$ for $aI - i(bX + cY + dZ)$, with $U$ and $-U$ taken as the same gate, and $d(U, V) = \sqrt{2 - 2|\langle U, V \rangle|}$. The net is built by breadth-first search over words, the base step is a linear scan of the net, and the recursion uses the balanced commutator of Step 2.

The tests check: the base gates against the matrices of `qsim.py` up to phase; the quaternion product against the matrix product; the distance formula against a scan over 360 phases with the operator norm; the net against brute-force enumeration of all $3^k$ words for $k \le 6$; and $V W V^\dagger W^\dagger = \Delta$ for 200 random small rotations. The main test uses the net of all words of length at most 14 (3404 gates modulo phase) and 10 Haar-random targets from `random.Random(2024)`. The table gives the measured worst and mean error and the longest word, from the simulator.

| depth $n$ | worst error | mean error | longest word |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.0993 | 0.0611 | 14 |
| 1 | 0.0396 | 0.0243 | 66 |
| 2 | 0.0204 | 0.0102 | 315 |
| 3 | 0.00212 | 0.00127 | 1486 |
| 4 | 0.000174 | 0.000087 | 7311 |

The tests assert that the worst error falls at every level, that it is below $0.1$ at depth 0 and below $5 \times 10^{-4}$ at depth 4, and that each level is at most $8 \varepsilon^{3/2}$ of the previous one (the constant 8 is an empirical fit for this run, not a proven constant). The depth is limited by the run time: depth $n$ makes $3^n$ net scans per target, and the words grow by a factor of about 5 per level, so the tests stop at depth 4 to stay near 1 s of CPU time. The first levels gain little because the net error $0.1$ is not yet small compared with $1/c^2$; the fall becomes fast from depth 2 on.

## History

Robert Solovay proved the result in an unpublished manuscript of 1995. Alexei Kitaev found it independently and published it in 1997 in Russian Mathematical Surveys, in his paper on quantum computations and error correction. The book of Kitaev, Shen and Vyalyi (2002) and Appendix 3 of Nielsen and Chuang (2000) gave textbook proofs. Harrow, Recht and Chuang (2002) gave the $\Omega(\log(1/\varepsilon))$ lower bound. Dawson and Nielsen (2006) wrote the algorithm as a short recursive program with the balanced commutator, which is the version most used. Ross and Selinger (2016) gave optimal number-theoretic synthesis for Clifford plus $T$, and Kuperberg (2023) broke the cubic exponent for general gate sets.
