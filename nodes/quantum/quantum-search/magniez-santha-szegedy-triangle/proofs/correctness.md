---
title: MSS-TRIANGLE returns only triangles and finds one with constant probability in Õ(n^(13/10)) queries
proves: [correctness]
status: full
---

We use the notation of the page: $G$ is a graph on $[n]$, read through its adjacency matrix; $A$ is an $r$-subset of $[n]$; $D(A)$ is the set of edges of $G$ inside $A$; a set $A$ is *marked* when CHECK$(A)$ finds a triangle. For a vertex $v$ let $f_v(u) = [uv \in E]$.

**Claim.** (a) Every triangle that the algorithm returns is a triangle of $G$, and on a triangle-free graph it returns "none". (b) CHECK$(A)$ finds a triangle if and only if some triangle of $G$ has an edge inside $A$. (c) If $G$ has a triangle, the fraction of marked $r$-sets is at least $\varepsilon = r(r-1)/(n(n-1))$. (d) The walk that swaps one vertex of $A$ has spectral gap $\delta = n/(r(n - r)) \ge 1/r$. (e) The number of queries is $O(r^2 + (n/r)(\sqrt{n}\,r^{2/3} + \sqrt{r}\cdot r))$ up to logarithmic factors, and this is $\tilde O(n^{13/10})$ at $r = n^{3/5}$, the best choice of the exponent.

*Proof.* **(a)** Line L9 returns $(u, u', v)$ only for an edge $uu'$ of $G|A$ with $f_v(u) = f_v(u') = 1$, so $uu'$, $uv$ and $u'v$ are edges, and since $f_v(v) = 0$ for a graph without loops, $v \notin \{u, u'\}$. The three edges can be read again with 3 queries before the output. So every output is a triangle of $G$. If $G$ has no triangle, no $(u, u', v)$ passes, and line L6 returns "none".

**(b)** If CHECK$(A)$ finds $(u, u', v)$, then by (a) it is a triangle with the edge $uu'$ inside $A$. Conversely, let $\{u, u', v\}$ be a triangle with $u, u' \in A$. Then $uu'$ is an edge of $G|A$ and $f_v(u) = f_v(u') = 1$, so the graph collision instance of line L8 for this $v$ has a solution, and the search of line L7 over $v$ has a marked item.

**(c)** Let $\{a, b, c\}$ be a triangle. Every $r$-set that contains $a$ and $b$ is marked by (b). The number of such sets is $\binom{n-2}{r-2}$, and $\binom{n-2}{r-2}/\binom{n}{r} = r(r-1)/(n(n-1))$.

**(d)** In one step of the walk on $r$-sets, a vertex $u \in A$ and a vertex $w \notin A$ are chosen uniformly, and $A$ becomes $A - \{u\} + \{w\}$; each of the $r(n - r)$ moves has probability $1/(r(n - r))$. This is the walk on the Johnson graph $J(n, r)$, and it is symmetric, so its stationary distribution is uniform. Take $f(A) = [1 \in A] - r/n$. If $1 \in A$, a move removes $1$ with probability $1/r$, so $(Pf)(A) = (1 - r/n) - 1/r$. If $1 \notin A$, a move adds $1$ with probability $1/(n - r)$, so $(Pf)(A) = -r/n + 1/(n - r)$. In both cases $(Pf)(A) = \lambda f(A)$ with $\lambda = 1 - n/(r(n - r))$, since $(1 - r/n)\,n/(r(n-r)) = 1/r$ and $(r/n)\,n/(r(n-r)) = 1/(n-r)$. The eigenvalues of the Johnson scheme are $1 - j(n + 1 - j)/(r(n - r))$ for $j = 0, \ldots, r$; the value for $j = 1$ is $\lambda$, they go down as $j$ grows, and the smallest is $-1/(n - r)$. When $r \ge 3$ and $n \ge 2r$, $\lambda \ge 1 - 2/r \ge 1/3 \ge 1/(n - r)$, so every other eigenvalue is at most $\lambda$ in absolute value (for smaller cases a lazy walk fixes the sign). So $\delta = n/(r(n - r))$, and $\delta \ge 1/r$ because $n \ge n - r$. We cite the spectrum of the Johnson scheme and do not prove it here.

**(e)** By the cost theorem of [quantum walk search](/quantum-walk-search/) (Theorem 3 of MNRS, or Theorem 2 of the paper for the walk of Ambainis), the outer walk finds a marked set with constant probability in $O(S + (1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})(U/\sqrt{\delta} + C))$ queries. Line L2 reads the $r(r-1)/2$ edges inside $A$, so $S = O(r^2)$. A step adds one vertex and removes one, and each reads or erases $r$ edge bits, so $U = O(r)$. By (c) and (d), $1/\sqrt{\varepsilon} = O(n/r)$ and $1/\sqrt{\delta} \le \sqrt{r}$. For $C$, the instance of line L8 has $r$ vertices, and $G|A$ is in the data register, so only $f_v$ costs queries. The walk of Ambainis on $q$-subsets of $A$ has setup $q$, update $1$ and check $0$, and costs $O(q + (r/q)\sqrt{q})$, which is $O(r^{2/3})$ at $q = r^{2/3}$. Line L7 runs [amplitude amplification](/amplitude-amplification/) over the $n$ choices of $v$ with $O(\sqrt{n})$ rounds of this procedure, so $C = \tilde O(\sqrt{n}\,r^{2/3})$; the logarithmic factor makes the error of each check small enough for all the rounds. Together the cost is
$$\tilde O\big(r^2 + (n/r)(\sqrt{n}\,r^{2/3} + \sqrt{r}\cdot r)\big).$$
With $r = n^a$ the three terms are $n^{2a}$, $n^{3/2 - a/3}$ and $n^{1 + a/2}$. The second term goes down and the third goes up with $a$, and they are equal when $3/2 - a/3 = 1 + a/2$, that is $a = 3/5$, with the value $13/10$. At $a = 3/5$ the first term is $n^{6/5}$, which is smaller. The maximum of three linear functions is convex in $a$, and at $a = 3/5$ the active terms have slopes of opposite sign, so $a = 3/5$ is the minimum, and the cost is $\tilde O(n^{13/10})$. With a constant number of repetitions the success probability is at least $2/3$, and by (a) the error is one-sided. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The proof of Theorem 4 in the arXiv version of the paper first writes $r = n^{2/3}$ and then evaluates the cost at $r = n^{3/5}$; the computation above shows that $n^{3/5}$ is the right value. At $r = n^{2/3}$ the third term is $n^{4/3}$, which is larger than $n^{13/10}$.
