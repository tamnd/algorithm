The variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) is a hybrid algorithm for [ground state energy estimation](/ground-state-energy-estimation/), introduced by Peruzzo et al. in 2014. A quantum computer prepares a trial state $|\psi(\theta)\rangle = U(\theta)|0\cdots0\rangle$ from a circuit with real parameters $\theta$, and measures the terms of a Hamiltonian $H = \sum_{j=1}^{L} c_jP_j$, given as a [Pauli string decomposition](/pauli-string-decomposition/). The average of the measured values estimates the energy $E(\theta) = \langle\psi(\theta)|H|\psi(\theta)\rangle$, and a classical optimizer changes $\theta$ to make the energy smaller. By the [variational method](/variational-method/), every exact value $E(\theta)$ is at least the ground energy $E_0$, so the lowest value found is an upper bound. The circuits are short and need no ancilla qubits, which made VQE a main candidate for small noisy devices. Nothing guarantees that the optimizer finds the best $\theta$, or that the trial family contains a state near the ground state, and the number of measurements grows as $1/\epsilon^2$ for an energy error $\epsilon$.

## In plain words

The goal is the lowest energy of a physical system. The quantum computer has a circuit with knobs. For each setting of the knobs it makes a state, and repeated measurements of that state give its energy, with some noise. A classical program turns the knobs, step by step, so that the energy goes down. A basic law of quantum physics says that no state has an energy below the true lowest energy, so each exact value found is a safe upper estimate. The method works well only if some setting of the knobs gives a state close to the true lowest state and the program can find it. Neither is certain. The program can stop in a valley that is not the deepest one, and for large random circuits the landscape can be so flat that the program cannot tell which way is down.

## The problem

The input is a list of terms $(c_j, P_j)$, $j = 1, \ldots, L$, with real $c_j$ and $n$-qubit Pauli strings $P_j$, so that $H = \sum_j c_jP_j$ is Hermitian, and a trial circuit $U(\theta)$ with $P$ real parameters. Write $\lambda = \sum_j |c_j|$ and let $E_0$ be the least eigenvalue of $H$. The output is a parameter vector $\theta^\ast$ and an estimate $\hat E$ of $E(\theta^\ast)$. The exact target is the least value of $E(\theta)$ over the trial family, and $\min_\theta E(\theta) - E_0$ is the error of the family, which the algorithm cannot reduce. In this page each rotation is $R_P(\theta) = e^{-i\theta P/2}$ for a Pauli string $P$, with the half angle as on the [Pauli gates](/pauli-gates/) page, and qubit 0 is the least significant bit, as on [quantum state](/quantum-state/).

## The algorithm

The pseudocode uses plain gradient descent with gradients from the parameter-shift rule. Other optimizers fit in line L7.

```pseudo id=main
VQE(((c_1, P_1), ..., (c_L, P_L)), U, θ_0, η, T, S)
L1  θ ← θ_0
L2  repeat T times
L3      for k ← 1 to P
L4          E_plus ← ENERGY(θ + (π/2) e_k, S)
L5          E_minus ← ENERGY(θ - (π/2) e_k, S)
L6          g_k ← (E_plus - E_minus) / 2                 ▷ parameter-shift rule
L7      θ ← θ - η g
L8  return θ, ENERGY(θ, S)

ENERGY(θ, S)
L9  for j ← 1 to L
L10     repeat S times: prepare U(θ)|0...0⟩, rotate to the eigenbasis of P_j, measure, record the parity ±1
L11     m_j ← the mean of the S recorded values
L12 return c_1 m_1 + ... + c_L m_L
```

Line L10 measures a Pauli string with one circuit run: a Hadamard gate on each qubit where $P_j$ has an $X$, the gate $HS^\dagger$ on each qubit where it has a $Y$, a measurement of all qubits in the computational basis, and the parity of the measured bits on the support of $P_j$, mapped to $+1$ for even and $-1$ for odd. Terms that commute qubit by qubit can share the same runs. Here $e_k$ is the $k$-th unit vector, and $\eta$ is the step size.

The trial circuit of the example and the code is a hardware-efficient circuit: a layer of $R_Y$ rotations on every qubit, then a chain of CNOT gates from qubit $q$ to qubit $q+1$, repeated $\ell$ times, and a last layer of $R_Y$ rotations, so $P = n(\ell + 1)$. Circuits built from the chemistry of the problem, such as the unitary coupled cluster circuits of [quantum chemistry simulation](/quantum-chemistry-simulation/), keep symmetries such as the particle number and usually need fewer parameters for molecules.

## Example

Take the transverse-field Ising model on two qubits, $H = -ZZ - XI - IX$, so $L = 3$ and $\lambda = 3$. Its eigenvalues are $-\sqrt5, -1, 1, \sqrt5$, so $E_0 = -\sqrt5 \approx -2.2360679775$, and the ground state is $a(|00\rangle + |11\rangle) + b(|01\rangle + |10\rangle)$ with $b/a = 2/(1 + \sqrt5)$, so $a \approx 0.6015$ and $b \approx 0.3717$. The trial circuit with $\ell = 1$ is $R_{IY}(\theta_1)$, $R_{YI}(\theta_2)$, CNOT from qubit 0 to qubit 1, $R_{IY}(\theta_3)$, $R_{YI}(\theta_4)$, in the order of application. All its amplitudes are real.

**Three gradients.** At $\theta = (0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4)$ the exact energy is $-1.67606$. The simulator gives these values (exact state vectors):

| $k$ | parameter shift | exact $2\,\mathrm{Re}\langle H\psi\vert\partial_k\psi\rangle$ | central difference, $h = 10^{-5}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | -0.24312782457194 | -0.24312782457194 | -0.24312782457292 |
| 2 | -0.38159345774080 | -0.38159345774080 | -0.38159345776556 |
| 3 | -0.73782549635780 | -0.73782549635780 | -0.73782549631085 |
| 4 | -0.30944952273042 | -0.30944952273042 | -0.30944952275069 |

The parameter shift agrees with the exact derivative to 15 digits, because the rule is exact. The central difference has errors of $10^{-12}$ to $5 \cdot 10^{-11}$, from the $h^2$ term and from rounding.

**Descent.** With a start drawn uniformly from $[0, 2\pi)^4$ (seed 0), $\eta = 0.1$ and exact energies ($S = \infty$), the simulator gives:

| step | 0 | 5 | 10 | 20 | 50 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $E(\theta)$ | 1.25894 | -0.74347 | -1.99411 | -2.20544 | -2.23603067 | -2.2360679770 |
| $E(\theta) - E_0$ | 3.495 | 1.49 | 0.242 | 0.0306 | $3.7 \cdot 10^{-5}$ | $5.0 \cdot 10^{-10}$ |

After 200 steps the gap is at rounding level, and the final amplitudes are $(0.601501, 0.371748, 0.371748, 0.601501)$, the ground state. Seeds 0 to 4 with $\ell = 1$ or $\ell = 2$ all reach $E_0$.

**A trial family that is too small.** For the open chain on three qubits, $H = -Z_0Z_1 - Z_1Z_2 - X_0 - X_1 - X_2$, $E_0 = -3.4939592074$. With $\ell = 1$, gradient descent for 300 steps stops between $1.1 \cdot 10^{-2}$ and $1.9 \cdot 10^{-2}$ above $E_0$ for seeds 0 to 4: this is the error of the family. With $\ell = 2$ the gaps after 300 steps are $9.7 \cdot 10^{-3}$, $1.6 \cdot 10^{-3}$, $1.2 \cdot 10^{-5}$, $1.4 \cdot 10^{-2}$ and $6.8 \cdot 10^{-2}$, from slow convergence and from local minima.

**Shots.** At the optimum of the two-qubit model (seed 1) with $S = 1000$ shots per term, 86 of 200 repeated estimates fall below $E_0$, between $-2.33$ and $-2.14$. The bound $E(\theta) \ge E_0$ is for the exact expectation, not for a noisy estimate.

## Correctness

Three facts are proved, and the full proofs are below. (1) For every $\theta$, $E(\theta) \ge E_0$: expand $|\psi(\theta)\rangle$ in an orthonormal eigenbasis of $H$; the energy is an average of eigenvalues with weights that add up to 1. (2) For a rotation $R_P(\theta) = e^{-i\theta P/2}$ with $P^2 = I$, the energy as a function of one parameter is $A + B\cos\theta + C\sin\theta$, so $\partial_kE(\theta) = (E(\theta + \tfrac\pi2 e_k) - E(\theta - \tfrac\pi2 e_k))/2$ exactly. (3) The value returned by ENERGY is an unbiased estimate of $E(\theta)$, and by the [Hoeffding inequality](/hoeffding-inequality/) and the [union bound](/union-bound/) its error is at most $\lambda\sqrt{2\ln(2L/\delta)/S}$ with probability at least $1 - \delta$.

What is not proved: that gradient descent reaches the minimum of $E(\theta)$, or how many steps it takes; that the minimum of the family is close to $E_0$; and that the overlap of the final state with the ground state is large. A small energy gap above $E_0$ gives a large overlap only when the spectral gap $\Delta = E_1 - E_0$ is known: if $E(\theta) - E_0 \le \epsilon$, the weight on the ground space is at least $1 - \epsilon/\Delta$. The guarantees in the literature for the optimization are negative: training is NP-hard in general (Bittel and Kliesch), and barren plateaus are proved under the assumptions stated in Pitfalls.

## Complexity

- **Shots per energy.** With $S = \lceil 2\lambda^2\ln(2L/\delta)/\epsilon^2\rceil$ shots per term, ENERGY uses $LS = O(L\lambda^2\log(L/\delta)/\epsilon^2)$ circuit runs for error $\epsilon$ with probability $1 - \delta$. For the two-qubit example with $\delta = 0.01$ and $S = 10\,000$ the radius is $0.107$. Grouping commuting terms, and drawing more shots for terms with larger $|c_j|$, lower the constant; the scaling $1/\epsilon^2$ stays.
- **Shots per gradient.** The parameter-shift rule needs $2P$ energies per gradient, so $O(PL\lambda^2\log(PL/\delta)/\epsilon^2)$ runs for all $P$ components within $\epsilon$.
- **Number of steps.** There is no general bound. On the two-qubit example, 100 steps of size 0.1 reach $5 \cdot 10^{-10}$.
- **Circuit.** The hardware-efficient circuit has $n(\ell + 1)$ rotations and $\ell(n - 1)$ CNOT gates, depth $O(\ell n)$ with the chain, and needs no ancilla qubits.
- **Against phase estimation.** [Quantum phase estimation](/quantum-phase-estimation/) reaches precision $\epsilon$ with $O(1/\epsilon)$ total evolution time, but with deep circuits and with an initial state of known overlap. VQE has short circuits and pays with $1/\epsilon^2$ repetitions and no guarantee.

## Properties

- **Upper bound.** Each exact energy is at least $E_0$, so the best value found is an upper bound on the ground energy, up to the statistical error of the estimate. Noise in the device breaks this: a noisy channel produces a mixed state, whose energy is still at least $E_0$, but readout errors and error mitigation can move the estimate either way.
- **Randomized.** The measurements are random, and so is the start point in most uses.
- **Heuristic.** The algorithm is a local search over $\theta$. It inherits the local minima of [local search](/local-search/) and the step size choices of [gradient descent](/gradient-descent/).
- **Short circuits.** One run is one preparation and one measurement, with no controlled evolution and no ancilla qubit.
- **Exact gradients from two energies.** For rotations generated by Pauli strings, the gradient is a difference of two energies at shifts of $\pm\pi/2$, with no step size bias, unlike [finite differences](/finite-differences/).

## Variants and improvements

- **Optimizers.** Gradient-free methods (Nelder-Mead, COBYLA, SPSA), [stochastic gradient descent](/stochastic-gradient-descent/) with few shots per step, [natural gradient descent](/natural-gradient-descent/) with the quantum Fisher information (the quantum natural gradient), and [Adam](/adam/) are all in use.
- **Trial circuits.** Unitary coupled cluster circuits for chemistry, hardware-efficient circuits, the Hamiltonian variational circuit (layers of $e^{-i\theta_kH_k}$ for parts $H_k$ of $H$), and circuits grown one gate at a time from a pool (ADAPT-VQE).
- **Excited states.** Subspace-search and variational quantum deflation minimize the energy with a penalty for overlap with states found before, using the min-max principle of the [Courant-Fischer theorem](/courant-fischer-theorem/).
- **Measurement reduction.** Grouping of commuting terms, importance sampling of terms, and classical shadows (see [quantum state tomography](/quantum-state-tomography/)).
- **Error mitigation.** Zero-noise extrapolation, symmetry checks and readout correction. They lower the bias at the price of more shots.
- **Imaginary time.** [Quantum imaginary-time evolution](/quantum-imaginary-time-evolution/) and its variational form replace the optimizer by an update rule that follows the flow $e^{-\tau H}$.

## When to use it

Use VQE on a small noisy device when the circuits of [quantum phase estimation](/quantum-phase-estimation/) are too deep, the Hamiltonian has few terms of moderate size, and an upper bound on the energy is useful. It is a fair method to prepare a good initial state for phase estimation or for [ground state preparation](/ground-state-preparation/). For a fault-tolerant computer and a target error of chemical accuracy, about $1.6 \cdot 10^{-3}$ Hartree, the $1/\epsilon^2$ shot count makes VQE slow, and phase estimation is the usual choice. For small systems, exact diagonalization or the [Lanczos algorithm](/lanczos-algorithm/) on a classical computer is faster.

## Pitfalls

- **A shot estimate can fall below $E_0$.** The variational bound holds for the exact expectation. A finite-shot estimate has noise of order $\lambda/\sqrt S$ and can be below $E_0$, as in the example. Report the estimate with its error bar.
- **Barren plateaus.** McClean et al. proved that if the circuit, or a part of it before or after the parameter, forms a unitary 2-design, the mean of each gradient component is 0 and its variance decays exponentially in $n$. Then exponentially many shots are needed to find a descent direction. Cerezo et al. proved that for circuits of alternating layers of local 2-designs, a global cost (such as the overlap with a fixed product state) has exponentially small gradients even at depth $O(\log n)$, while a local cost (a sum of terms on few qubits) has gradients at most polynomially small at that depth. These are statements for random circuits under these assumptions, not for a given circuit at a given point. On the circuit of this page, which is not a 2-design, with $2n$ layers and 100 random parameter vectors (seed 5) the simulator measures a variance of $\partial_1E$ of 0.220, 0.131, 0.056, 0.030 and 0.017 for $n = 2, \ldots, 6$ for the cost $Z_1Z_0$; a fall by about half per qubit, consistent with, but not a proof of, exponential decay.
- **Hardness of training.** Bittel and Kliesch proved that finding the global minimum of the variational cost is NP-hard, and that the hardness stays for systems of only logarithmically many qubits and for free fermions, which are easy to simulate classically. So no optimizer works on all instances.
- **Local minima and the start point.** On the three-qubit example, five seeds give five different final gaps. Use several starts, a start from a classical approximation such as Hartree-Fock, or a circuit that grows.
- **Energy is not overlap.** A low energy does not give a large overlap with the ground state when the gap is small. Do not use the VQE state as an approximate ground state without a bound on the gap.
- **Rotation conventions.** The shift $\pm\pi/2$ and the factor $1/2$ are for $e^{-i\theta P/2}$. For $e^{-i\theta P}$ the shift is $\pm\pi/4$ and the factor is 1.
- **Many terms.** Molecular Hamiltonians in a basis of $n$ spin orbitals have of order $n^4$ terms, and the shot count grows with $L$ and $\lambda^2$. Measurement, not the circuit, is often the bottleneck.

## History

The variational principle goes back to Rayleigh and Ritz. Peruzzo et al. (2014) put it on a quantum processor: a two-qubit photonic chip prepared trial states for the helium hydride ion and a classical computer minimized the measured energy. McClean, Romero, Babbush and Aspuru-Guzik (2016) gave the theory: the variational bound for the measured energy, the shot count for a sum of Pauli terms, and the role of the optimizer. Kandala et al. (2017) ran a hardware-efficient VQE on superconducting qubits for small molecules and magnets. Mitarai et al. (2018) and Schuld et al. (2019) gave the parameter-shift rule. McClean et al. (2018) found barren plateaus, Cerezo et al. (2021) showed their dependence on the cost function, and Bittel and Kliesch (2021) proved that training is NP-hard.
