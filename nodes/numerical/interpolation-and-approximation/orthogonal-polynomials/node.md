A family of orthogonal polynomials is a sequence $p_0, p_1, p_2, \ldots$ with $\deg p_k = k$ that are pairwise orthogonal for an inner product $\langle f, g \rangle = \int f g\, w$ given by a weight $w$ on an interval, or by weights at a set of points. Every such family satisfies a three-term recurrence, has real simple zeros that interlace, and is the right basis for least-squares approximation. The [Legendre](/legendre-polynomials/) and [Chebyshev](/chebyshev-polynomials/) polynomials are the main examples in numerical work. Orthogonal polynomials connect approximation, [Gaussian quadrature](/gaussian-quadrature/), the [Lanczos algorithm](/lanczos-algorithm/) and Krylov methods such as [Chebyshev iteration](/chebyshev-iteration/).

## In plain words

Any polynomial can be built from the powers $1, x, x^2, \ldots$, but these building blocks look very much alike on an interval, so recovering how much of each one a curve contains is unstable. Orthogonal polynomials are a better set of building blocks. Each one is "perpendicular" to all the others in a precise averaged sense, so the amount of each block in a curve can be found on its own, by one averaging step, without disturbing the others.

The perpendicularity depends on how much weight you give to each part of the interval, so different weightings give different families. Every such family can be produced one member at a time by a short rule that uses only the previous two members.

## The idea

Notation on the interpolation pages: $w$ is a weight, positive and integrable on an interval $(a, b)$ (possibly infinite) with finite moments $\mu_j = \int_a^b x^j w(x)\, dx$; $\langle f, g \rangle = \int_a^b f g\, w$ and $\|f\| = \langle f, f \rangle^{1/2}$; $\mathcal{P}_n$ is the space of polynomials of degree at most $n$; $T_k$ and $P_k$ are the Chebyshev and Legendre polynomials.

**Definition and existence.** A sequence $p_0, p_1, \ldots$ with $\deg p_k = k$ is orthogonal for $w$ if $\langle p_j, p_k \rangle = 0$ for $j \ne k$. Then $p_k$ is orthogonal to all of $\mathcal{P}_{k-1}$, which $p_0, \ldots, p_{k-1}$ span. Gram-Schmidt applied to $1, x, x^2, \ldots$ produces the family, and it is unique up to a nonzero factor for each $k$. The **monic** family $\pi_k = x^k + \cdots$ is the usual normalization in proofs.

**Three-term recurrence.** The monic polynomials satisfy $\pi_{-1} = 0$, $\pi_0 = 1$ and
$$\pi_{k+1}(x) = (x - \alpha_k)\, \pi_k(x) - \beta_k\, \pi_{k-1}(x), \qquad \alpha_k = \frac{\langle x\pi_k, \pi_k \rangle}{\langle \pi_k, \pi_k \rangle}, \quad \beta_k = \frac{\langle \pi_k, \pi_k \rangle}{\langle \pi_{k-1}, \pi_{k-1} \rangle}.$$
*Proof.* $x\pi_k - \pi_{k+1}$ has degree at most $k$, so $x\pi_k = \pi_{k+1} + \sum_{j \le k} c_j \pi_j$ with $c_j = \langle x\pi_k, \pi_j \rangle / \|\pi_j\|^2$. Because multiplication by $x$ is symmetric, $\langle x\pi_k, \pi_j \rangle = \langle \pi_k, x\pi_j \rangle$, and $x\pi_j \in \mathcal{P}_{j+1}$ is orthogonal to $\pi_k$ when $j + 1 < k$. So only $c_k = \alpha_k$ and $c_{k-1} = \langle \pi_k, x\pi_{k-1} \rangle / \|\pi_{k-1}\|^2 = \|\pi_k\|^2/\|\pi_{k-1}\|^2 = \beta_k$ remain, the last step because $x\pi_{k-1} = \pi_k + (\text{lower degree})$. Conversely, Favard's theorem says that any family defined by such a recurrence with real $\alpha_k$ and $\beta_k > 0$ is orthogonal for some positive measure.

**Zeros.** $\pi_k$ has $k$ real simple zeros in $(a, b)$. (If it changed sign at only $r < k$ points $z_i$ of $(a, b)$, then $\pi_k \prod_i (x - z_i)$ would not change sign, so its integral against $w$ would be nonzero, contradicting orthogonality to $\mathcal{P}_{k-1}$.) The zeros of $\pi_k$ and $\pi_{k+1}$ interlace. They are the eigenvalues of the symmetric tridiagonal **Jacobi matrix** $J_k$ with diagonal $\alpha_0, \ldots, \alpha_{k-1}$ and off-diagonal $\sqrt{\beta_1}, \ldots, \sqrt{\beta_{k-1}}$, since the recurrence says that $(\pi_0(x), \ldots, \pi_{k-1}(x))$, suitably scaled, is an eigenvector of $J_k$ exactly when $\pi_k(x) = 0$.

**Best approximation in $L^2$.** For $f$ with $\|f\| < \infty$, the best approximation to $f$ from $\mathcal{P}_n$ in the norm $\|\cdot\|$ is the truncated orthogonal expansion $\sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{\langle f, p_k \rangle}{\|p_k\|^2}\, p_k$. Each coefficient is computed independently of the others, and raising $n$ does not change the earlier coefficients. If the polynomials are dense in the weighted $L^2$ space (true on a finite interval by the [Weierstrass theorem](/weierstrass-approximation-theorem/)), the expansion converges to $f$ in that norm.

**Gauss quadrature.** The $n$-point rule whose nodes are the zeros of $\pi_n$, with the weights of [interpolatory quadrature](/interpolatory-quadrature/), integrates $\int p\, w$ exactly for every $p \in \mathcal{P}_{2n-1}$, the highest possible degree. The [Golub-Welsch algorithm](/golub-welsch-algorithm/) gets the nodes and weights from the eigen-decomposition of $J_n$.

**Christoffel-Darboux formula.** $\sum_{k=0}^{n} \dfrac{\pi_k(x)\pi_k(y)}{\|\pi_k\|^2} = \dfrac{\pi_{n+1}(x)\pi_n(y) - \pi_n(x)\pi_{n+1}(y)}{\|\pi_n\|^2 (x - y)}$. It expresses the kernel of the orthogonal projection onto $\mathcal{P}_n$ in closed form and is the main tool for convergence proofs.

**Computing them.** For classical weights the $\alpha_k, \beta_k$ are known in closed form. For a general weight there are three routes. Moments: the map from $\mu_j$ to $\alpha_k, \beta_k$ is severely ill-conditioned (Gautschi 1982). The Stieltjes procedure: discretize the inner product by a quadrature or by data points and apply the recurrence, computing $\alpha_k$ and $\beta_k$ from inner products of the computed $\pi_k$. Lanczos: apply the [Lanczos algorithm](/lanczos-algorithm/) to the diagonal matrix of nodes with the square roots of the weights as starting vector. The last two are stable and are the standard methods.

**Evaluating series.** A sum $\sum_{k=0}^{n} c_k p_k(x)$ is evaluated by Clenshaw's backward recurrence in $O(n)$ operations, the analogue of [Horner's rule](/horner-rule/) for an orthogonal basis; it never forms the monomial coefficients, which for Legendre and Chebyshev families grow like $2^k$ and cancel.

## Examples

**The classical families.**

| family | interval | weight $w(x)$ | recurrence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legendre $P_k$ | $[-1, 1]$ | $1$ | $(k+1)P_{k+1} = (2k+1)xP_k - kP_{k-1}$ |
| Chebyshev, first kind $T_k$ | $[-1, 1]$ | $(1 - x^2)^{-1/2}$ | $T_{k+1} = 2xT_k - T_{k-1}$ |
| Chebyshev, second kind $U_k$ | $[-1, 1]$ | $(1 - x^2)^{1/2}$ | $U_{k+1} = 2xU_k - U_{k-1}$ |
| Jacobi $P_k^{(\alpha,\beta)}$ | $[-1, 1]$ | $(1 - x)^\alpha (1 + x)^\beta$, $\alpha, \beta > -1$ | coefficients rational in $k, \alpha, \beta$ |
| Laguerre $L_k$ | $[0, \infty)$ | $e^{-x}$ | $(k+1)L_{k+1} = (2k + 1 - x)L_k - kL_{k-1}$ |
| Hermite $H_k$ | $(-\infty, \infty)$ | $e^{-x^2}$ | $H_{k+1} = 2xH_k - 2kH_{k-1}$ |

The Legendre, Chebyshev and Gegenbauer families are special cases of the Jacobi family.

**Discrete orthogonal polynomials for data fitting.** Fit a polynomial of degree $n$ by least squares to $f(x) = 1/(1 + 25x^2)$ at 101 equispaced points of $[-1, 1]$. The exact least-squares fit was computed in rational arithmetic from the double-precision data. In double precision, the monomial normal equations solved by Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting give fitted values with maximum error $1.8 \times 10^{-13}$, $4.2 \times 10^{-9}$ and $1.9 \times 10^{-2}$ for $n = 8, 16, 24$. Forsythe's method, which generates the polynomials orthogonal over the data points by the Stieltjes recurrence and projects onto them, has errors $3.3 \times 10^{-16}$, $1.6 \times 10^{-15}$ and $3.3 \times 10^{-15}$. The fits themselves differ from $f$ by $0.15$, $0.031$ and $0.0062$, so at $n = 24$ the monomial rounding error exceeds the approximation error. The cause is the Gram matrix of the monomials, a matrix of [Hilbert](/hilbert-matrix/) type, whose condition number grows exponentially with $n$.

**A three-term recurrence in action.** For Chebyshev polynomials, $\alpha_k = 0$ and $T_2 = 2x \cdot x - 1$, $T_3 = 2x(2x^2 - 1) - x = 4x^3 - 3x$; the zeros of $T_3$ are $0, \pm\sqrt{3}/2$, and those of $T_2$, $\pm 1/\sqrt{2}$, lie between them.

## Where it is used

- **Least-squares approximation and data fitting.** Continuous and discrete [least-squares approximation](/least-squares-approximation/) in an orthogonal basis, which avoids the ill-conditioned normal equations of the monomial basis.
- **Quadrature.** [Gaussian quadrature](/gaussian-quadrature/) for each weight: Gauss-Legendre, Gauss-Laguerre, Gauss-Hermite, and Gauss-Chebyshev rules, computed by the [Golub-Welsch algorithm](/golub-welsch-algorithm/).
- **Spectral methods.** Legendre and Chebyshev expansions in [spectral methods](/spectral-methods/) and in the polynomial approximation of functions to machine precision.
- **Krylov methods.** The conjugate gradient and Lanczos methods implicitly build orthogonal polynomials for the spectral measure of a matrix; [Chebyshev iteration](/chebyshev-iteration/) uses the Chebyshev recurrence explicitly.
- **Rational approximation and continued fractions.** The denominators of [Padé approximants](/pade-approximation/) of a Stieltjes function are orthogonal polynomials, and the convergents of the associated continued fraction satisfy the same recurrence.
- **Probability and physics.** Hermite polynomials for the Gaussian distribution and the quantum harmonic oscillator, Laguerre polynomials for the hydrogen atom, and polynomial chaos expansions for uncertainty quantification.
