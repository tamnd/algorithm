---
title: Dragon4 prints the shortest, nearest decimal in the rounding interval
proves: [correctness]
status: full
---

**Setting.** Binary64, $x > 0$ finite, $x = m \cdot 2^{q}$ as returned by L1 ($m < 2^{53}$, $q \ge -1074$, and $m \ge 2^{52}$ unless $q = -1074$). Let $x^{-}$ and $x^{+}$ be the neighbors of $x$ (with $x^{+} = 2^{1024}$ for the largest finite number), $g^{-} = (x - x^{-})/2$ and $g^{+} = (x^{+} - x)/2$, and let $R$ be the rounding interval $[x - g^{-}, x + g^{+}]$ when $m$ is even and $(x - g^{-}, x + g^{+})$ when $m$ is odd. $R$ is the set of reals that a reader with round to nearest, ties to even, maps to $x$ (IEEE 754-2019, Clause 4.3.1; for the largest finite number the upper end is the overflow threshold of Clause 7.4). "Inside $R$" means in this interval, closed or open according to the parity of $m$; this is the variable `inclusive`. A decimal $y > 0$ has $n$ significant digits if $y = N \cdot 10^{t}$ with integers $N, t$ and $10^{n - 1} \le N < 10^{n}$.

**Lemma 1 (setup, L2).** After L2, $r/s = x$, $m^{+}/s = g^{+}$ and $m^{-}/s = g^{-}$.

*Proof.* If $m \ne 2^{52}$ or $q = -1074$, both neighbors are at distance $2^{q}$: in a binade the floats are the multiples of $2^{q}$, and at $q = -1074$ the subnormals and the smallest binade share that spacing. Then $g^{\pm} = 2^{q - 1}$, and the two branches give $r/s = 2m2^{q}/2$ or $2m/2^{1 - q}$, equal to $x$, and $m^{\pm}/s = 2^{q}/2$ or $1/2^{1 - q}$, equal to $2^{q-1}$. If $m = 2^{52}$ and $q > -1074$, then $x$ is a power of two, $x^{+} - x = 2^{q}$ and $x - x^{-} = 2^{q - 1}$, so $g^{+} = 2^{q - 1}$ and $g^{-} = 2^{q - 2}$; the branches give $r/s = 4m2^{q}/4$ or $4m/2^{2 - q}$, equal to $x$, $m^{+}/s = 2^{q + 1}/4$ or $2/2^{2 - q}$, equal to $2^{q - 1}$, and $m^{-}/s = 2^{q - 2}$. For the largest finite number, $m = 2^{53} - 1$ and $g^{+} = 2^{q - 1}$ matches the virtual neighbor $2^{1024}$. $\square$

In every later step $r$, $m^{+}$, $m^{-}$ and $s$ are multiplied by the same powers of ten in a way that L3 and L4 record in $k$, and L5 multiplies the first three by $10$ once per digit. Define, after the $j$-th execution of L6, the integer $D_{j}$ whose decimal digits are the digits produced so far, the last one being $d$ before any adjustment in L9. The next lemma is the invariant.

**Lemma 2 (invariant).** After L3 and L4, $r/s = x \cdot 10^{-k}$ and $m^{\pm}/s = g^{\pm} \cdot 10^{-k}$. After the $j$-th execution of L6, $0 \le r < s$ and
$$x \cdot 10^{j - k} = D_{j} + \frac{r}{s}, \qquad g^{\pm} \cdot 10^{j - k} = \frac{m^{\pm}}{s}.$$

*Proof.* L3 multiplies $s$ by $10^{k}$ when $k \ge 0$, and $r$, $m^{\pm}$ by $10^{-k}$ when $k < 0$; each iteration of L4 multiplies $s$ by $10$ and adds $1$ to $k$. This gives the first claim from Lemma 1. For the second, induct on $j$: before step $j$, $x \cdot 10^{j - 1 - k} = D_{j - 1} + r/s$ with $D_0 = 0$ and $0 \le r < s$ (for $j = 1$, $r/s = x\cdot 10^{-k} < 1$ by Lemma 3 below). L5 makes $r/s$ ten times larger, and L6 writes $10r = ds + r'$ with $0 \le r' < s$, so $d \le 9$ and $x \cdot 10^{j - k} = 10 D_{j - 1} + d + r'/s = D_{j} + r'/s$. The relation for $m^{\pm}$ is multiplied by $10$ on both sides. A step that continues (L8) keeps $d$ as the last digit, so $D_j$ is as defined. $\square$

**Lemma 3 (the exponent, L3 and L4).** After L4, $k$ is the least integer such that every point of $R$ is below $10^{k}$. In particular $x < 10^{k}$, and $R$ contains no number $\ge 10^{k}$.

*Proof.* Let $e_2 = q + \ell(m) - 1$, where $\ell(m)$ is the length of $m$ in binary, so $2^{e_2} \le x < 2^{e_2 + 1}$. The constant $c = 30103/100000$ satisfies $0 < c - \log_{10} 2 < 5 \cdot 10^{-9}$, and $|e_2| \le 1075$, so $|e_2 c - e_2\log_{10} 2| < 10^{-5}$. Hence the estimate $k_0 = \lfloor e_2 c\rfloor - 1 \le e_2 c - 1 < e_2 \log_{10} 2 \le \log_{10} x$, so $10^{k_0} < x$. By Lemma 2 applied before the digit loop, the test of L4, $r + m^{+} > s$ or (inclusive and $r + m^{+} = s$), says exactly that the upper end $x + g^{+}$ of $R$ is above $10^{k}$, or equal to it with the end included, that is, that $R$ has a point $\ge 10^{k}$. This property is monotone in $k$: it holds for $k_0$ (since $x > 10^{k_0}$ and $x \in R$) and fails for large $k$. L4 increments $k$ until it fails, so the final $k$ is the least integer for which it fails. The loop runs at most $3$ times, since $x + g^{+} \le 2^{e_2 + 1}$ gives $\log_{10}(x + g^{+}) \le (e_2 + 1)\log_{10} 2 < k_0 + 3.31$. $\square$

**Lemma 4 (what the tests mean).** After step $j$, let $y_{j}^{-} = D_{j} \cdot 10^{k - j}$ and $y_{j}^{+} = (D_{j} + 1)\cdot 10^{k - j}$. Then $y_{j}^{-} \le x < y_{j}^{+}$, these are the consecutive multiples of $10^{k - j}$ around $x$, and `low` holds if and only if $y_{j}^{-}$ is inside $R$, `high` if and only if $y_{j}^{+}$ is inside $R$.

*Proof.* The bracketing is Lemma 2 with $0 \le r < s$. Multiplying by $s \cdot 10^{k-j}$, the test $r < m^{-}$ reads $x - y_{j}^{-} < g^{-}$, that is $y_{j}^{-} > x - g^{-}$, and equality $r = m^{-}$ is the lower end itself, admitted only when inclusive. Since $y_{j}^{-} \le x$, this is exactly "$y_{j}^{-}$ is inside $R$" (for $D_j = 0$ the test fails, consistent with $0 \notin R$ because $g^{-} < x$). Likewise $r + m^{+} > s$ reads $y_{j}^{+} < x + g^{+}$, with equality at the upper end. $\square$

**Lemma 5 (numbers with few digits).** For $n \ge 1$, $R$ contains a number with at most $n$ significant digits if and only if $y_{n}^{-}$ or $y_{n}^{+}$ is inside $R$.

*Proof.* By Lemma 3, every point of $R$ is below $10^{k}$, and at $k - 1$ the property of L4 holds, so $R$ has a point $z_0 \ge 10^{k - 1}$. Suppose $y \in R$ has $n' \le n$ significant digits. If $y \ge 10^{k - 1}$, its leading digit is at position $k - 1$ and its last nonzero digit at a position $\ge k - n' \ge k - n$, so $y$ is a positive multiple of $10^{k - n}$. If $y < 10^{k - 1}$, then $R$, an interval containing $y$ and $z_0$, contains $10^{k - 1}$, again a positive multiple of $10^{k - n}$. So in both cases $R$ contains a positive multiple $z$ of $10^{k - n}$. If $z \le x$ then $0 < z \le y_{n}^{-} \le x$, and if $z > x$ then $x < y_{n}^{+} \le z$; since $R$ is an interval containing $x$ and $z$, it contains $y_{n}^{-}$ or $y_{n}^{+}$ respectively. Conversely, $y_{n}^{\pm} \in R$ is a positive multiple of $10^{k - n}$ below $10^{k}$, which has at most $n$ significant digits. $\square$

**Theorem.** Let the loop stop after step $j$ and let $N$ be the integer whose digits are returned, so the output denotes $y = N \cdot 10^{k - j}$. Then:

1. $y$ is inside $R$, so a correctly rounding reader (ties to even) maps $y$ back to $x$;
2. no number with fewer than $j$ significant digits is inside $R$, and $y$ has exactly $j$ significant digits, its first and last digits being nonzero;
3. $y$ is the multiple of $10^{k - j}$ inside $R$ nearest to $x$, with a tie broken to an even last digit; it is also the nearest to $x$ of all the numbers with $j$ significant digits inside $R$.

*Proof.* By Lemma 4, the loop continues at step $n$ exactly when neither $y_{n}^{-}$ nor $y_{n}^{+}$ is inside $R$, which by Lemma 5 means that $R$ contains no number with at most $n$ significant digits. So $j$ is the least $n$ for which $R$ contains a number with at most $n$ significant digits; this is claim 2 except for the shape of the output. L9 returns $y_{j}^{-}$ when only `low` holds, $y_{j}^{+}$ when only `high` holds, and one of them when both hold; in each case $y$ is inside $R$, which is claim 1. $y$ is a positive multiple of $10^{k - j}$ below $10^{k}$, so it has at most $j$ significant digits, and by the minimality of $j$ not fewer. Hence $N \ge 10^{j - 1}$ and $10 \nmid N$ (for $j = 1$, $1 \le N \le 9$). The returned string is $D_{j - 1}$ followed by $d$ or $d + 1$; if $d + 1 = 10$ then $N$ would be divisible by $10$, so the last digit is a single digit, and a leading zero in $D_{j}$ would force $N = D_j + 1 = 10^{j - 1}$, divisible by $10$ for $j \ge 2$; so the string is exactly the decimal numeral of $N$.

For claim 3, the multiples of $10^{k - j}$ nearest to $x$ on each side are $y_{j}^{-}$ and $y_{j}^{+}$, and any other one inside $R$ is farther from $x$. If both are inside, then $x - y_{j}^{-} = (r/s) 10^{k - j}$ by Lemma 2, so $y_{j}^{-}$ is nearer when $2r < s$, $y_{j}^{+}$ when $2r > s$, and they tie when $2r = s$; L9 then chooses $d$ if it is even and $d + 1$ otherwise, the even last digit. By the proof of Lemma 5, a number with $j$ significant digits inside $R$ that is not a multiple of $10^{k - j}$ lies below $10^{k - 1}$, and then $10^{k - 1} \in R$ and $j = 1$. Such a one-digit number $z \le 9 \cdot 10^{k - 2}$ could beat $y$ only if $x < 10^{k - 1}$; then $R$ contains $z$ and $10^{k - 1}$, so its width, which is at most $2^{q}$, is at least $10^{k - 2}$, and $m = x/2^{q} < 10^{k - 1}/10^{k - 2} = 10$. So $x$ is one of the subnormals $m \cdot 2^{-1074}$ with $m \le 9$. Among them only $m = 2$ has $x < 10^{k - 1}$: there $R = [3 \cdot 2^{-1075}, 5 \cdot 2^{-1075}] \approx [7.41, 12.36] \cdot 10^{-324}$, $k = -322$, the output is $10^{-323}$ at distance about $0.12 \cdot 10^{-324}$ from $x \approx 9.88 \cdot 10^{-324}$, and the other one-digit candidates $8 \cdot 10^{-324}$ and $9 \cdot 10^{-324}$ are farther. $\blacksquare$

**Remarks.** The rounding interval is that of a reader with round to nearest, ties to even; for a reader that breaks ties differently, set `inclusive` from that rule. The proof uses only exact integer arithmetic after L1, so it does not depend on the rounding mode of the machine.
