A Hamiltonian system is a system of ordinary differential equations generated by a single scalar function $H(q, p)$, the Hamiltonian or energy, through

$$q' = \nabla_p H(q, p), \qquad p' = -\nabla_q H(q, p), \qquad q, p \in \mathbb{R}^d.$$

The motion of planets and molecules, rigid bodies, charged particles and ideal fluids takes this form. Its flow keeps $H$ constant and is symplectic, which implies that it preserves phase-space volume. The numerical problem is to follow such a system over very long times, millions of periods for a planetary orbit, which ordinary error bounds growing like $e^{Lt}$ cannot cover. Methods whose discrete flow is also symplectic, such as [Störmer-Verlet](/verlet-integration/) and the [Gauss methods](/gauss-legendre-runge-kutta/), keep the energy error bounded over exponentially long times; this is the subject of [symplectic integrators](/symplectic-integrators/).

## In plain words

A swinging pendulum without friction, or a planet around the sun, never gains or loses energy. Its motion is fixed by one formula, the energy written in terms of position and speed, and the equations of motion follow from that formula alone.

Computing such a motion step by step makes small errors. Most methods make errors that slowly push the energy up or down, so after many orbits the planet spirals away or falls in. Some methods are built to respect the hidden geometry of these systems. Their energy wobbles a little but does not wander off, even over very long runs, and this is what long simulations of the solar system and of molecules rely on.

## Definition

**Input.** A Hamiltonian $H : U \to \mathbb{R}$, $U \subset \mathbb{R}^{2d}$ open, $H \in C^2$ (or smoother for higher order methods); initial values $(q_0, p_0)$; a final time $T$; a step $h$. Writing $y = (q, p)$ and

$$J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & I \\ -I & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad y' = J^{-1}\nabla H(y),$$

the problem is the [initial value problem](/ode-initial-value-problem/) for this right-hand side. It has a unique local solution, since $J^{-1}\nabla H$ is locally Lipschitz.

**Output.** Approximations $y_n \approx y(t_n)$, judged both by the global error and by the size of $H(y_n) - H(y_0)$ and of other invariants over long times. The cost is counted in evaluations of $\nabla H$ (forces), plus linear or nonlinear solves for implicit methods, times the number of steps.

**Structural properties of the exact flow $\varphi_t$** (proved in HLW, Chapter VI, and Arnold):

- **Energy.** $\frac{d}{dt}H(y(t)) = \nabla H^{T}J^{-1}\nabla H = 0$, since $J^{-1}$ is skew-symmetric.
- **Symplecticity.** The Jacobian $\Psi = \partial\varphi_t/\partial y_0$ satisfies $\Psi^{T}J\Psi = J$ for all $t$. Proof: $\Psi' = J^{-1}\nabla^2 H\,\Psi$, so $(\Psi^{T}J\Psi)' = \Psi^{T}\nabla^2 H J^{-T}J\Psi + \Psi^{T}JJ^{-1}\nabla^2 H\Psi = -\Psi^{T}\nabla^2 H\Psi + \Psi^{T}\nabla^2 H\Psi = 0$, using $J^{-T}J = -I$.
- **Volume.** $\det\Psi = 1$ (Liouville), a consequence of symplecticity.
- **Reversibility.** If $H(q, -p) = H(q, p)$, reversing the momenta reverses the motion.
- **Conversely**, a flow that is symplectic on a simply connected domain is locally Hamiltonian.

## Variants

- **Separable Hamiltonians** $H = T(p) + V(q)$, usually $T = \frac12p^{T}M^{-1}p$: second order systems $Mq'' = -\nabla V(q)$. Explicit symplectic methods exist for these.
- **Non-separable** $H$, such as charged particles in magnetic fields or geodesics: symplectic methods are then implicit in general.
- **Integrable and nearly integrable systems**, such as the Kepler problem and the solar system: invariant tori, where symplectic methods give linear growth of the global error.
- **Constrained Hamiltonian systems**, such as molecules with fixed bond lengths: the RATTLE and SHAKE methods.
- **Poisson systems** $y' = B(y)\nabla H$ with a non-constant skew $B$, such as rigid bodies and Lotka-Volterra in suitable variables.
- **Hamiltonian PDEs**, such as the wave and nonlinear Schrödinger equations, after a structure-preserving space discretization.
- **Time-dependent $H$**, made autonomous by adding $t$ and its conjugate momentum.

## Examples

**Harmonic oscillator.** $H = \frac12(p^2 + q^2)$, $q' = p$, $p' = -q$. [Euler's method](/euler-method/) multiplies $(q, p)$ by a matrix with determinant $1 + h^2$, and multiplies $H$ by exactly $1 + h^2$ each step: the energy grows like $e^{h t}$. [Symplectic Euler](/symplectic-euler-method/), $p_{n+1} = p_n - hq_n$, $q_{n+1} = q_n + hp_{n+1}$, has step matrix with determinant 1 and conserves the modified energy $\tilde H = \frac12(p^2 + q^2) - \frac h2 pq$ exactly. This was checked with exact fractions for $h = 1/10$.

**Kepler problem.** $H = \frac12|p|^2 - 1/|q|$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$, eccentricity $0.6$, $q_0 = (0.4, 0)$, $p_0 = (0, 2)$, period $2\pi$, $H = -\frac12$. Maximum of $|H(y_n) - H(y_0)|$ over $[0, t]$:

| method, step | $t = 10$ | $t = 100$ | $t = 1000$ | $t = 10000$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| explicit Euler, $h = 0.001$ | $3.3 \cdot 10^{-2}$ | $0.19$ | $0.42$ | |
| symplectic Euler, $h = 0.001$ | $1.4 \cdot 10^{-3}$ | $1.4 \cdot 10^{-3}$ | $1.4 \cdot 10^{-3}$ | |
| Störmer-Verlet, $h = 0.01$ | $3.7 \cdot 10^{-4}$ | $3.7 \cdot 10^{-4}$ | $3.7 \cdot 10^{-4}$ | $3.7 \cdot 10^{-4}$ |
| [RK4](/classical-runge-kutta-4/), $h = 0.04$ | $2.5 \cdot 10^{-5}$ | $1.8 \cdot 10^{-4}$ | $1.8 \cdot 10^{-3}$ | $1.8 \cdot 10^{-2}$ |

Verlet and RK4 here use the same number of force evaluations (one and four per step). RK4 is more accurate at first, but its energy error grows linearly in $t$ and is fifty times larger than Verlet's at $t = 10^4$. The energy errors of the symplectic methods oscillate without drift. These are observed runs.

**Pendulum, molecular dynamics, $N$-body problems.** $H = \frac12p^2 - \cos q$; $H = \sum\frac{|p_i|^2}{2m_i} + \sum_{i < j}V(|q_i - q_j|)$ with Lennard-Jones potentials; the outer solar system over $10^8$ years.

## Known algorithms

- **[Symplectic Euler](/symplectic-euler-method/)**, order 1, explicit for separable $H$.
- **[Störmer-Verlet](/verlet-integration/) (leapfrog)**, order 2, explicit for separable $H$, symmetric and symplectic: the workhorse of molecular dynamics and [Hamiltonian Monte Carlo](/hamiltonian-monte-carlo/).
- **Gauss-Legendre collocation**, the [implicit midpoint rule](/implicit-runge-kutta-methods/) and the [Gauss methods](/gauss-legendre-runge-kutta/) of order $2s$: symplectic for every $H$, since a Runge-Kutta method with $b_ia_{ij} + b_ja_{ji} - b_ib_j = 0$ is symplectic (Sanz-Serna 1988, Lasagni 1988, Suris 1988).
- **Splitting and composition methods** ([operator splitting](/operator-splitting/)), such as the triple jump of order 4 and optimised compositions; Wisdom-Holman maps for near-Keplerian motion.
- **Partitioned Runge-Kutta methods** (Lobatto IIIA-IIIB pairs) and variational integrators from discrete Lagrangians.
- **Energy-preserving methods**, such as the averaged vector field method and discrete gradients: conserve $H$ exactly but are not symplectic.
- **Standard methods** ([RK4](/classical-runge-kutta-4/), adaptive Runge-Kutta, multistep): fine over short times; their energy error typically drifts like $th^p$.

## Bounds

- **Long-time energy conservation (proved).** For a symplectic method of order $p$ applied to an analytic $H$ with a bounded numerical solution, there is a modified Hamiltonian $\tilde H$ with $\tilde H = H + O(h^p)$, conserved by the method up to $O(e^{-c/h})$ per step. Hence $|H(y_n) - H(y_0)| = O(h^p)$ for $nh \le e^{c/(2h)}$ (HLW, Theorem IX.8.1, Benettin and Giorgilli 1994). Symmetric non-symplectic methods have similar results for reversible integrable systems.
- **Linear error growth (proved).** For integrable systems and symplectic methods of order $p$, the global error grows like $O(th^p)$ over long times instead of $O(t^2h^p)$ for general methods (HLW, Chapter X).
- **No free lunch (proved).** For general $H$ a symplectic method with a fixed step cannot conserve $H$ exactly unless it is the exact flow up to a time change (Ge and Marsden 1988). So symplectic and energy-preserving are alternatives.
- **Adaptive steps.** Varying $h$ with a standard controller destroys the long-time behaviour; reversible step size control or time transformations are needed (HLW, Chapter VIII).
- **Lyapunov exponents.** In chaotic systems the global error grows like $e^{\lambda t}$ for every method; then only statistical properties can be computed, and the modified-Hamiltonian argument explains why they are correct to $O(h^p)$ (observed in practice, proved only in special cases).

## Reductions

- **Second order systems.** $Mq'' = -\nabla V(q)$ is Hamiltonian with $p = Mq'$.
- **From Lagrangians.** A Lagrangian $L(q, \dot q)$ with invertible $\partial^2 L/\partial\dot q^2$ gives a Hamiltonian via the Legendre transform $p = \partial L/\partial\dot q$, $H = p^{T}\dot q - L$.
- **Splitting.** $H = H_1 + H_2$ with exactly solvable $H_i$ reduces the problem to the sub-flows, composed by [operator splitting](/operator-splitting/), and the result is symplectic.
- **Sampling.** Hamiltonian Monte Carlo reduces sampling from a density $e^{-V(q)}$ to integrating $H = \frac12|p|^2 + V(q)$ with a volume-preserving reversible method, which is why leapfrog is used.

## Applications

Celestial mechanics and the long-term stability of the solar system; molecular dynamics of proteins and materials; particle accelerators, where maps must be symplectic over billions of turns; plasma physics (guiding-centre motion); Hamiltonian Monte Carlo in statistics; geophysical fluid models in Hamiltonian form; quantum dynamics through the Schrödinger equation.
