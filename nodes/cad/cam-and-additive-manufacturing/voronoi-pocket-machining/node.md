Voronoi pocket machining builds a [contour-parallel toolpath](/contour-parallel-toolpath/) for a [pocket](/pocket-machining/) from the [Voronoi diagram](/voronoi-diagram/) of its boundary. The part of the diagram inside the pocket is the [medial axis](/medial-axis/). Each point of it carries its clearance, the distance to the boundary, and the offset curve at distance $d$ is the set of points of the pocket at clearance exactly $d$, so all offsets come from one diagram. Held (1991) made this the core of a pocketing method for areas bounded by segments and circular arcs, with islands. The diagram also shows where an offset splits into parts, and it gives safe links between loops along its arcs. This is a manufacturing method of mechanical CAD, not an EDA router. The code handles convex polygons only, where the medial axis has straight arcs and an exact wavefront builds it.

## In plain words

Pour water around the inside of a pocket wall and let it flow inward at the same speed everywhere. The front of the water at each moment has the shape of an offset loop, the loop that a tool center would follow at that distance from the wall.

Mark the places where two fronts from different parts of the wall meet. These marks form a thin tree, the skeleton of the pocket. Each point of the skeleton knows when the water reached it, which is its distance to the wall.

Once the skeleton is known, any loop is found by walking along the skeleton and picking the points with the right distance. The skeleton also shows where a loop breaks into two, and it gives a path from one loop to the next that stays far from the wall.

## The problem

The input is a pocket $P$, a tool radius $r$ and a step-over $s$. The output is the set of offset loops at distances $d_k = r + ks$, and the links between them, as in [contour-parallel toolpath](/contour-parallel-toolpath/), which also proves that the loops cover what the tool can reach when $s \le r$.

The depth $\delta(x)$ of a point is its distance to the boundary of $P$. The medial axis $M$ is the closure of the set of points of $P$ that have at least two nearest points on the boundary. For a convex polygon $P = \{x : n_i \cdot x \le c_i\}$ with unit outward normals $n_i$, the depth of $x \in P$ is $\delta(x) = \min_i f_i(x)$ with $f_i(x) = c_i - n_i \cdot x$, and $M$ is the closure of the set of points where at least two indices reach the minimum. The offset at distance $d$ is $P_d = \{x \in P : \delta(x) \ge d\}$.

In a convex polygon, $M$ is a tree with straight arcs. Each arc is a piece of the bisector of two edges, and $\delta$ is affine along it. In a pocket with reflex vertices or circular arcs, the Voronoi diagram also has parabolic and other conic arcs, and the offsets have circular arcs around the reflex vertices.

## The algorithm

The medial axis of a convex polygon is the track of a wavefront: the offset $P_t$, whose edges move inward at unit speed as $t$ grows. A vertex of $P_t$ is the meeting point of two moved edges, and it moves along their bisector. An edge $e$ between neighbours $p$ and $q$ vanishes at the time $t$ when the three moved lines $n_i \cdot x = c_i - t$ for $i \in \{p, e, q\}$ meet in one point. This is a $3 \times 3$ linear system in $(x, y, t)$, solved by [Cramer's rule](/cramers-rule/) with exact rationals.

```pseudo id=main
MEDIAL-AXIS(P)
L1  F ← the edges of P in order; start one arc at each vertex, with clearance 0
L2  while F has more than 3 edges do
L3      for each edge e of F with neighbours p and q: t_e ← time when the lines of p, e, q meet
L4      take e with the smallest t_e, and let N be the meeting point
L5      end the arcs (p, e) and (e, q) at N, with clearance t_e
L6      start the arc (p, q) at N, with clearance t_e
L7      remove e from F
L8  end the three open arcs at the meeting point of the last three lines, and return the arcs

VORONOI-POCKET(P, r, s)
L9  A ← MEDIAL-AXIS(P), δmax ← clearance of the deepest node
    OFFSET(A, d):
L10     collect the point of clearance d on each arc whose clearance range holds d
L11     return the convex hull of the collected points
L12 for d ← r, r + s, r + 2s, ... while d ≤ δmax: append the loop OFFSET(A, d)
L13     if d + s ≤ δmax then link the loop to the next one along the spine
```

The spine is the chain of arcs from one polygon vertex to the deepest node. The clearance does not decrease along it, so the link between the loops at $d$ and $d + s$ is the part of the spine between these two clearances, and the tool stays at clearance at least $d$ during the link. Each loop starts where the spine meets it.

In a convex pocket the points of line L10 are in convex position, so line L11 only puts them in order. In a general pocket, line L10 returns one or more closed curves, and Held reads their order from the tree structure of the diagram.

## Example

Take the pentagon with vertices $(0, 0)$, $(20, 0)$, $(20, 9)$, $(8, 25)$, $(0, 10)$. Its edges have lengths 20, 9, 20, 17 and 10, and the outward unit normals are $(0, -1)$, $(1, 0)$, $(\tfrac45, \tfrac35)$, $(-\tfrac{15}{17}, \tfrac{8}{17})$ and $(-1, 0)$.

| step | edge that vanishes | node | clearance |
|---|---|---|---|
| L3 to L7 | the right edge $x = 20$ | $(\tfrac{53}{4}, \tfrac{27}{4})$ | $\tfrac{27}{4} = 6.75$ |
| L3 to L7 | the left edge $x = 0$ | $(8, 8)$ | $8$ |
| L8 | the last three edges meet | $(\tfrac{37}{4}, \tfrac{35}{4})$ | $\tfrac{35}{4} = 8.75$ |

The medial axis has $2 \cdot 5 - 3 = 7$ arcs: five from the vertices and two between the nodes. With $r = 2$ and $s = 2$, the loops are at $d = 2, 4, 6, 8$. The first three are pentagons, for example $(2, 2)$, $(18, 2)$, $(18, \tfrac{25}{3})$, $(\tfrac{58}{7}, \tfrac{149}{7})$, $(2, \tfrac{19}{2})$ at $d = 2$. The loop at $d = 8$ is the triangle $(\tfrac{43}{4}, 8)$, $(\tfrac{64}{7}, \tfrac{71}{7})$, $(8, 8)$, because both vertical edges have vanished by then. The spine from vertex $(20, 0)$ gives the links $(18, 2) \to (16, 4)$, $(16, 4) \to (14, 6)$ and $(14, 6) \to (\tfrac{53}{4}, \tfrac{27}{4}) \to (\tfrac{43}{4}, 8)$. The last one bends at the node where the right edge vanished. The deepest node has clearance 8.75, so no loop fits at $d = 10$.

## Correctness

Each wavefront vertex between edges $i$ and $j$ at time $t$ satisfies $f_i = f_j = t$ and lies in $P_t$, so it has depth $t$ and two nearest edges: it is on the medial axis. Conversely, a point of the medial axis with depth $t > 0$ lies on two different moved lines that support the convex set $P_t$, so it is a vertex of $P_t$, and thus on the track of a wavefront vertex. Each edge length of the wavefront is an affine function of $t$, so the edge that vanishes first is the one with the smallest meeting time of line L3. For OFFSET, every point found by line L10 has depth $d$, so it lies in $P_d$, and every vertex of $P_d$ is a point of the medial axis with clearance $d$, so it is found. The convex hull of a subset of a convex polygon that holds all its vertices is the polygon.

The proof file gives these steps in full for the generic case, where no two events happen at the same time and no two parallel edges meet. Degenerate inputs, such as a rectangle, where two edges vanish at the same time and two parallel edges meet along a segment of constant clearance, are handled by the same steps, one event at a time. For those the code is checked against direct offsets by exact tests, but the proof does not cover them.

## Complexity

Line L2 runs $n - 3$ times, and line L3 solves at most $n$ systems of size 3, so the code builds the medial axis in $O(n^2)$ time. A priority queue of the meeting times makes this $O(n \log n)$, because each event changes the meeting times of two edges only. The axis has $n$ leaves, $n - 2$ inner nodes counted with multiplicity, and $2n - 3$ arcs. Line L10 scans the $2n - 3$ arcs, and the hull of line L11 takes $O(n \log n)$, so each loop costs $O(n \log n)$, and $O(n)$ if the points are collected in order around the tree. The total is $O(n^2 + K n \log n)$ for $K$ loops.

For a general pocket with $n$ segments and arcs, the Voronoi diagram has $O(n)$ size and can be computed in $O(n \log n)$ time, for example by [Fortune's sweep](/fortunes-algorithm/) for segments. Held (1998) uses a wavefront algorithm that is fast in practice.

## Properties

- **One diagram, all offsets.** The diagram is built once. Every offset, at any distance, comes from it in time linear in its size.
- **Topology from the diagram.** Where the clearance along the axis has a local minimum, at a neck of the pocket, the offsets deeper than that clearance split in two. Where it has a local maximum, a loop shrinks to a point and ends. The diagram shows each such place, so the order of the loops and the places where the tool must lift are known before any loop is cut. In a convex pocket the offsets never split.
- **Safe links.** The tool center moves along the arcs between loops. Along the spine the clearance does not decrease, so the tool does not cut into the wall.
- **Exact in the convex case.** With rational edge normals, every node and every loop vertex of the code is rational.

## Variants and improvements

- **Curvilinear pockets.** Held (1998) handles areas bounded by segments and circular arcs. The offsets of such an area are again made of segments and arcs, which a CNC controller can follow directly.
- **Islands.** The boundary of a pocket with islands has several components. The Voronoi diagram is the same object, and the offsets of the islands and of the outer wall merge where their fronts meet.
- **The straight skeleton.** The [straight skeleton](/straight-skeleton/) is the track of a wavefront that keeps sharp corners at reflex vertices. It coincides with the medial axis for convex polygons only. Offsets read from it are mitered offsets, not Euclidean ones.
- **Medial axis toolpaths.** Some methods move the tool along the medial axis itself, to clear the middle of a narrow pocket in one pass.

## When to use it

Use the Voronoi method when the pocket has islands, reflex corners or arcs, and when many offsets of the same pocket are needed, as in roughing with a small step-over. For a convex pocket, direct [half-plane intersection](/half-plane-intersection/) at each depth is simpler, as on the contour-parallel page.

## Pitfalls

- **Arcs are required.** At a reflex vertex the Voronoi diagram has a parabolic arc between the vertex and an edge, and the offset has a circular arc. A method that uses only straight bisectors builds the straight skeleton, not the medial axis.
- **Degenerate events.** Several edges can vanish at the same time, and two parallel edges can meet along a segment of constant clearance, as in a rectangle. The code handles them one event at a time with exact arithmetic. With floating point, the order of near-equal events can flip and give a wrong tree.
- **Robustness of the diagram.** A Voronoi diagram of segments in floating point needs care with nearly parallel and nearly touching segments. See [exact geometric computation](/exact-geometric-computation/).
- **A hull only in the convex case.** Line L11 uses the convex hull to order the points, which is valid only because the offsets of a convex pocket are convex.

## History

Held's 1991 monograph gave the Voronoi diagram of segments and arcs as the tool for pocket machining, with the offsets, their splits and the order of the loops all read from it. Held, Lukács and Andor (1994) described a full system with this method, and Held (1998) gave a wavefront algorithm for curvilinear polygons.
