---
title: The planar selective geometric complex is a cell decomposition, and its Booleans, topological operations and simplification are exact
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

We use the notation and the pseudocode of the page. A complex is cut out by a finite set $\Sigma$ of segments with distinct endpoints and a finite set $\Pi$ of points. Its *skeleton* $K$ is the union of the closed segments and the points. Its cells are the vertices, the open edges and the faces, which are the connected components of $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus K$. An SGC is a complex and a set $M$ of marked cells, and it represents the set $\bigcup M$.

**Lemma 1 (cells).** The vertices, edges and faces are pairwise disjoint, each is connected and they cover the plane. Two edges meet at most in a common endpoint, and no vertex lies on an open edge. The skeleton is the union of the vertices and edges.

*Proof.* A segment $s$ is cut at every point of $s$ that is an endpoint of $s$, a crossing with a segment that is not parallel to it, an endpoint of another segment, or a point of $\Pi$. Let $u, v$ be consecutive cuts on $s$. The open segment $(u, v)$ contains no point of $\Pi$. Suppose it meets another segment $s'$. If $s'$ is not parallel to $s$, the meeting point is their crossing, which is a cut. If $s'$ is parallel, it lies on the same line, and the overlap is a segment whose endpoints are endpoints of $s$ or of $s'$. These are cuts, so the overlap cannot contain a point of $(u, v)$ without containing $u$ and $v$. In that case $(u, v)$ is also a piece of $s'$ between consecutive cuts: a cut of $s'$ inside it would be a crossing, an endpoint or a point of $\Pi$, and so a cut of $s$. Edges are stored with sorted endpoints in a set, so the two copies are one edge. Hence distinct edges are disjoint, and no vertex lies on an open edge. The vertices and the open edges cover $K$. The faces are the components of the complement, which is open. Every cell is connected. $\square$

**Lemma 2 (local structure).** Let $p$ be a point of a cell $c$. There is a disc $U$ around $p$ that meets only $c$ and the cells of its star, the cells $c'$ with $c \subseteq \operatorname{cl}(c')$. Every cell of the star meets every such disc.

*Proof.* If $c$ is a face, take a disc in the open set $c$. If $c$ is an edge, take a disc that misses every edge and vertex other than $c$, possible because those closed sets do not contain $p$ by Lemma 1. The line of $c$ cuts $U$ into two open half-discs. Each is connected and misses $K$, so it lies in one face, the face on that side. These are the faces of the star of $c$. If $c$ is a vertex, take a disc that misses every closed edge not ending at $p$ and every other vertex. The edges that end at $p$ cut $U$ into open sectors, each in one face, and $U$ meets exactly $p$, those edges and those faces. If no edge ends at $p$, then $U \setminus \{p\}$ is connected and lies in one face. Conversely, a face whose closure contains $p$ meets every disc around $p$, and the cells met by small discs are exactly these. $\square$

**Lemma 3 (faces from cycles).** The half-edge cycles of the code, with the faces assigned as in the code, give each face of the complex exactly once. `side[e]` is the pair of faces beside $e$, and `star[c]` is the star of $c$.

*Proof.* Split the skeleton into its connected components $G_1, \dots, G_r$, as plane graphs, with isolated points as single-vertex components. The rule $\mathrm{next}(a, b) = (b, w)$, where $(b, w)$ is the half-edge just clockwise of $(b, a)$ around $b$, is the face-tracing rule of a [doubly connected edge list](/doubly-connected-edge-list/). For a connected plane graph $G_j$ with at least one edge, it traces each face of $G_j$ once, with the face on the left. The bounded faces of $G_j$ give cycles of positive signed area, equal to the area of the face. The unbounded face gives one cycle of signed area $\le 0$, with area $0$ when $G_j$ is a tree. (This is the standard result on boundary cycles, as in de Berg et al., chapter 2.) So the positive cycles are the outer boundaries of the bounded faces of the components. Let $R(\gamma)$ be the open region, the bounded face of its component, that a positive cycle $\gamma$ bounds.

Now take two components $G_i \ne G_j$ and their positive cycles $\gamma_i$, $\gamma_j$. They are disjoint, so $G_i$ lies in one face of $G_j$. Then $R(\gamma_i) \subseteq R(\gamma_j)$, or $R(\gamma_j) \subseteq R(\gamma_i)$, or the two are disjoint, because each $R$ is simply connected. So the regions around any point that come from components other than its own form a chain under inclusion, and the smallest has the least area. A face $F$ of the full complex is bounded by parts of some components. If $F$ is bounded, its outer boundary is a positive cycle $\gamma$ of one component with $F \subseteq R(\gamma)$. Every other boundary component of $F$ is a component $G_j$ inside $R(\gamma)$, seen from outside through its non-positive cycle, or an isolated point. For a point $q$ of such a $G_j$, $R(\gamma)$ is the smallest region around $q$ among the components other than $G_j$, since any smaller one would separate $G_j$ from $\gamma$. The code assigns $G_j$'s outer cycle and isolated points by exactly this rule: the innermost positive cycle with nonzero winding around $q$, among the cycles not through $q$. If no region contains $q$, the hole lies in the unbounded face 0. So each face is listed once, as face 0 or as one positive cycle. Every half-edge gets the face on its left, so `side` is correct. The star of an edge is its two side faces. The star of a vertex is its incident edges with their sides, or the face around it if it is isolated. These are the stars of Lemma 2. $\square$

**Lemma 4 (locate and sample).** `locate(p)` returns the cell that contains $p$, and `sample(c)` returns a point of $c$.

*Proof.* For `locate`: the vertex and edge tests are direct. Otherwise $p$ is in a face. If that face $F$ is bounded with outer cycle $\gamma$, then $p \in R(\gamma)$. Every other positive cycle around $p$ has a region that is nested with $R(\gamma)$, by the chain property of Lemma 3. A region strictly inside $R(\gamma)$ that contains $p$ would have its boundary inside $F$'s outer boundary, separating $p$ from $\gamma$, which contradicts $\gamma$ bounding $F$. So $\gamma$ is the innermost cycle. If no positive cycle winds around $p$, then $p$ is in face 0.

For `sample`: a vertex is its own point. The midpoint of an open edge lies on it. For face 0, the point $(x_{\max} + 1, 0)$ lies to the right of every vertex, and so of the whole skeleton, and it joins infinity by a horizontal ray that misses $K$. For a bounded face, let $ab$ be the first half-edge of its outer cycle. The face is on the left of $ab$, so the points $m + t n$ for small $t > 0$ lie in it, where $m$ is the midpoint and $n$ the left normal. The code collects the parameters $t > 0$ at which the ray meets another edge or a vertex. It checks every edge, collinear edges by their endpoints and the others by a crossing test on a long prefix of the ray, and it checks every vertex on the ray line. Let $t^\ast$ be the least such parameter. Then the segment from $m$ to $m + t^\ast n$, without its end, misses $K$, so the point at $t^\ast / 2$ is in the face. If no hit lies within the prefix, the point at $t = 1$, which is inside the prefix, is in the face for the same reason. $\square$

**Theorem 1 (Booleans).** COMBINE returns an SGC that represents $\operatorname{op}(A, B)$.

*Proof.* Every edge of $A$ is one of the segments of L1, and every vertex of $A$ is one of the points, so $K_A \subseteq K_C$. Let $c$ be a cell of $C$. If $c$ is a vertex, it is either a vertex of $A$, a point of an open edge of $A$, or a point of a face of $A$. If $c$ is an open edge, then it lies on an edge of $A$, and so inside it, because it contains no vertex of $C \supseteq$ the vertices of $A$. Otherwise it misses $K_A$: it can meet a closed edge of $A$ only at a point that would be a cut (Lemma 1). Then, being connected, it lies in one face of $A$. If $c$ is a face, it is connected and misses $K_C \supseteq K_A$, so it lies in one face of $A$. So every cell of $C$ lies in exactly one cell of $A$. It is therefore contained in $A$ or disjoint from it, and the same holds for $B$. Line L4 takes a point of $c$ (Lemma 4), and line L5 marks $c$ exactly when $\operatorname{op}$ holds at that point, which is when $c \subseteq \operatorname{op}(A, B)$. The cells partition the plane, so the marked cells form $\operatorname{op}(A, B)$. Line L6 keeps the set by Theorem 3. $\square$

**Theorem 2 (topological operations).** For an SGC with marks $M$, a cell $c$ lies in $\operatorname{int}\bigcup M$ exactly when $c$ and its whole star are marked, and in $\operatorname{cl}\bigcup M$ exactly when $c$ or a cell of its star is marked. So `interior`, `closure`, `boundary` and `regularization` return the stated sets.

*Proof.* Let $p \in c$ and let $U$ be the disc of Lemma 2. If $c$ and its star are marked, then $U \subseteq \bigcup M$. If some cell among them is unmarked, it meets every smaller disc around $p$ (Lemma 2), so $p$ is not interior. The rule depends only on $c$, so the interior is a union of cells. The closure is the complement of the interior of the complement, which gives the second rule. The boundary is the closure minus the interior, computed by Theorem 1, and regularization composes the two. Simplification keeps the set (Theorem 3). $\square$

So the sets represented by planar SGCs are closed under complement, the four Booleans, interior, closure and boundary, with no regularization. The r-sets are not: two closed squares that share an edge are r-sets, and their intersection, the segment, is not.

**Theorem 3 (simplification).** SIMPLIFY returns an SGC that represents the same set. Its result satisfies the two conditions:

- (E) every edge has a mark that differs from that of one of its side faces;
- (V) every vertex either has a number of edges other than two, or two edges that are not collinear, or a mark that differs from that of one of its star cells.

So a second simplification keeps every cell.

*Proof.* *The new skeleton is part of the old one.* `merge_collinear` joins kept edges that lie on one line and share an endpoint. So the union of the merged segments is the union of the closed kept edges, and the new skeleton $K'$ is the union of the closed kept edges and the kept vertices. It is a union of old cells, and every old cell lies in one new cell, by the argument of Theorem 1 with $K' \subseteq K$. An old open edge that is not kept is not in $K'$, since it meets other closed edges only at its ends.

*Each new cell is uniformly marked.* Let $X$ be a new cell and $D$ the old cells inside it.

- If $X$ is a new face, every old edge in $D$ was not kept, so by L7 it is marked like both its side faces. Every old vertex $v$ in $D$ is neither kept nor the end of a kept edge, so it had no kept edges and was not kept. By L8, it is marked like its whole star. Join two cells of $D$ when one is in the star of the other, which gives a graph on $D$. Joined cells have equal marks. The union of the cells in a connected component of this graph is open in $X$: around a point of a cell $c$, a small disc inside $X$ meets only $c$ and its star (Lemma 2), and these cells are in $D$ and joined to $c$. Since $X$ is connected, the graph is connected, and all cells of $D$ share one mark.
- If $X$ is a new edge, it is a run of kept collinear old edges and the old vertices between them. Such a vertex $v$ is not a new vertex. So it is not kept, and it has no third kept edge, which would be a cut. So it has exactly two kept edges, which are collinear. By L8, both are marked like $v$. Walking along $X$, the marks are all equal.
- If $X$ is a new vertex, it is one old vertex.

Line L10 marks $X$ by a point inside it (Lemma 4), so $X$ gets the common mark of $D$, and the set is unchanged.

*(E) and (V).* A new edge contains a kept old edge $e$, and the new faces beside it contain the old faces beside $e$, with their marks. By L7, one of those differs from the mark of $e$. A new vertex $v$ is an old vertex that is kept. Its new edges are the merged runs through its kept old edges, with the same directions and marks. If $v$ had no kept edges, it was kept because some star cell is marked unlike $v$. All of its old star cells lie in the one new face around $v$, which has their common mark, so (V) holds. If $v$ had two kept edges that are not collinear, or marked unlike $v$, then the two new edges are the same. If $v$ had one kept edge or three or more, then so does the new vertex. By (E) and (V), L7 keeps every edge and L8 keeps every vertex on a second pass, so the complex and marks are rebuilt unchanged. $\square$

**Termination.** Every loop in the code runs over a finite list: segments, cuts, half-edges, cycles, cells or vertices. Cycle tracing stops because each step marks a new half-edge and there are finitely many. SIMPLIFY is a single pass and does not recurse. The topological operations call COMBINE and SIMPLIFY a bounded number of times. $\square$
