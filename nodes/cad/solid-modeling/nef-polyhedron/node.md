A Nef polyhedron in $\mathbb{R}^d$ is a set that can be built from finitely many open half-spaces by complement and intersection, and so also by union and difference. Walter Nef introduced these sets in 1978. They contain far more than solids. A Nef polyhedron may be open, closed or neither, and it may have dangling faces, isolated edges and points, and cuts inside it. Nef polyhedra are closed under all Boolean operations and under interior, closure, boundary and [regularization](/regularized-set-operations/). This is not true of the [r-sets](/r-set/) of classical solid modeling, which need regularized operations to stay closed. The usual representation is *selective*: take the arrangement of all bounding hyperplanes, and mark each of its faces as in or out. Hachenberger, Kettner and Mehlhorn built the three-dimensional version, the *selective Nef complex*, with exact arithmetic, and it is the basis of exact Boolean operations and [Minkowski sums](/minkowski-sum-of-polyhedra/) of polyhedra in CGAL. This page gives the data structure in the plane, where the faces are vertices, edges and cells of a line arrangement, and the closure results with proofs.

## In plain words

Draw a few straight lines across a sheet of paper. They cut it into pieces: points where lines cross, bits of lines between crossings, and open regions between the lines. Now colour some of the pieces. Any set you can make from half-planes by "and", "or" and "not" is exactly such a colouring, for some lines.

That gives a simple way to store the set: the lines, and a yes or no for every piece. To combine two sets, draw both sets of lines, so the pieces get smaller, and colour each small piece by the rule. To find the inside of a set, keep a coloured piece only if all pieces that touch it are coloured too. To find the closure, add every piece that touches a coloured one.

## The abstract type

A Nef polygon (the case $d = 2$) is a subset of the plane with these operations:

- **Build** an open or closed half-plane $\{a x + b y + c > 0\}$ or $\{a x + b y + c \ge 0\}$, the empty set or the whole plane.
- **Booleans:** union, intersection, difference, symmetric difference and complement, with no regularization.
- **Topology:** interior, closure, boundary and regularization $\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{int}(\cdot))$.
- **Queries:** does the set contain a point $p$; is it empty; how many marked vertices, edges and cells does it have.
- **Simplify:** drop the lines on which the set does not depend.

The values are the sets, not their descriptions: two formulas that give the same set give equal Nef polygons, and emptiness of the symmetric difference tests this.

## Representation

Let $H$ be a finite set of lines, each in a canonical form $a x + b y + c = 0$ with the first nonzero coefficient of $(a, b)$ equal to 1. The *sign vector* of a point $p$ is $\sigma(p) = (\operatorname{sign}(a_i p_x + b_i p_y + c_i))_{i \in H}$. The *faces* of the arrangement of $H$ are the classes of points with equal sign vectors: vertices, open edges and open cells, of dimension 0, 1 and 2 by the number of zero signs. A face $g$ is *above* a face $f$ when $f \subseteq \operatorname{cl}(g)$ and $f \ne g$; the faces above $f$ are its *star*. In sign vectors, $g$ is above $f$ exactly when $g$ agrees with $f$ wherever $f$ is nonzero.

A Nef polygon is stored as the pair $(H, M)$, where $M$ is the set of marked faces, given by their sign vectors. The arrangement keeps, for each face, its dimension and its star. The code builds them from the lines. Each pair of lines that cross gives a vertex. The vertices on each line, sorted along it, cut it into edges. Each edge has two cells, its sign vector with the zero for its line replaced by $+1$ and by $-1$. The star of an edge is its two cells. The star of a vertex is the edges that end at it and their cells.

## Operations

```pseudo id=main
CONTAINS(A, p)
L1  return σ_A(p) ∈ A.marks                            ▷ O(n) sign tests

COMBINE(A, B, op)                                       ▷ op is and, or, minus or xor
L2  H ← A.lines ∪ B.lines, as canonical lines
L3  build the arrangement of H: its faces, their dimensions and stars
L4  for each face f of the arrangement do
L5      f_A ← f restricted to A.lines; f_B ← f restricted to B.lines
L6      mark f if op(f_A ∈ A.marks, f_B ∈ B.marks)
L7  return (H, marks)

INTERIOR(A)
L8  return the marked faces f whose whole star is marked

CLOSURE(A)
L9  return the faces f that are marked or have a marked face in their star

BOUNDARY(A)
L10 return CLOSURE(A) minus INTERIOR(A)
```

Complement flips every mark on the same arrangement. A half-plane is built on the arrangement of its one line: two cells and the line itself, which is marked when the half-plane is closed. Regularization is CLOSURE(INTERIOR(A)).

**Simplify.** Line $i$ can be dropped when the mark of every face depends only on its signs on the other lines, that is, when the set is a union of faces of the coarser arrangement. The code drops such lines one at a time, rebuilding the arrangement after each.

## Example

Take the closed square $[0,2]^2$, the segment from $(2,1)$ to $(3,1)$ and the point $(4,4)$, each built as an intersection of four closed half-planes, and let $S$ be their union. Its arrangement has the 8 lines $x = 0, 2, 3, 4$ and $y = 0, 1, 2, 4$, with 16 vertices, 40 edges and 25 cells. The line $y = 1$ runs through the square, so the square is two cells, the edge between them and the six vertices and six edges around them.

| set | marked vertices | marked edges | marked cells | lines after simplify |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $S$ | 8 | 8 | 2 | 8 |
| $\operatorname{int} S$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 4 (the open square: 0, 0, 1) |
| $\operatorname{cl} S$ | 8 | 8 | 2 | 8 (it is $S$, which is closed) |
| $\partial S$ | 8 | 7 | 0 | 8 |
| $\operatorname{cl} \operatorname{int} S$ | 6 | 7 | 2 | 4 (the closed square: 4, 4, 1) |
| complement of $S$ | 8 | 32 | 23 | 8 |

The interior of $S$ keeps the inner edge on $y = 1$, since both cells beside it are marked, and drops the segment and the point, whose stars are not marked. Regularization removes the segment and the point and gives back the square, which is the r-set that a classical modeler would keep. A Nef polygon keeps all three parts. Every line of $S$ is needed: $x = 3$ and $y = 1$ for the segment, $x = 4$ and $y = 4$ for the point.

A second example shows why r-sets need regularized operations. The closed half-planes $x \ge 0$ and $x \le 0$ are both r-sets, but their intersection is the line $x = 0$: one marked edge and no cells. It is a Nef polygon, but not an r-set, and its regularization is empty.

## Correctness

The three facts below are proved in the proof file.

1. **Every Nef polygon is a finite union of faces of a line arrangement, and every such union is a Nef polygon.** A half-plane is a union of faces of the arrangement of its line. Unions of faces of a fixed arrangement are closed under complement and intersection, and a finer arrangement refines every face of a coarser one. Conversely, each face is an intersection of open half-planes and lines, and a line is the complement of the union of two open half-planes.
2. **The topological operations depend only on the star.** Every point $p$ of a face $f$ has a neighbourhood that meets exactly $f$ and the faces of its star. So $p$ is interior to a union of faces $S$ exactly when $f$ and its whole star are in $S$, and in the closure exactly when $f$ or a face of its star is in $S$. Lines L8 and L9 compute this, and the results are again unions of faces, so Nef polygons are closed under interior, closure, boundary and regularization.
3. **Booleans are exact on the common refinement.** Each face of the arrangement of $H_A \cup H_B$ lies inside the face of $A$'s arrangement with the restricted sign vector, so line L6 marks the face exactly when its points satisfy the Boolean rule.

By contrast, the r-sets, the closed regular sets with $S = \operatorname{cl}\operatorname{int} S$, are not closed under intersection, as the example of the line shows, and not under complement, since the complement of a closed set is open. The regularized operations $A \cap^* B = \operatorname{cl}\operatorname{int}(A \cap B)$ fix this, and Nef polygons give them by line L8, then line L9.

The tests check the code against the defining formulas. A random formula over 1 to 16 half-planes with integer coefficients in $[-3, 3]$, open or closed, combined by and, or, minus, xor and not, is evaluated at every grid point with denominator 2 in $[-3, 3]^2$ and at every crossing of two of its lines. The answer is compared with the Nef polygon's CONTAINS. Interior, closure and boundary are checked the same way against an independent oracle. The oracle evaluates the formula at $p$ and at $p + \varepsilon d$ for a fixed set of directions $d$ that has a direction in every wedge between two possible lines, with $\varepsilon$ small enough that no other line is crossed. Nested topological operators shrink $\varepsilon$ by a factor of 100 per level. The tests also check $\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cl} = \operatorname{cl}$, $\operatorname{int}\operatorname{int} = \operatorname{int}$, $\operatorname{cl}(\complement S) = \complement\operatorname{int} S$ and $\partial S = \operatorname{cl} S \cap \operatorname{cl}\complement S$, that regularization is idempotent, and that simplification keeps the set.

## Complexity

The following is proved for the plane code, where $n$ is the number of distinct lines:

- An arrangement of $n$ lines has at most $\binom{n}{2}$ vertices, $n^2$ edges and $\binom{n}{2} + n + 1$ cells, so $O(n^2)$ faces. Each sign vector has $n$ entries, so the representation takes $O(n^3)$ space, and building the arrangement takes $O(n^3)$ time, plus $O(n^2 \log n)$ for sorting the vertices along the lines.
- CONTAINS takes $O(n)$ time. A Boolean operation on sets with $n_A$ and $n_B$ lines takes $O((n_A + n_B)^3)$ time. Complement, interior, closure and boundary take time linear in the total size of the stars, which is $O(n^2)$, times $O(n)$ for hashing a sign vector.
- Simplification tries each line and rebuilds the arrangement after each drop, so it takes $O(n^4)$ time.

A plane map, a [doubly connected edge list](/doubly-connected-edge-list/) with a mark on each vertex, edge and face, stores the same thing in $O(n^2)$ space and overlays two maps in $O((N + k) \log N)$ time for maps of total size $N$ with $k$ crossings. In three dimensions, the selective Nef complex of a polyhedron with $N$ vertices, edges and faces is built and combined in time that depends on the overlay of the local sphere maps and on the number of crossings. Hachenberger, Kettner and Mehlhorn measured the running times of their implementation on large models; this page states no worst-case bound for it.

## Variants

- **Selective Nef complex (three dimensions).** Hachenberger, Kettner and Mehlhorn store a cell complex of vertices, edges, facets and volumes, each with a mark. Each vertex has a *sphere map*: the intersection of the set with a small sphere around the vertex. It is a Nef polygon on the sphere, made of points (edges leaving the vertex), arcs (facets) and regions (volumes), each with a mark. Booleans overlay the sphere maps at every vertex of both inputs and at new vertices where edges cross faces, combine the marks, and then simplify by removing the parts that do not change the set. The local picture at a vertex is what Nef called the *local pyramid*.
- **Plane maps.** In the plane the same idea is a plane map with marks, the two-dimensional structure on which the three-dimensional one is built.
- **Without the arrangement.** A set can also be kept as a Boolean formula over half-spaces, a [CSG tree](/constructive-solid-geometry/) without regularization. Membership is then easy, but equality, emptiness and the topological operations need the arrangement.
- **Selective geometric complexes.** The [SGC](/selective-geometric-complex/) of Rossignac and O'Connor uses the same in-or-out marks on the cells of a complex, with curved cells and any dimension, but without the half-space generation of Nef sets.
- **Regularized subsets.** A modeler that needs only solids can apply regularization after every operation and store the result as a [B-rep](/boundary-representation/), which is smaller. The Nef structure is then used only where the exactness or the lower-dimensional parts matter.

## When to use it

Use Nef polyhedra when the Boolean operations must be exact and must handle every degenerate case: touching faces, coplanar faces, edges that meet in a point, sets that pinch to a line. This is the case in robust Minkowski sums, in configuration spaces, and in geometric software where a wrong answer is worse than a slow one. Use them also when the lower-dimensional parts are part of the model, such as cuts, sheets and wires in a non-manifold model, or when the topological operations are needed. For everyday solid modeling with curved surfaces and tolerances, a B-rep kernel with [regularized operations](/regularized-set-operations/) is faster and smaller. The Nef structure is limited to linear geometry.

## Pitfalls

- **The same line twice.** If $x \ge 0$ and $-x \le 0$ give two different line records, the arrangement has a duplicate line, and the faces are counted twice. Bring every line to a canonical form, as the code does, before merging line sets.
- **Floating-point coefficients.** A sign vector is only meaningful if every sign is exact. With floating-point arithmetic, a point near a crossing gets inconsistent signs, and two faces that should be equal differ. Use rational or integer coefficients, or an exact predicate kernel such as the [orientation test](/orientation-test/) with exact arithmetic.
- **Forgetting the lower-dimensional faces.** A Boolean operation that marks only cells computes the regularized result, not the Nef result. The edges and vertices must be marked too.
- **Growth of the arrangement.** Every operation adds the lines of both inputs, and the arrangement grows quadratically in their number. Simplify after operations, or the size grows with the length of the modeling history and not with the complexity of the set.
- **Unbounded sets.** Nef polyhedra include unbounded sets such as half-planes. A modeler that expects bounded solids must check boundedness, or intersect with a big box.

## Implementations

## History

Walter Nef defined the polyhedra generated by half-spaces in a 1978 book, as a basis for computer graphics, and showed their closure under the Boolean and topological operations. The theory was developed further by Hanspeter Bieri and Nef in the 1980s. Rossignac and Requicha argued in 1991 for non-regularized sets in solid modeling, and the [selective geometric complex](/selective-geometric-complex/) of Rossignac and O'Connor (1989) gave a general structure with in-or-out marks on cells. Michael Seel implemented planar Nef polyhedra in CGAL with plane maps. Peter Hachenberger, Lutz Kettner and Kurt Mehlhorn extended this to three dimensions with the selective Nef complex and published the data structure, the algorithms and experiments in 2007. CGAL's exact Boolean operations and Minkowski sums of polyhedra use it.
