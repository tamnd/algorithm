---
title: The swept members give the grown solid, and the classification of grow and shrink is exact
proves: [correctness]
status: full
---

Let $S \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ be the regularized set of a CSG tree of closed axis-aligned rectangles, and let $r > 0$. Let $E$ and $V$ be the edges and vertices computed at line L1, let $U$ be the union of the swept members SLAB$(e, r)$ for $e \in E$ and DISK$(p, r)$ for $p \in V$, and let $T = S \cup U$. A slab is the closed rectangle of points $a + s(b - a) + \tau \nu$ with $s \in [0, 1]$ and $|\tau| \le r$, for an edge from $a$ to $b$ with unit normal $\nu$.

**Lemma 1 (boundary evaluation).** $S$ is the union of the closed grid cells whose centers are in, and $\partial S$ is the union of the edges in $E$.

*Proof.* Every rectangle boundary lies on grid lines, so each open cell lies inside or outside each open rectangle, and the Boolean expression of the tree on the open rectangles is constant on each open cell. The regularized operations take the closure of the interior of the result, so $S$ is the closure of the union of the open cells that are in, which is the union of the closed cells that are in. A point inside a cell, or on a grid edge between two cells of the same status, has a neighborhood of one status. A point on a grid edge between an in cell and an out cell is on $\partial S$. At a grid vertex, going around the four cells, the status changes between two consecutive cells exactly when the vertex is on $\partial S$, and two consecutive cells share an edge that ends at the vertex. So $\partial S$ is the union of the separating grid edges, and merging collinear edges into maximal segments does not change their union. $\square$

**Lemma 2 (decomposition).** $S \uparrow r = T$, and for $x \in S$, $x \in U$ if and only if $\operatorname{dist}(x, \partial S) \le r$.

*Proof.* Each point of a slab or a disk is within $r$ of a point of $\partial S \subseteq S$, so $T \subseteq S \uparrow r$. Let $x$ be within $r$ of a closed set $Z \in \{S, \partial S\}$ with $x \notin S$ when $Z = S$, and let $y$ be a nearest point of $Z$ to $x$. If $Z = S$, then $y \in \partial S$, since otherwise a point of $S$ on the segment from $x$ to $y$ would be closer. By Lemma 1, $y$ lies on an edge $e$ from $a$ to $b$ in $E$, so $\operatorname{dist}(x, e) \le r$. The nearest point of $e$ to $x$ is either a point $a + s(b - a)$ with $0 < s < 1$, where $x - a - s(b - a)$ is normal to $e$ and of length at most $r$, so $x$ is in the slab of $e$, or an end $a$ or $b$, which is in $V$, so $x$ is in its disk. This proves $S \uparrow r \subseteq T$ and, with $Z = \partial S$, the second claim. $\square$

**Lemma 3 (the neighborhood test).** Let $x$ be a point that lies on the boundary of every member of $U$ that contains it, and let $M$ be these members. Line L8 returns true if and only if $x \in \operatorname{int} U$.

*Proof.* Members that do not contain $x$ are at positive distance from it and do not matter. For a rectangle $R \in M$, let $K_R$ be its closed tangent cone at $x$; for $\rho$ smaller than the distance from $x$ to the sides of $R$ that miss $x$, $R \cap B(x, \rho) = (x + K_R) \cap B(x, \rho)$. For a disk with center $c$ and $w = c - x$, $|w| = r$, a point $x + y$ is in the disk if and only if $|y|^2 \le 2 y \cdot w$. The critical directions are $\pm e_1$, $\pm e_2$ and $\pm w^\perp$ for each disk. Each $K_R$ is bounded by axis directions, and $v \cdot w$ has a constant sign on each open sector between two consecutive critical directions. Consecutive directions are less than $\pi$ apart, because the four axis directions are among them, so the sum $m$ of two consecutive ones lies in the open sector between them.

*If the test passes, $x \in \operatorname{int} U$.* Fix an open sector $\sigma$ from $v$ to $v'$ with midpoint $m$. If a rectangle has $m \in K_R$, then $K_R \supseteq \bar\sigma$, since $K_R$ is a closed cone bounded by critical directions, and all of $\bar\sigma$ is covered near $x$. Otherwise a disk has $m \cdot w > 0$, so $v \cdot w > 0$ on all of $\sigma$. By the germ checks at $v$ and $v'$, each end of $\sigma$ is covered by a rectangle with $K_R \supseteq \bar\sigma$, which covers the sector, or by a disk with $v \cdot w' > 0$ (or $v' \cdot w' > 0$), which by continuity has $u \cdot w' \ge c|u|$ for some $c > 0$ on a closed sub-sector next to that end, and so covers the points $x + y$ there with $|y| \le 2c$. The rest of $\sigma$ is a closed sub-sector on which $u \cdot w \ge c' |u|$, covered for $|y| \le 2c'$. Each critical direction $v$ is covered on its ray by a rectangle with $v \in K_R$ or by a disk with $v \cdot w > 0$. There are finitely many sectors and directions, so some $\rho_0 > 0$ works for all, and $B(x, \rho_0) \subseteq U$.

*If a check fails, $x \notin \operatorname{int} U$.* (i) If no member covers the midpoint $m$ of a sector, consider $x + t m$ for small $t > 0$. It is in no rectangle, since $m \notin K_R$, and in no disk, since $m \cdot w \ne 0$ for a non-critical $m$, so $m \cdot w < 0$ and $t^2 |m|^2 > 2 t m \cdot w$. (ii) If the ray of a critical direction $v$ is not covered, $x + t v$ is in no rectangle, and for each disk $v \cdot w \le 0$, so $t^2 |v|^2 > 2 t v \cdot w$. (iii) If the germ on the side of $m$ at $v$ is not covered, let $y_t = x + t v + t^3 m$ for small $t > 0$. Its direction $v + t^2 m$ lies in the open sector between $v$ and $m$. A rectangle that holds $y_t$ for arbitrarily small $t$ has $v + t^2 m \in K_R$, hence $m \in K_R$, since membership in $K_R$ is constant on the open sector, and $v \in K_R$, since $K_R$ is closed; no such rectangle exists. A disk with $v \cdot w < 0$ misses $y_t$ for small $t$. A disk with $v \cdot w = 0$ holds $y_t$ only if $t^2|v|^2 + O(t^4) \le 2 t^3 m \cdot w$, which fails for small $t$. No disk has $v \cdot w > 0$, by assumption. In each case points in no member come arbitrarily close to $x$. $\square$

**Lemma 4 (no positive local minimum).** For $x \in S$ let $d(x) = \operatorname{dist}(x, \partial S)$. No point $x$ with $d(x) > 0$ is a local minimum of $d$. Hence $\operatorname{int}\{x \in S : d(x) \ge r\} = \{x \in S : d(x) > r\}$.

*Proof.* Let $y$ be a nearest point of $\partial S$ to $x$. The points $x + t(y - x)$, $0 < t < 1$, are at distance $(1 - t) d(x) < d(x)$ from $y$. The set $\{d > r\}$ is open, as $d$ is continuous and $\partial S \cap \{d > r\} = \emptyset$, and it is contained in the set $\{d \ge r\}$; a point with $d = r$ has points with $d < r$ arbitrarily close, so it is not interior. $\square$

**Theorem.** For every point $x$ with rational coordinates, CLASSIFY$(T, x)$ returns in, on or out as $x$ is in the interior, on the boundary or outside of $S \uparrow r$, and SHRINK-CLASSIFY$(S, r, x)$ does the same for the regularized shrink $R = \operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{int}(S \downarrow r))$. Every decision is exact.

*Proof.* *Grow.* By Lemma 2, $T = S \uparrow r$. If $x \in \operatorname{int} S$, it is interior to $T$, and line L6 returns in. If $x \in \partial S$, it is inside its edge's slab, or it is a vertex and inside its disk, because $r > 0$, so L6 returns in, and it is indeed interior. If $x \notin S$, a neighborhood of $x$ misses the closed set $S$, so $x$ is interior to $T$ exactly when it is interior to $U$. Line L6 handles a point inside a member, line L7 a point in no member, and lines L8 and L9 are correct by Lemma 3.

*Shrink.* A closed ball $x + B_r$ lies in $S$ exactly when $x \in S$ and $d(x) \ge r$, because for $x \in S$ the distance to the closure of the complement equals $d(x)$. By Lemma 4 and Lemma 2, $\operatorname{int}(S \downarrow r) = \{x \in S : d(x) > r\} = S \setminus U$, so $R = \operatorname{cl}(S \setminus U)$. If $x \notin S$, then $x \notin R \subseteq S$, and L10 returns out. Let $x \in S$. If $x \notin U$, then $x \notin \partial S \subseteq U$, so $x$ has a neighborhood in $S \setminus U$ and is interior to $R$; CLASSIFY returns out and L12 returns in. If $x \in \operatorname{int} U$, a neighborhood of $x$ misses $S \setminus U$, so $x \notin R$; CLASSIFY returns in and L12 returns out. Otherwise $x$ is on the boundary of $U$. Since $\partial S \subseteq \operatorname{int} U$, $x \in \operatorname{int} S$, so points of $S \setminus U$ come arbitrarily close to $x$ and $x \in R$. Each member is the closure of its interior, so points of $\operatorname{int} U$, which are not in $R$, also come arbitrarily close, and $x \in \partial R$; CLASSIFY returns on, by Lemma 3, and so does L12.

*Exactness.* The coordinates, $r$, the corners of the slabs and the centers of the disks are rational; the tests compare coordinates, squared distances with $r^2$, and signs of dot and cross products of rational vectors. $\blacksquare$

**What is not claimed.** The proof covers the planar method on trees of axis-aligned rectangles. In three dimensions the decomposition of Lemma 2 holds with face prisms, edge cylinders and vertex balls by the same argument, but the neighborhood test of Lemma 3 for cylinders and balls, whose tangent cones have curved germs in more directions, is not proved here.
