---
title: Angles that satisfy the ABF constraints give a consistent flat mesh with every triangle counterclockwise and every inner vertex locally one to one
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

**Setting.** $M$ is a triangle mesh with the topology of a disk, with faces oriented so that the outward normal follows the right-hand rule. For each triangle $T = (a, b, c)$ we are given angles $\alpha_{T,a}, \alpha_{T,b}, \alpha_{T,c} \in (0, \pi)$. For a corner of $T$ at $v$, let $\mathrm{nx}(T, v)$ and $\mathrm{pv}(T, v)$ be the corners of $T$ that follow and precede $v$ in the order of $T$. The ABF constraints are:

- (triangle) $\alpha_{T,a} + \alpha_{T,b} + \alpha_{T,c} = \pi$ for every triangle,
- (vertex) $\sum_{T \ni v} \alpha_{T,v} = 2\pi$ for every inner vertex $v$,
- (wheel) $\prod_{T \ni v} \sin \alpha_{T, \mathrm{nx}(T,v)} = \prod_{T \ni v} \sin \alpha_{T, \mathrm{pv}(T,v)}$ for every inner vertex $v$, which is the log form $\sum \log \sin \alpha_{\mathrm{nx}} - \sum \log \sin \alpha_{\mathrm{pv}} = 0$ used in the code.

The *layout* fixes the first edge of the first triangle and then places each new triangle across an edge that is already placed, by the law of sines.

**Claim.** If the angles satisfy the three constraints exactly, then:

1. There are edge lengths $\ell_e > 0$, unique up to one common factor, such that each triangle with the lengths of its edges has the given angles.
2. The layout gives the same position to a vertex whichever triangle places it, so it is a well defined piecewise linear map $x$.
3. Every triangle of $x$ is counterclockwise, ORIENT2D $> 0$, and $x$ is one to one on the star of every inner vertex.
4. If $M$ is a planar mesh with counterclockwise triangles, its own angles minimize the objective with value 0, and the layout is a similarity of $M$.

The layout need not be one to one globally: the claim says nothing about boundary vertices, and two far parts of the boundary can overlap.

**Step 1 (a triangle from its angles).** By the triangle constraint, each triangle has positive angles with sum $\pi$, so a Euclidean triangle with these angles exists and is unique up to a similarity. By the law of sines, in $T$ the length of the edge opposite a corner is proportional to the sine of its angle.

**Step 2 (lengths around an inner vertex).** Let $v$ be an inner vertex with neighbours $w_1, \ldots, w_d$ in the order of the faces, and $T_k = (v, w_k, w_{k+1})$, indices mod $d$. In $T_k$, $\mathrm{nx}(T_k, v) = w_k$ and $\mathrm{pv}(T_k, v) = w_{k+1}$. By the law of sines in $T_k$,

$$\frac{\ell(v w_{k+1})}{\ell(v w_k)} = \frac{\sin \alpha_{T_k, w_k}}{\sin \alpha_{T_k, w_{k+1}}}.$$

Going once around $v$ multiplies the length of $v w_1$ by the product of these ratios, which is 1 by the wheel constraint.

**Step 3 (part 1).** Give the first triangle a scale. Across each inner edge the neighbour triangle gets the scale that gives the shared edge the same length. This defines a scale for every triangle along every path in the dual graph, and the scale is well defined if it is the same along any two paths, that is, if the product of the ratios around every closed dual path is 1. A closed dual path is a closed curve in the disk that misses the vertices. The disk with the inner vertices removed has a fundamental group generated by small loops around the inner vertices, and the scale ratio along a path is a product of positive numbers, so it is a homomorphism to the commutative group $(\mathbb{R}_{>0}, \cdot)$, which is trivial on every generator by Step 2. So it is trivial, and the scales are well defined. Uniqueness up to one factor follows from the connectedness of the dual graph.

**Step 4 (part 2).** Now each triangle is a fixed Euclidean triangle, and two triangles that share an edge give it the same length. The layout places a triangle across a placed edge by a rotation and translation, which is unique once the edge is placed and the triangle is on the side given by its orientation. Developing along a dual path gives a rigid motion for each triangle, and the layout is well defined if the motion along any closed dual path is the identity. As in Step 3, it suffices to check a small loop around an inner vertex $v$. Along that loop the image of $v$ stays fixed, because every triangle of the loop contains $v$, and the image of the edge $v w_1$ is turned by $\alpha_{T_1, v}, \alpha_{T_2, v}, \ldots$ in turn, with lengths that agree by Step 2. The total turn is $2\pi$ by the vertex constraint, so the motion is a rotation by $2\pi$ about the image of $v$, the identity. Rigid motions do not commute, but the motion along a loop is still a homomorphism from the fundamental group of the punctured disk, and it is trivial on the generators, so it is trivial. So a vertex placed by two triangles gets one position.

**Step 5 (part 3).** In the layout each triangle is placed with its corners in the order of the face and a turn of $+\alpha \in (0, \pi)$ from the edge $ab$ to the edge $ac$, so ORIENT2D$(x_a, x_b, x_c) = \ell(ab)\,\ell(ac) \sin \alpha_{T,a} > 0$. Around an inner vertex $v$ the triangles $T_1, \ldots, T_d$ occupy the angular sectors $[\theta_k, \theta_k + \alpha_{T_k, v}]$ at $x_v$, which follow each other and add up to exactly $2\pi$. So they cover a small disk around $x_v$ once, and $x$ is one to one on the star of $v$.

**Step 6 (part 4).** For a planar mesh with counterclockwise triangles, its own angles satisfy the triangle constraint, the vertex constraint (the angles around an inner vertex of a planar mesh fill a full turn), and the wheel constraint (the lengths of a real mesh agree, so the product of the length ratios of Step 2 is 1). The targets $\beta$ are the angles themselves, because the scaling at each inner vertex is by $2\pi / 2\pi = 1$, so the objective $\sum (\alpha - \beta)^2 / \beta^2$ is 0 there, its smallest value. By part 1 the layout has the edge lengths of $M$ times one factor and the same orientation, so it is a similarity of $M$.

**Termination.** The layout visits each triangle once in breadth-first order over the dual graph and stops. The solver for the angles is an iteration with a fixed maximum number of steps, and it stops either with the constraints met to the tolerance or with an error. Its convergence is not proved here: the wheel constraint is not convex, and the iteration uses an approximate Hessian. The tests of the page measure it. $\blacksquare$

**What the floating point result satisfies.** The computed angles meet the constraints only up to rounding, so the triangles do not close exactly, and the position of a vertex depends slightly on the order. The code measures this as the largest distance between a vertex and the position that one of its triangles gives it, and the tests keep it below $10^{-9}$. Parts 2 and 3 hold for the exact angles; for the computed angles they hold up to that residual.
