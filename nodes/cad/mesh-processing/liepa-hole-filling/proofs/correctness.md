---
title: The loop dynamic program is optimal for monotone weights, its patch closes the hole with a consistent orientation, and the membrane fairing has a unique solution
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

**Setup.** The loop is $v_0, \ldots, v_{n-1}$, $n \ge 3$, listed in the direction in which the patch must use its edges. For $0 \le i < k \le n - 1$, let $P(i, k)$ be the abstract polygon $v_i, v_{i+1}, \ldots, v_k$, closed by the chord $\{v_k, v_i\}$. A *triangulation* of $P(i, k)$ is a set of triangles $(i, m, k)$ with $i < m < k$ over these vertices that cut it into triangles without crossing chords, in the sense of a convex polygon with the same vertex order. The polygon $P(0, n - 1)$ is the whole loop. A *weight* is a value $\omega(t)$ for each triangle in a set with a total order $\le$, and an operation $\oplus$ that is associative and commutative with a neutral element $0$. The weight of a triangulation $T$ is $w(T) = \bigoplus_{t \in T} \omega(t)$, and $w(\emptyset) = 0$. The operation is *monotone* if $x \le x'$ implies $x \oplus y \le x' \oplus y$.

**Lemma 1 (decomposition).** For $k - i \ge 2$, every triangulation $T$ of $P(i, k)$ contains exactly one triangle with the chord $\{i, k\}$ as an edge, say $(i, m, k)$, and $T \setminus \{(i, m, k)\}$ is the union of a triangulation of $P(i, m)$ and one of $P(m, k)$. Every choice of $m$ and of the two smaller triangulations gives a triangulation of $P(i, k)$ in this way, and different choices give different triangulations.

*Proof.* The chord $\{i, k\}$ is an edge of the polygon $P(i, k)$, so it lies in exactly one triangle of $T$, whose third vertex $m$ is strictly between $i$ and $k$. The chords $\{i, m\}$ and $\{m, k\}$ split $P(i, k)$ into this triangle, $P(i, m)$ and $P(m, k)$, and no chord of $T$ crosses them, so the other triangles of $T$ fall into the two parts and triangulate them. Conversely, a triangle $(i, m, k)$ and triangulations of the two parts have no crossing chords, since chords inside $P(i, m)$ and inside $P(m, k)$ only use vertices of one part. The triple $m$, left part, right part is recovered from $T$, so the map is one to one. $\square$

The number $t_n$ of triangulations of an $n$-vertex loop therefore satisfies $t_2 = 1$ and $t_{n} = \sum_{m} t_{m+1} t_{n-m}$, the recurrence of the Catalan numbers, so $t_n = C_{n-2}$. The tests check this count for $n \le 9$ by enumeration.

**Claim 1 (optimality).** Suppose $\omega(i, m, k)$ does not depend on the apexes $a, b$ chosen inside the parts, and $\oplus$ is monotone. Then after lines L1 to L7, $\mathrm{cost}[i][k]$ is the least weight of a triangulation of $P(i, k)$, and line L8 returns a triangulation that has this weight.

*Proof.* By induction on $k - i$. For $k = i + 1$ the only triangulation is empty, and line L1 sets $\mathrm{cost} = 0$. For $k - i \ge 2$, the loop of line L2 has already set the costs of all shorter intervals. Line L6 computes, for each $m$, the value $x_m = \mathrm{cost}[i][m] \oplus \mathrm{cost}[m][k] \oplus \omega(i, m, k)$, which by induction is the weight of the triangulation made of $(i, m, k)$ and the stored optimal parts, and line L7 keeps the least $x_m$. Now let $T$ be any triangulation of $P(i, k)$, with the triangle $(i, m, k)$ and the parts $T_1, T_2$ of Lemma 1. Then $w(T) = w(T_1) \oplus w(T_2) \oplus \omega(i, m, k)$, and by induction $\mathrm{cost}[i][m] \le w(T_1)$ and $\mathrm{cost}[m][k] \le w(T_2)$. Monotonicity, used twice with commutativity, gives $x_m \le w(T)$. So the least $x_m$ is at most the weight of every triangulation, and it is the weight of one. Line L8 follows the stored apexes from $(0, n - 1)$ and outputs exactly that triangulation. $\square$

Sum and maximum are monotone, so the program is exact for the total area and for the largest weight of one triangle.

**Claim 2 (the lexicographic pair is not monotone).** With pairs $(\alpha, s)$ that add by $(\alpha, s) \oplus (\alpha', s') = (\max(\alpha, \alpha'), s + s')$ and are ordered lexicographically, the program can return a triangulation that is not optimal.

*Proof.* We have $(1, 10) < (2, 0)$, but $(1, 10) \oplus (5, 0) = (5, 10) > (5, 0) = (2, 0) \oplus (5, 0)$. Take $n = 5$ with $\omega(0, 1, 4) = (5, 0)$, $\omega(1, 2, 4) = (1, 10)$, $\omega(2, 3, 4) = (0, 0)$, $\omega(1, 2, 3) = (2, 0)$, $\omega(1, 3, 4) = (0, 0)$, and $(9, 0)$ for every other triangle. For $P(1, 4)$ the program keeps the part $\{(1, 2, 4), (2, 3, 4)\}$ of weight $(1, 10)$ over $\{(1, 2, 3), (1, 3, 4)\}$ of weight $(2, 0)$. Every triangulation that does not use $(0, 1, 4)$ contains a triangle of weight $(9, 0)$, so the program returns weight $(5, 10)$, while $\{(0, 1, 4), (1, 2, 3), (1, 3, 4)\}$ has weight $(5, 0)$. $\square$

Liepa's weight is such a pair, and its angle term also depends on the apexes $a, b$ of the triangles chosen inside the parts, so Claim 1 does not apply to it. The program then returns a triangulation whose stored weight is its true weight, since each pair of neighbouring patch triangles is a parent and a child in the recursion and is counted once, but the triangulation need not be the best one. The tests compare it with exhaustive search on loops of up to 7 vertices and check only that it is never better than the optimum.

**Claim 3 (the patch closes the hole consistently).** Suppose the mesh is an oriented manifold mesh and the loop is a border loop listed as returned by `border_loops`, with distinct vertices. Then the triangles $(v_i, v_m, v_k)$ of line L8, added to the mesh, give an oriented manifold mesh in which each loop edge has two faces.

*Proof.* Each triangle $(i, m, k)$ with $i < m < k$ uses its edges as $i \to m$, $m \to k$ and $k \to i$. A chord $\{i, k\}$ with $k - i \ge 2$, other than $\{0, n - 1\}$, is the chord of exactly one triangle $(i, m, k)$, where it is used as $k \to i$. In the recursion of line L8, the interval $(i, k)$ comes from a parent triangle that has $i$ and $k$ as its first and second, or second and third, vertices, so the parent uses the same edge as $i \to k$, and no other triangle contains it. So inside the patch each chord has two faces with opposite directions. A loop edge $\{i, i + 1\}$ lies in exactly one patch triangle, used as $i \to i + 1$, and the loop edge $\{n - 1, 0\}$ is used as $n - 1 \to 0$ by the top triangle. The mesh uses each loop edge in the opposite direction, because `border_loops` lists the loop against the border edges. So each loop edge gets a second face with the opposite direction. The chords join loop vertices that are not neighbours in the loop, and if such a chord were already an edge of the mesh, it would get a third face. This case is excluded by the hypothesis of a manifold result and must be checked in use. $\square$

**Claim 4 (refinement keeps the closing).** A centroid split replaces a triangle $(a, b, c)$ by $(a, b, x)$, $(b, c, x)$ and $(c, a, x)$ for a new vertex $x$. It keeps the three directed edges of the triangle, and adds the edges $a x$, $b x$, $c x$, each with two faces in opposite directions. A flip in line L10 replaces two triangles $(a, b, c)$ and $(b, a, d)$ by $(c, a, d)$ and $(d, b, c)$, which keep the four outer directed edges and replace $a \to b$, $b \to a$ by $d \to c$, $c \to d$. The flip is skipped when $c = d$ or when $\{c, d\}$ is already an edge of the patch, so no edge gets a third face, and loop edges are never flipped. So the conclusions of Claim 3 hold after every step. Each round of line L9 either splits a triangle or ends the loop, and the number of rounds is bounded by the parameter, so the refinement stops. Each loop of flips stops after a bounded number of flips.

**Claim 5 (the membrane is unique, flat for a flat loop).** Let $F$ be the new vertices of the patch, and let every vertex of $F$ be joined to a loop vertex by a path of patch edges. Then the system $x_v = \frac{1}{|N(v)|} \sum_{u \in N(v)} x_u$ for $v \in F$, with the loop vertices fixed, has a unique solution, Gauss-Seidel sweeps converge to it, and if the loop lies in a plane, so does the solution.

*Proof.* Write the system as $L x_F = B x_{\mathrm{loop}}$, where $L$ is the graph Laplacian restricted to $F$. $L$ is symmetric and weakly diagonally dominant, with strict dominance in each row of a vertex next to the loop. Because every free vertex reaches the loop, $L$ is irreducibly diagonally dominant, so it is non-singular and positive definite. Gauss-Seidel converges for every symmetric positive definite matrix. For a plane $h(x) = c$ that contains the loop, the function $h$ is affine, so $h(x_v)$ also solves the system for the values $h = c$ on the loop, and the constant $c$ is a solution. By uniqueness $h(x_v) = c$ for all $v \in F$. $\square$
