---
title: The discrete conformal energy is convex with the angle error as its gradient, its minimizer with valid triangles is the unique solution, and a returned answer is correct
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

**Setting.** $M$ is a connected triangle mesh with vertices $1, \dots, n$ and edge lengths $\ell$ that satisfy the strict triangle inequality in every triangle. $F$ is the set of free vertices, it is not all of the vertices, and the other vertices keep a fixed value $u_i$. For $u \in \mathbb{R}^n$ the new lengths are $\tilde\ell_{ij} = e^{(u_i + u_j)/2} \ell_{ij}$, and $x = \log\tilde\ell$ on each side. A triangle is *valid* if its new lengths satisfy the strict triangle inequality. A valid triangle has the angles of the law of cosines; a triangle that is not valid has the angle $\pi$ opposite its longest side and 0 at the other two corners. $\Lambda(\alpha) = -\int_0^\alpha \log\lvert 2\sin t\rvert\,dt$, so $\Lambda'(\alpha) = -\log(2\sin\alpha)$ for $0 < \alpha < \pi$, and $\Lambda(0) = \Lambda(\pi) = 0$. $E$ is the energy of the page, a function of $u_F$. The algorithm and its lines L1 to L12 are as on the page.

**Claim.**

1. For a valid triangle with sides $x_1, x_2, x_3$ (logarithms of lengths) and opposite angles $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3$, the function $f = \sum_m \alpha_m x_m + \sum_m \Lambda(\alpha_m)$ has $\partial f / \partial x_m = \alpha_m$. For $\{a, b, c\} = \{1, 2, 3\}$, $\partial\alpha_a / \partial x_a = \cot\alpha_b + \cot\alpha_c$ and $\partial\alpha_a / \partial x_b = -\cot\alpha_c$.
2. $\partial E / \partial u_i = \Theta_i - \sum\alpha$, the target minus the angle sum at $i$, for every $u$. Where all triangles at $i$ are valid or strictly not valid, the second derivatives of $E$ are the entries of the cotangent Laplacian $H$ of the new lengths, built from the valid triangles only.
3. $H$ is positive semidefinite. If all triangles are valid, $H$ restricted to $F \times F$ is positive definite.
4. $E$ is convex on $\mathbb{R}^F$ and has a continuous gradient.
5. **Key invariant.** $u$ has all triangles valid and the angle sum $\Theta_i$ at every $i \in F$ if and only if $u$ minimizes $E$ and all its triangles are valid. There is at most one such $u$.
6. (Correctness and termination.) In exact arithmetic, each search of line L10 stops, each step that line L11 takes lowers $E$, the loop ends after at most $K$ steps, and a returned $u$ has all triangles valid and all angle errors below $\varepsilon$.

**Step 1 (part 1).** In a valid triangle, the law of sines gives $\tilde\ell_m / (2\sin\alpha_m) = R$, the circumradius, for each $m$, so $x_m - \log(2\sin\alpha_m) = \log R$ for each $m$. Then

$$df = \sum_m \alpha_m\, dx_m + \sum_m \big(x_m + \Lambda'(\alpha_m)\big)\, d\alpha_m = \sum_m \alpha_m\, dx_m + \log R \sum_m d\alpha_m = \sum_m \alpha_m\, dx_m,$$

because the angles add up to $\pi$, so $\sum_m d\alpha_m = 0$. For the angle derivatives, write $l_m = e^{x_m}$ and change $x_c$ alone. The law of cosines $l_c^2 = l_a^2 + l_b^2 - 2 l_a l_b \cos\alpha_c$ gives $2 l_c^2\, dx_c = 2 l_a l_b \sin\alpha_c\, d\alpha_c$, so $\partial\alpha_c / \partial x_c = l_c^2 / (l_a l_b \sin\alpha_c) = l_c^2 / (2A)$, with $A$ the area. The height $h$ from the corner $c$ splits the side $l_c$ into $h\cot\alpha_a$ and $h\cot\alpha_b$, and $2A = l_c h$, so $l_c^2 / (2A) = \cot\alpha_a + \cot\alpha_b$. The law of cosines at the corner $a$, $l_a^2 = l_b^2 + l_c^2 - 2 l_b l_c\cos\alpha_a$, gives $0 = 2 l_c^2\, dx_c - 2 l_b l_c \cos\alpha_a\, dx_c + 2 l_b l_c \sin\alpha_a\, d\alpha_a$. So $\partial\alpha_a / \partial x_c = (l_b\cos\alpha_a - l_c) / (l_b \sin\alpha_a)$. Since $l_c = l_b\cos\alpha_a + l_a\cos\alpha_b$, the numerator is $-l_a\cos\alpha_b$, and by the law of sines $l_a / (l_b\sin\alpha_a) = 1 / \sin\alpha_b$, so $\partial\alpha_a / \partial x_c = -\cot\alpha_b$. The other cases follow by renaming.

**Step 2 (the extended triangle function).** Let $T \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ be the open set of valid $x$, and extend $f$ to all of $\mathbb{R}^3$ with the angles of the setting. Outside $T$, say $e^{x_1} \ge e^{x_2} + e^{x_3}$, the angles are $(\pi, 0, 0)$ and $f = \pi x_1$, so $\nabla f = (\pi, 0, 0) = \alpha$ in the interior of that set. The angles are continuous on $\mathbb{R}^3$: as $l_1 \to l_2 + l_3$ from inside $T$, $\cos\alpha_1 = (l_2^2 + l_3^2 - l_1^2) / (2 l_2 l_3) \to -1$, so $\alpha_1 \to \pi$ and the other two go to 0. So $f$ is continuous.

Take a line $x(s) = x + s y$ and $\varphi(s) = f(x(s))$. The line meets the boundary of $T$ where $e^{x_1 + s y_1} - e^{x_2 + s y_2} - e^{x_3 + s y_3} = 0$ or one of the two similar equations holds. A real exponential sum $\sum_{m=1}^{p} c_m e^{\lambda_m s}$ with distinct $\lambda_m$ and not all $c_m = 0$ has at most $p - 1$ real zeros. For $p = 1$ it has none. For larger $p$, multiply by $e^{-\lambda_1 s}$, which keeps the zeros. The derivative is an exponential sum with $p - 1$ terms $c_m (\lambda_m - \lambda_1) e^{(\lambda_m - \lambda_1)s}$. If these are all 0, the sum is $c_1 e^{\lambda_1 s}$ with $c_1 \ne 0$, which has no zeros. Otherwise the derivative has at most $p - 2$ zeros, and by Rolle's theorem the function has at most $p - 1$. So, after equal exponents are merged, each equation either holds on the whole line or at most at 2 points. If it holds on the whole line, the line stays in the boundary set where the angles are constant, and $\varphi$ is linear with $\varphi' = \alpha \cdot y$. Otherwise $\varphi$ is continuous, and away from finitely many points it is differentiable with $\varphi'(s) = \alpha(x(s)) \cdot y$, which is continuous in $s$. By the mean value theorem, $\varphi$ is then differentiable at the finitely many points too, with the same formula. So every directional derivative of $f$ is $\alpha \cdot y$, and these are continuous, so $f$ is $C^1$ with $\nabla f = \alpha$.

**Step 3 (part 2).** In a triangle $t$ with corners $i, j, k$, the vertex value $u_i$ enters the sides opposite $j$ and opposite $k$, each with the factor $\frac12$, and no other side. By Step 2,

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial u_i}\Big(2 f_t - \pi(u_i + u_j + u_k)\Big) = 2 \cdot \tfrac12 (\alpha_j + \alpha_k) - \pi = -\alpha_i,$$

since the angles of $t$ add up to $\pi$, also for a triangle that is not valid. Adding over the triangles at $i$ and the term $\Theta_i u_i$ gives $\partial E / \partial u_i = \Theta_i - \sum\alpha$.

Inside a region where $t$ stays valid, $\partial\alpha_i / \partial u_j$ for $j \ne i$ is $\frac12$ times the sum of $\partial\alpha_i / \partial x$ over the two sides at $j$, the side opposite $i$ and the side opposite $k$. By part 1 this is $\frac12\big((\cot\alpha_j + \cot\alpha_k) - \cot\alpha_j\big) = \frac12\cot\alpha_k$. So $\partial^2 E / \partial u_i \partial u_j$ gets $-\frac12\cot\alpha_k$ from $t$, where $\alpha_k$ is the angle opposite the edge $ij$. In the same way, $\partial\alpha_i / \partial u_i = \frac12(-\cot\alpha_k - \cot\alpha_j)$, so the diagonal entry gets $\frac12(\cot\alpha_j + \cot\alpha_k)$, minus the sum of the two off-diagonal entries. These are the entries of the cotangent Laplacian of $t$. Where $t$ stays strictly not valid, its angles are constant and it adds nothing.

**Step 4 (part 3).** For a valid triangle $t$, its new lengths form a triangle in the plane. For a function $v$ on its corners, extended linearly over it, Step 3 of the proof on the [BFF](/boundary-first-flattening/) page gives $v^\top H_t v = \frac12 \sum \cot\theta\, (v_a - v_b)^2 = A_t \lVert\nabla v\rVert^2 \ge 0$, summed over the three edges $ab$ of $t$ with opposite angle $\theta$, and this is 0 only if $v$ is constant on $t$. $H$ is the sum of the $H_t$ over the valid triangles, so it is positive semidefinite. If all triangles are valid and $v^\top H v = 0$ for some $v$ that is 0 outside $F$, then $v$ is constant on each triangle, hence on the connected mesh, and it is 0 at a vertex outside $F$, so $v = 0$. So $H_{FF}$ is positive definite.

**Step 5 (part 4).** Each new side $x$ is an affine function of $u$, so a line $u(s) = u + s v$ in $\mathbb{R}^F$ gives a line in the sides of each triangle. Let $\psi(s) = E(u(s))$. By Steps 2 and 3, $\psi$ is $C^1$ with $\psi'(s) = \nabla E(u(s)) \cdot v$, so $\nabla E$ is continuous. By Step 2 there are finitely many points of the line where a triangle changes between valid and not valid. Between two such points, every triangle is valid throughout, not valid throughout, or on the boundary throughout with constant angles, so $\psi$ is twice differentiable there with $\psi'' = v^\top H v \ge 0$ by Step 4. So $\psi'$ does not decrease on each of these intervals, and since $\psi'$ is continuous on the whole line, it does not decrease on the whole line. A $C^1$ function of one variable with a nondecreasing derivative is convex. This holds on every line, so $E$ is convex.

**Step 6 (part 5).** For a convex $C^1$ function, $u$ is a minimizer if and only if $\nabla E(u) = 0$. By part 2, $\nabla E(u) = 0$ says exactly that the angle sum at every $i \in F$ is $\Theta_i$. This proves the equivalence.

For uniqueness, let $u^*$ be a minimizer with all triangles valid and $u'$ any other minimizer, $v = u' - u^* \ne 0$. By convexity, $\psi(s) = E(u^* + s v)$ is convex and has its minimum at $s = 0$ and $s = 1$, so $\psi$ is constant on $[0, 1]$. Validity is a strict inequality, so all triangles stay valid for $s$ in some $[0, \delta)$, and there $\psi''(s) = v^\top H_{FF} v > 0$ by Step 4 for $s$ near 0, which contradicts $\psi$ being constant. So $u^*$ is the only minimizer.

On a closed mesh with one fixed vertex $f$ and targets with $\sum_{i \in F} \Theta_i + \Theta_f = \pi \cdot (\text{number of triangles})$, the condition of the discrete Gauss-Bonnet theorem, the angle sum at $f$ is also $\Theta_f$ at the solution, because the angle sums of all vertices add up to $\pi$ times the number of triangles.

**Step 7 (part 6).** Line L5 returns only when all $\lvert g_i\rvert < \varepsilon$, and line L6 returns $u$ only when all triangles are valid. For line L10, let $g \ne 0$ be the gradient at line L9. If $H_{FF}$ is not singular, it is positive definite by Step 4 (positive semidefinite and nonsingular), so the slope $g \cdot d = -g^\top H_{FF}^{-1} g < 0$. If it is singular, $d = -g$ and $g \cdot d = -\lVert g \rVert^2 < 0$. Since $E$ is $C^1$, $E(u + t d) = E(u) + t\, g \cdot d + o(t)$, and since $c < 1$, $E(u + t d) \le E(u) + c\, t\, g \cdot d$ for all small enough $t > 0$. So the halving stops, and the accepted step lowers $E$ by at least $c\, t\, \lvert g \cdot d \rvert > 0$. The loop of line L2 runs at most $K$ times, and each pass does a bounded amount of work, so the algorithm terminates.

The code of the page adds two guards for floating point: it stops the halving at $t < 10^{-12}$, and it takes the full step without the test when $\lvert g \cdot d\rvert$ is below $10^{-12} \max(1, \lvert E \rvert)$, where rounding makes the test meaningless. Neither guard changes what a returned $u$ satisfies, because the checks of lines L5 and L6 are made on the final $u$.

**What is not claimed.** The proof does not show that a solution with valid triangles exists, and it need not: on the pyramid of the page, no $u$ gives the apex an angle sum of $4\pi$ or more with valid triangles. It does not show that Newton's method reaches the minimizer from every start, or bound the number of steps. Near a minimizer with valid triangles, $H_{FF}$ is positive definite and $E$ is smooth, and the standard theory of Newton's method with backtracking then gives quadratic convergence once the iterates are close; this is cited, not proved here. $\blacksquare$
