A configuration of points and lines is constructible by ruler and compass when every coordinate can be computed from the given values by the four arithmetic operations and square roots. These are exactly the numbers that a geometer can draw with a straightedge and a compass, since a straight line meets a line in a point with rational coordinates over the known ones, and a circle meets a line or a circle in points that need one square root. Wantzel proved in 1837 that a number is constructible if and only if it lies at the top of a tower of fields, each of degree 2 over the one below. For constraint solving, the notion gives the exact scope of the [graph-constructive](/constructive-constraint-solving/) solvers: a solver that only intersects lines and circles can solve a system exactly when its solution is constructible, and many well-constrained systems are not.

## In plain words

With a ruler you can draw the line through two points. With a compass you can draw a circle with a given center through a given point. New points are where these lines and circles cross. In numbers, finding where two lines cross needs only adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing; finding where a circle crosses something needs one square root more.

So a shape can be drawn with ruler and compass exactly when its numbers come from the given lengths by these operations and square roots, perhaps nested many times. Some shapes need more: an angle of 20 degrees, or a regular seven-sided figure, needs the solution of an equation of degree 3 that no chain of square roots can give. A CAD solver that works only with lines and circles cannot draw such a shape exactly, even when the constraints fix it.

## The idea

**Fields of constructible numbers.** Let $F_0$ be the field $\mathbb{Q}(c_1, \ldots, c_m)$ generated by the values of the constraints, and remove the rigid motions by placing one point at the origin and a second point on the $x$-axis at a known distance. A step of ruler and compass solves, over the current field $F$, either a linear system (two lines) or one quadratic equation (a line and a circle, or two circles, which reduce to a line and a circle by subtraction). The new coordinates lie in $F$ or in $F(\sqrt{\delta})$ for some $\delta \in F$, an extension of degree 1 or 2.

**Wantzel's criterion.** A real number $\alpha$ is constructible from $F_0$ if and only if there is a chain of fields
$$ F_0 \subset F_1 \subset \cdots \subset F_k, \qquad F_{i+1} = F_i(\sqrt{\delta_i}),\ \delta_i \in F_i,\ \delta_i > 0, $$
with $\alpha \in F_k$. Each step has degree 2, so $[F_k : F_0] = 2^k$, and the degree of the [minimal polynomial](/minimal-polynomial-algebraic-element/) of $\alpha$ over $F_0$ divides $2^k$. This gives the test that is used most:

> If the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ over $F_0$ has a degree that is not a power of 2, then $\alpha$ is not constructible.

The converse of the test is false: a root of an irreducible quartic is constructible exactly when the Galois group of the quartic has an order that is a power of 2, which fails for the groups $S_4$ and $A_4$ of a general quartic. For a cubic the test is complete: an [irreducible](/irreducible-polynomial/) cubic over $F_0$ has no constructible root, and the [rational root theorem](/rational-root-theorem/) often proves the irreducibility over $\mathbb{Q}$.

**Constructible constraint systems.** A well-constrained system is *ruler-and-compass constructible*, or RC-constructible, when every solution has coordinates in a tower of quadratic extensions of the field of its values, for generic values. Each merge of three rigid clusters in the [Fudos-Hoffmann reduction](/fudos-hoffmann-reduction/), and each triangle in [Owen's decomposition](/owen-triconnected-decomposition/), is a [circle-circle intersection](/circle-circle-intersection/) after a rigid motion, so a triangle-decomposable system is constructible, and its solutions lie in an extension of degree at most $2^{n-2}$ for $n$ points; each square root also doubles the number of solutions. Gao and Chou (1998) gave a symbolic decision method: transform the polynomial system into triangular form, in the style of [Wu's method](/wu-characteristic-set-method/), and decide whether the triangular sets can be brought to a form in which each new variable satisfies an equation of degree at most 2 over the earlier ones.

**Systems that are not constructible.** Owen and Power (2007) proved that for a generic 3-connected planar Laman graph with more than three vertices, the coordinates of the solution are not even solvable by radicals. So for these graphs, which include the triangular prism, no method based on square roots, or on radicals of any order, gives the solution exactly; a solver must use a numerical method or an exact representation of algebraic numbers. This explains why triangle-decomposable is a strictly smaller class than well-constrained: the prism is well-constrained for generic lengths, has up to 24 complex solutions, and is not constructible.

**Signs and exact arithmetic.** Each square root has two signs, and each sign is one branch of the solution. The choice is made by the orientation of the draft, which is the [root identification problem](/root-identification-problem/). Since the coordinates are nested square roots, a solver can keep them exactly as elements of the tower and decide each sign exactly, in the style of [exact geometric computation](/exact-geometric-computation/), instead of with floating point, where a sign near zero is unsafe because of [catastrophic cancellation](/catastrophic-cancellation/).

## Examples

**A triangle with sides 2, 3 and 4.** Put $A = (0, 0)$ and $B = (4, 0)$, and ask for $C$ with $\lvert AC \rvert = 2$ and $\lvert BC \rvert = 3$. Subtracting the two circle equations $x^2 + y^2 = 4$ and $(x - 4)^2 + y^2 = 9$ gives the line $8x = 16 + 4 - 9$, so $x = 11/8$, and then $y^2 = 4 - 121/64 = 135/64$, so $C = (11/8, \pm 3\sqrt{15}/8)$. The tower is $\mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{15})$, of degree 2, and the two signs are the two solutions.

**Trisection of 60 degrees.** An angle of 60 degrees is constructible, since $\cos 60^\circ = 1/2$. Its third, 20 degrees, would give $c = \cos 20^\circ$, and the identity $\cos 3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta$ gives $8c^3 - 6c - 1 = 0$. The rational candidates for a root are $\pm 1, \pm 1/2, \pm 1/4, \pm 1/8$, and none of them is a root, so the cubic is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ and $c$ has degree 3. A sketch with four lines through one point, the outer two at an angle of 60 degrees, and equal-angle constraints that split this angle into three equal parts, has only finitely many solutions, but no ruler-and-compass solver places the inner lines exactly.

**The regular heptagon.** The number $x = 2\cos(2\pi/7)$ is a root of $x^3 + x^2 - 2x - 1$, so $c = \cos(2\pi/7)$ is a root of $8c^3 + 4c^2 - 4c - 1$. Again no rational candidate is a root, the degree is 3, and a regular heptagon is not constructible. A sketch of seven equal sides and seven equal angles fixes the heptagon, but a constructive solver cannot draw it.

**The triangular prism.** Two triangles joined by three disjoint bars, with generic lengths, are well-constrained, but by Owen and Power the coordinates are not solvable by radicals. The [DR-plan](/dr-plan/) handles this part with a numerical or algebraic solver.

## Where it is used

- **Scope of constructive solvers.** [Graph-constructive solving](/constructive-constraint-solving/) is complete exactly for the systems that its rules decompose; constructibility explains why the rules stop where they do, and why a solver needs a fallback for parts such as the prism.
- **Exact solutions.** A constructible solution can be kept as nested square roots, which allows exact comparisons and exact signs in the later steps of modeling.
- **All solutions.** The tower shows the number of solutions directly: one sign per square root, so up to $2^k$ real solutions for a tower of height $k$.
- **Theorem proving in geometry.** The symbolic methods that decide constructibility, such as Gao and Chou's, are also used to prove geometric theorems with [Gröbner bases](/groebner-basis/) and characteristic sets.
- **Classical problems.** The trisection of the angle, the duplication of the cube (the root of $x^3 - 2$, of degree 3) and the regular polygons are the standard examples; a regular $n$-gon is constructible exactly when $n$ is a power of 2 times a product of distinct Fermat primes (Gauss and Wantzel).
