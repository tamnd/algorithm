We use the definitions of the page. The mesh is closed and oriented: each half-edge $(u, v)$ lies in exactly one face, its twin $(v, u)$ lies in exactly one other face, and the faces around each vertex form one cycle (a manifold vertex). A face $f$ is upper if $s_f = +1$ and lower if $s_f = -1$, after line L1. $B$ is the set of half-edges of upper faces whose twins lie in lower faces.

**Lemma 1 (exact signs).** For a planar face with vertices $p_0, \dots, p_{k-1}$ in counterclockwise order seen from outside, the Newell vector $n = \sum_i (y_i - y_{i+1})(z_i + z_{i+1}, \dots)$ equals $\sum_i p_i \times p_{i+1}$, which is twice the vector area of the face and points outward. On integer input it is an integer vector, so line L1 computes the sign of $n_f \cdot d$ with no error.

*Proof.* Expand the first component of $p_i \times p_{i+1}$: it is $y_i z_{i+1} - z_i y_{i+1}$. The term of the Newell sum is $(y_i - y_{i+1})(z_i + z_{i+1}) = y_i z_i - y_{i+1} z_{i+1} + y_i z_{i+1} - y_{i+1} z_i$. The first two terms cancel in the cyclic sum. The other components are the same with the coordinates shifted. The vector $\frac12 \sum_i p_i \times p_{i+1}$ is the vector area of a closed polygon, which for a planar polygon is its area times its unit normal, with the orientation of the vertex order. $\square$

**Lemma 2 (the turn ends).** For $h = (u, v) \in B$ in the upper face $f$, the loop of lines L4 and L5 ends after at most $\deg(v)$ steps, at a half-edge $g = (v, w)$ of an upper face whose twin lies in a lower face.

*Proof.* Let $f_0 = f, f_1, f_2, \dots$ be the faces around $v$ in the order in which the loop visits them. Line L4 takes the half-edge $g_0 = (v, w_0)$ of $f_0$ that leaves $v$. If the twin $(w_0, v)$ lies in an upper face $f_1$, line L5 sets $g_1 = \mathrm{succ}(w_0, v) = (v, w_1)$, the half-edge of $f_1$ that leaves $v$, and so on. Each step crosses one edge at $v$ and moves to the next face in the cyclic order around $v$. The faces around $v$ form one cycle, and the edge $\{u, v\}$ is in it, with $f$ on one side and the lower face of $(v, u)$ on the other. Going around $v$ from $f$, the loop reaches the edge $\{u, v\}$ from the side of the lower face only after it has passed that lower face, so it stops at the latest at the edge just before the lower face. Every face it visits is upper, since it only crosses into a face when that face is upper. $\square$

**Lemma 3 (next is a permutation of $B$).** The map $h \mapsto \mathrm{next}[h]$ is a bijection from $B$ to $B$.

*Proof.* By Lemma 2, $\mathrm{next}[h] \in B$. Define $\mathrm{prev}$ by the same turn in the opposite direction: for $g = (v, w) \in B$ in the upper face $f'$, take the half-edge of $f'$ that enters $v$; while its twin lies in an upper face, cross into that face and take its half-edge that enters $v$. This visits, in reverse order, exactly the upper faces that the turn from $h$ to $g$ visited, because both turns move between the same consecutive faces around $v$ and both stop at the first lower face. So $\mathrm{prev}(\mathrm{next}[h]) = h$, and $\mathrm{next}$ is injective. An injective map of a finite set to itself is a bijection. $\square$

**Theorem 1.** PARTING-LOOPS returns loops that are closed, in which every edge between an upper face and a lower face occurs exactly once, with the upper face on its left.

*Proof.* Each edge between an upper and a lower face has exactly one half-edge in an upper face, and line L3 keeps exactly that one, with the upper face on its left by the orientation of the faces. By Lemma 3, $\mathrm{next}$ is a permutation of $B$, so $B$ splits into disjoint cycles. Line L7 starts at each half-edge not yet used and follows $\mathrm{next}$. In a permutation the orbit of $h$ returns to $h$ before it repeats any other element, so line L7 records exactly the cycle of $h$ and marks it used. Each half-edge of $B$ is in exactly one recorded cycle. Consecutive half-edges $h = (u, v)$ and $\mathrm{next}[h] = (v, w)$ share the vertex $v$, so the start vertices form a closed loop. $\square$

**Theorem 2.** Let $P$ be a convex polyhedron (a bounded 3-dimensional [convex polytope](/convex-polytope/)) whose faces are its facets, and let $d \ne 0$ with $n_f \cdot d \ne 0$ for every facet $f$. Let $S$ be the union of the silhouette edges. Then $S$ is homeomorphic to a circle. So the silhouette edges form one simple cycle, and PARTING-LOOPS returns one loop with no repeated vertex.

*Proof.* Write $P = \{x : n_f \cdot x \le c_f \text{ for all facets } f\}$. Let $U$ be the union of the closed upper facets and $L$ the union of the closed lower facets. Let $\pi$ be the orthogonal projection onto the plane $H$ through the origin normal to $d$, and $Q = \pi(P)$. $Q$ is a convex polygon with nonempty interior in $H$, since $P$ has nonempty interior.

*Step 1: $S = U \cap L$.* A point in the relative interior of an edge lies in exactly the two facets of that edge, and a point in the relative interior of a facet lies only in that facet. So a point of $U \cap L$ that is not a vertex lies on an edge between an upper and a lower facet. If a vertex $v$ is in $U \cap L$, the facets around $v$ in cyclic order include both kinds, so the sign changes at least twice around $v$, at edges between consecutive facets, and these are silhouette edges through $v$. So $U \cap L \subseteq S$, and $S \subseteq U \cap L$ is clear.

*Step 2: $\pi$ restricted to $U$ is a homeomorphism onto $Q$.* Take $y \in Q$. The line $\pi^{-1}(y)$ meets $P$ in a segment with a highest point $b$ in the direction $d$. For small $\varepsilon > 0$, $b + \varepsilon d \notin P$. The facets not through $b$ have slack at $b$, so some facet $f$ through $b$ has $n_f \cdot (b + \varepsilon d) > c_f$, that is $n_f \cdot d > 0$: $f$ is upper and $b \in U$. If another point $p = b - t d$ with $t > 0$ of the segment lay on an upper facet $g$, then $n_g \cdot p = c_g$ and $n_g \cdot b = c_g + t\, n_g \cdot d > c_g$, which contradicts $b \in P$. So each $y \in Q$ has exactly one preimage in $U$. The map $\pi|_U$ is a continuous bijection from a compact set to a Hausdorff space, so it is a homeomorphism.

*Step 3: $\pi(S) \subseteq \partial Q$.* Let $x$ lie on an edge between the upper facet $f$ and the lower facet $g$. Set $m = (n_f \cdot d)\, n_g - (n_g \cdot d)\, n_f$. The two coefficients are positive, and $n_f, n_g$ are not parallel, so $m \ne 0$, and $m \cdot d = 0$. For every $p \in P$, $m \cdot p \le (n_f \cdot d) c_g - (n_g \cdot d) c_f = m \cdot x$. Since $m \perp d$, $m \cdot \pi(p) = m \cdot p$, so the nonzero linear function $m$ on $H$ has its maximum over $Q$ at $\pi(x)$, and $\pi(x) \in \partial Q$.

*Step 4: $\pi(U \setminus S) \subseteq \operatorname{int} Q$.* Let $x \in U \setminus S$. By Step 1, every facet through $x$ is upper. Suppose $\pi(x) \in \partial Q$. Then some $m \in H$, $m \ne 0$, has $m \cdot \pi(x) \ge m \cdot y$ for all $y \in Q$, so $m \cdot x \ge m \cdot p$ for all $p \in P$, since $m \perp d$. So $m$ is in the normal cone of $P$ at $x$, which is the cone generated by the normals of the facets through $x$: $m = \sum_f \lambda_f n_f$ with $\lambda_f \ge 0$, not all zero. Then $m \cdot d = \sum_f \lambda_f (n_f \cdot d) > 0$, which contradicts $m \cdot d = 0$.

*Step 5.* By Steps 2 to 4, $\pi|_U$ is a homeomorphism that maps $S$ into $\partial Q$ and $U \setminus S$ into $\operatorname{int} Q$. It is onto $Q$, so every point of $\partial Q$ has its preimage in $S$. So $\pi|_S$ is a homeomorphism from $S$ onto $\partial Q$, the boundary of a convex polygon with nonempty interior, which is a simple closed curve. A graph whose underlying space is homeomorphic to a circle is connected and has degree 2 at every vertex, so it is one simple cycle.

*The algorithm.* Each vertex of $S$ has exactly two silhouette edges, one half-edge in $B$ entering it and one leaving it, so $\mathrm{next}$ has no choice to make and follows the cycle. The cycle is connected, so by Theorem 1 line L7 records it as one loop, and each vertex of the cycle appears once. $\blacksquare$
