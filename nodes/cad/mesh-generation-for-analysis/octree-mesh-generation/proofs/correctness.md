---
title: OCTREE-MESH gives a balanced octree and a conforming tetrahedral mesh whose template tetrahedra have radius-edge ratio at most the square root of 5/2 and dihedral angles between 35.26 and 125.26 degrees
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

**Claim.** Let $D \ge 1$, $S = 2^{D+1}$, and let the ball $|x - c|^2 < r^2$, with rational $c$ and $r^2$, lie inside the root cube $[0, S]^3$, with $f(v) \ne 0$ at every vertex of the template mesh. Then:

1. both loops of OCTREE-MESH stop, and at the end any two leaves that share a face or an edge differ by at most one level;
2. the tetrahedra of L6 to L10 have positive volume and form a face-to-face tetrahedralization of the root cube;
3. each of them has radius-edge ratio $R/\ell_{\min} \le \sqrt{5/2}$ and all its dihedral angles in $[\theta_0, 90° + \theta_0]$, where $\theta_0 = \arctan(1/\sqrt2) \approx 35.26°$, and both bounds are attained;
4. the tetrahedra of L12 to L16 form a face-to-face tetrahedralization of the polyhedron $\{x : f_{\mathrm{lin}}(x) > 0\}$, where $f_{\mathrm{lin}}$ is the piecewise linear interpolant of $f$ on the template mesh, and this polyhedron lies inside the ball.

**What is not claimed.** No bound is claimed for the tetrahedra made by the cut. The bounds of Mitchell and Vavasis are cited on the page and not proved here.

**Notation.** A box of level $k$ has side $w_k = S/2^k$. Two closed boxes have *contact of dimension* $d$ if their intersection is a point, a segment or a square for $d = 0, 1, 2$. The *tree corners* are the corners of the leaves. Since $w_D = 2$, every corner, box center and center of a quarter of a face is an integer point.

**Step 1: the loops stop and the tree is balanced.** The first loop splits only leaves of level less than $D$, and each box is split at most once, so it stops. In the second loop, a leaf is split only if a leaf two or more levels finer touches it, so leaves of level $D$ are never split, and the number of possible splits is again finite. The second loop stops only when no leaf has a face or edge contact with a leaf two or more levels finer. The test `unbalanced` looks at the box $N$ of the same level across each of the 6 faces and 12 edges of the leaf $b$; if $N$ is split, a leaf two levels finer than $b$ touches $b$ along a face or an edge exactly when one of the children of $N$ that touch $b$ along that face or edge is split. So the loop condition is the balance condition.

**Step 2: the vertices on a boundary square.** Let $b$ be a leaf of level $k$ and $F$ a face of $b$, and let $N$ be the box of level $k$ across $F$.

First a fact. If $q$ is a corner of a leaf $b'$ and $q$ lies in the relative interior of an edge or a face of a leaf $c$, then $b'$ and $c$ have contact of dimension at least 1. Indeed, one of the three edges of $b'$ at $q$ is parallel to that edge, or lies in that face, and starts into it, so a segment of it lies in $c$. By Step 1, $b'$ and $c$ then differ by at most one level.

*Case A: $N$ is not split.* The square is $s = F$. Take a tree corner $q \in F$ that is not a corner of $F$. By the fact, $q$ is a corner of a leaf of level at most $k + 1$, so $q$ lies on the lattice $w_{k+1} \mathbb{Z}^3$. On the closed face $F$ this lattice has 9 points: the 4 corners, the 4 side midpoints and the center. The center is not a tree corner: on the side of $b$ the only leaf there is $b$, and on the other side nothing finer than level $k$ exists, since $N$ is not split. So the ring of $s$ is its corners and some side midpoints.

*Case B: $N$ is split.* Its four children that touch $F$ are leaves, since a split child would put leaves of level $k + 2$ in face contact with $b$. So $F$ is cut into four quarters, each the face of a leaf $c$ of level $k + 1$. Let $s$ be one quarter and $q$ a tree corner on a side of $s$ that is not a corner of $s$. The side lies in $F$, so by the fact applied to $b$ (when $q$ is inside a side of $F$ or inside $F$), $q$ is a corner of a leaf of level at most $k + 1$, so it lies on $w_{k+1}\mathbb{Z}^3$. A side of $s$ has length $w_{k+1}$ and contains no such lattice point except its ends. So the ring of $s$ is its four corners only.

*Both sides agree.* If the leaf across $F$ has level $k$, it is in Case A for the same face. If the leaves across are the children of $N$, each of them sees, across its face, a box of level $k + 1$ inside the leaf $b$, which is not in the tree, so it is in Case A with its whole face, which is one quarter $s$. If the leaf across has level $k - 1$, it is in Case B, and $F$ is one of its quarters. In every case both leaves cut the common part of their faces into the same squares. They compute the ring of each square from the same global set of tree corners, and the same center $m$, so L9 and L10 make the same triangles on every square, from the two sides.

**Step 3: the template mesh tiles the cube.** Fix a leaf $b$ with center $p$. Its boundary is cut by Step 2 into squares, and each square into the triangles $(m, r_i, r_{i+1})$. These triangles cover the boundary of $b$ without overlap. The cube $b$ is convex and $p$ is an interior point, so it lies on no plane of a face. Hence the cones from $p$ over these triangles are tetrahedra of positive volume that tile $b$, meeting face to face. The leaves tile the root cube, and by Step 2 the triangles on every shared square are the same from both sides. So the tetrahedra of all leaves tile the root cube face to face.

**Step 4: the shape bound.** Rotations, reflections and scaling preserve both the radius-edge ratio and the dihedral angles. So scale $b$ to $[0, 8]^3$, with $p = (4, 4, 4)$, and move the square to the bottom face $z = 0$. By Step 2 the square is either the whole face, with center $(4, 4, 0)$, its 4 corners and any set of its 4 side midpoints, or one of the 4 quarters, with center $(2, 2, 0)$, $(6, 2, 0)$, $(2, 6, 0)$ or $(6, 6, 0)$ and its 4 corners only. The whole face gives $\sum_{M} (4 + |M|) = 16 \cdot 4 + 32 = 96$ tetrahedra over the 16 sets $M$ of midpoints, and the quarters give $4 \cdot 4 = 16$. So every template tetrahedron is similar to one of these 112. Some cases do not occur in a balanced tree; including them only makes the bound safe.

For each of the 112, the squared radius-edge ratio is a rational number, computed exactly from the circumcenter by Cramer's rule. The largest is $5/2$, attained for instance by $(4,4,4), (2,2,0), (0,0,0), (4,0,0)$. Each dihedral angle $\theta$ has $\cos\theta = -n_1 \cdot n_2 / (|n_1| |n_2|)$ for the outward normals $n_1, n_2$ of its two faces, which are integer vectors, so $\cos^2\theta$ is rational. Over the 112 tetrahedra, the angles that occur are $\theta_0$, $45°$, $90° - \theta_0 \approx 54.74°$, $60°$, $90°$ and $90° + \theta_0$, with $\cos^2\theta \in \{2/3, 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 0, 1/3\}$. The smallest is $\theta_0 = \arctan(1/\sqrt2)$ and the largest is $90° + \theta_0$; the quarter tetrahedron $(4,4,4), (2,2,0), (0,0,0), (4,0,0)$ has both, at its edges from $(4,4,4)$ to $(4,0,0)$ and from $(2,2,0)$ to $(4,0,0)$. The tetrahedra on whole faces without midpoints, the standard cut of a cube into 24, have angles $45°$, $60°$ and $90°$ only. The test file repeats this enumeration.

**Step 5: the cut.** For a tetrahedron $\tau$ with vertices of values $f \ne 0$, let $g$ be the linear function equal to $f$ at its vertices. The kept part $\tau \cap \{g > 0\}$ is convex, and its new vertices are the zeros of $g$ on the edges with a sign change, the cut points of L14 to L16.

- With one positive vertex $a$, it is the tetrahedron $a$ and the three cut points on the edges at $a$.
- With two positive vertices $a, b$ and negative $c, d$, its facets are the triangles $(a, c_{ac}, c_{ad})$ and $(b, c_{bc}, c_{bd})$ on the faces $acd$ and $bcd$, the quadrilaterals $(a, b, c_{bc}, c_{ac})$ and $(a, b, c_{bd}, c_{ad})$ on the faces $abc$ and $abd$, and the quadrilateral $(c_{ac}, c_{ad}, c_{bd}, c_{bc})$ on the plane $g = 0$. It is a prism with the edges $ab$, $c_{ac} c_{bc}$ and $c_{ad} c_{bd}$.
- With three positive vertices $a, b, c$ and negative $d$, it is a prism with the triangles $abc$ and $c_{ad} c_{bd} c_{cd}$.

*Prism lemma.* Let a convex prism have bottom $b_0 b_1 b_2$, top $t_0 t_1 t_2$ and quadrilaterals $Q_i = b_i b_{i+1} t_{i+1} t_i$, and let each $Q_i$ take the diagonal through its smallest vertex in a total order. Then `split_prism` returns three tetrahedra of positive volume that tile the prism and induce exactly these diagonals. Proof: let $v$ be the smallest of the six vertices, say $v = b_0$ (the other cases are the same after renaming). It is the smallest vertex of $Q_0$ and $Q_2$, so their diagonals are $b_0 t_1$ and $b_0 t_2$. The cone from $b_0$ over the facets that do not contain $b_0$, which are the top triangle and $Q_1$, tiles the prism, since the prism is convex. Splitting $Q_1$ by its chosen diagonal gives the three tetrahedra $(b_0, t_0, t_1, t_2)$ and two with base on $Q_1$. Each has positive volume, since $b_0$ is not on the plane of a facet it does not belong to. On $Q_0$ and $Q_2$ the cone induces the fan from $b_0$, which is the chosen diagonal. $\square$

So the faces of the output inside a face $T$ of the template mesh are: nothing if all three vertices are negative, $T$ if all are positive, the triangle at the positive vertex if one is, and the quadrilateral $T \cap \{g > 0\}$ split by the diagonal through its smallest vertex if two are. The function $g$ on $T$ depends only on the values of $f$ at the vertices of $T$, and the order is global, so the two tetrahedra that share $T$ produce the same triangles on it. The faces on the planes $g = 0$ lie on the boundary of $\{f_{\mathrm{lin}} > 0\}$. With Step 3, this shows that the kept tetrahedra tile $\{f_{\mathrm{lin}} > 0\}$ face to face.

Last, $f$ is concave, since $-|x - c|^2$ is. On each tetrahedron, at a point $x = \sum \lambda_i v_i$ with $\lambda_i \ge 0$ and $\sum \lambda_i = 1$, we get $f(x) \ge \sum \lambda_i f(v_i) = g(x)$. So $g > 0$ implies $f > 0$, and the kept polyhedron lies in the ball.

Together, Steps 1 to 5 prove the claim. The test file checks Steps 1, 3, 4 and 5 directly: balance by brute force over all pairs of leaves, the tiling by exact volumes and face twins, the 112 shapes and the quality of every template tetrahedron, and the cut by comparing the parts where $f > 0$ and $f < 0$, which fill the cube and share their cut triangles with opposite orientation. $\blacksquare$
