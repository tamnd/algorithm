---
title: DUAL-SHEETS finds the sheets of a hex mesh, each sheet crosses one layer of hexes through parallel edges, and the sheet boundaries are the loops
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

**Setting.** A hex mesh is a finite list of hexes. Each hex is a list of 8 vertex ids in the standard order, and its 12 edges fall into three classes of 4 parallel edges (the classes $u$, $v$ and $w$ of the reference cube). An edge is the set of its two end vertices, and a face is the set of its four vertices. The mesh is conforming: a face belongs to at most two hexes. A face that belongs to one hex is a boundary quad. Let $H$ be the number of hexes, $I$ the number of faces in two hexes, and $B$ the number of boundary quads.

On a face $(a, b, c, d)$, the edges $ab$ and $cd$ are opposite, and so are $bc$ and $da$. Let $\sim$ be the smallest equivalence relation on the edges of the mesh in which opposite edges of each face are equivalent. A sheet is a class of $\sim$. Let $\approx$ be the smallest equivalence relation on the edges of boundary quads in which opposite edges of each boundary quad are equivalent. A loop is a class of $\approx$.

**Claim.**

1. DUAL-SHEETS stops, and the classes of L5 are the sheets.
2. (Sheet decomposition.) Each class of parallel edges of a hex is inside one sheet. So a sheet meets each hex in $0$, $4$, $8$ or $12$ edges, and these edges are whole classes of parallel edges. L6 gives each hex the sheets of its three classes.
3. (The sheet is a layer.) Fix a sheet $s$. Make a 2D cell complex $D_s$: one vertex for each edge in $s$, one edge for each face $f$ and each pair of opposite edges of $f$ that are both in $s$, and one quadrilateral cell for each hex $h$ and each class of $h$ in $s$. The four sides of the cell of $(h, \text{class})$ are the four faces of $h$ that contain two edges of the class. Each edge of $D_s$ is a side of one or two cells: of two cells if its face is interior, and of one cell if its face is a boundary quad. So $D_s$ is a layer of hexes, one quadrilateral per hex and class, glued across the shared faces.
4. L7 gives the boundary quads, and $6H = 2I + B$. In particular $B$ is even.
5. The classes of L11 are the loops. Each loop is inside one sheet, and the boundary edges of each sheet are a union of loops. The boundary of $D_s$ (the edges of $D_s$ that are a side of one cell only) is the graph of the loops of $s$. If every boundary edge of the mesh is in exactly two boundary quads, each loop is a cycle.

**Proof of part 1.** L2 to L4 visit each hex once and do 9 unions per hex, so the loop stops. Inside one hex, take a class, for example the class $u$ with the edges $(0,1), (3,2), (4,5), (7,6)$. The edges $(0,1)$ and $(3,2)$ are opposite on the face $(0,3,2,1)$, the edges $(0,1)$ and $(4,5)$ on the face $(0,1,5,4)$, and the edges $(3,2)$ and $(7,6)$ on the face $(2,3,7,6)$. So the four edges of a class are $\sim$ equivalent, and L4 joins only $\sim$ equivalent edges. Conversely, two opposite edges of a face of a hex are parallel in that hex, so they are in the same class, and L4 joins them. The union-find classes are the classes of the smallest equivalence relation that contains the joined pairs. Both relations have the same generating pairs up to equivalence, so they are equal, and L5 gives the sheets.

**Proof of part 2.** By part 1 the four edges of a class of a hex are in one sheet. The 12 edges of a hex are the disjoint union of its three classes, so the edges of a hex in a sheet $s$ are a union of whole classes, which gives $0$, $4$, $8$ or $12$ edges. L6 reads the sheet of the first edge of each class, which is the sheet of the whole class.

**Proof of part 3.** Take a cell $(h, c)$ of $D_s$ with the class $c$ of $h$ in $s$. Each of the four edges of $c$ is a vertex of $D_s$. A face of $h$ contains two edges of $c$ as opposite edges exactly when it is one of the four faces parallel to the direction of $c$, for example for the class $u$ the faces $(0,3,2,1)$, $(4,5,6,7)$, $(0,1,5,4)$ and $(2,3,7,6)$. Each of these faces gives an edge of $D_s$ between two vertices of the cell. These four edges form a cycle through the four vertices in the order $(0,1), (4,5), (7,6), (3,2)$. So the cell is a quadrilateral. Now take an edge of $D_s$, given by a face $f$ and a pair of opposite edges of $f$ in $s$. A cell $(h, c)$ has this edge as a side exactly when $f$ is a face of $h$ and the pair is in $c$. In each hex that contains $f$, the pair is parallel, so it is in one class of that hex. So the edge is a side of one cell for each hex that contains $f$: one cell for a boundary quad and two cells for an interior face. The cells of $D_s$ are in one to one correspondence with the pairs (hex, class in $s$). So $D_s$ meets each hex in one quadrilateral per class in $s$, which is the layer of hexes that the sheet crosses. When the class is unique in each hex, the sheet crosses each of its hexes once, through four parallel edges.

**Proof of part 4.** L7 counts the occurrences of each face over all hexes and keeps the faces that occur once. Each hex has 6 faces, so the total number of occurrences is $6H$. A face in two hexes occurs twice, and a boundary quad once, and by conformity there is no other case. So $6H = 2I + B$, and $B = 6H - 2I$ is even.

**Proof of part 5.** L8 to L10 join the opposite edges of each boundary quad, so as in part 1 the classes of L11 are the loops. Two opposite edges of a boundary quad are opposite edges of a face of a hex, so they are $\sim$ equivalent, and each generating pair of $\approx$ is inside one sheet. So each loop is inside one sheet. The loops partition the boundary edges, so the boundary edges of a sheet are the union of the loops that contain them. By part 3, an edge of $D_s$ is a side of one cell exactly when its face is a boundary quad. These edges join opposite edges of boundary quads, which are the generating pairs of $\approx$. So the boundary graph of $D_s$ has the boundary edges of $s$ as vertices and the generating pairs of $\approx$ inside $s$ as edges, and its components are the loops of $s$. Finally, let each boundary edge $e$ be in exactly two boundary quads. In each of them $e$ has one opposite edge, so $e$ has degree 2 in this graph. A finite graph in which each vertex has degree 2 is a disjoint union of cycles, so each loop is a cycle.

**Termination and cost.** Each line is a finite loop over the hexes or the boundary quads. With union by size and path halving, the cost is $O(H \alpha(H))$, plus the sorting that makes the output canonical. $\blacksquare$
