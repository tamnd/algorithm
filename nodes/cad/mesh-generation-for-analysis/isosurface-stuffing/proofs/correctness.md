**Claim.** Work in doubled coordinates, so the lattice is $\Lambda = \{(i, j, k) \in \mathbb{Z}^3 : i \equiv j \equiv k \pmod 2\}$ and $h = 2$. Then:

1. the BCC tetrahedron $T_0 = (p_0, p_1, p_2, p_3) = ((0,0,0), (2,0,0), (1,1,1), (1,-1,1))$ has dihedral angles $90°$ at its two black edges and $60°$ at its four red edges, volume $2/3$ (that is $h^3/12$), circumradius $\sqrt{5}/2$ (that is $\sqrt{5/16}\,h$) and radius-edge ratio $\sqrt{5/12}$;
2. its circumsphere has no other point of $\Lambda$ inside or on it, and the BCC tetrahedra tile $\mathbb{R}^3$ face to face;
3. the stencils of L7 to L12 match on every common face of two BCC tetrahedra, every prism of L11 and L12 splits into three tetrahedra with the prescribed diagonals, and so the pieces form a face-to-face tetrahedralization of the region they cover, provided they keep positive volume after warping.

**What is not claimed.** That the warped pieces keep positive volume and have dihedral angles in $[10.7843°, 164.7373°]$ is Theorem 1 of Labelle and Shewchuk (2007), proved there by a computer search with interval arithmetic. It is cited, not proved here. The test file checks it on balls, with exact orientation tests.

**Step 1: the BCC tetrahedron.** The black edges $p_0 p_1$ and $p_2 p_3$ have squared length 4, and the four red edges, such as $p_2 - p_0 = (1,1,1)$, have squared length 3. The volume is $\frac16 \det[(2,0,0), (1,1,1), (1,-1,1)] = \frac16 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = \frac23$, and $12 \cdot \frac23 = 8$ is the volume of a cell of the cubic lattice $2\mathbb{Z}^3$. The point $o = (1, 0, \frac12)$ has $|o - p|^2 = 1 + \frac14 = \frac54$ for all four vertices, so it is the circumcenter, $R = \sqrt5/2$ and $R^2/\ell_{\min}^2 = \frac{5/4}{3} = \frac{5}{12}$.

For the dihedral angles, take the outward normals $n_0 = (1, 0, 1)$, $n_1 = (-1, 0, 1)$, $n_2 = (0, -1, -1)$ and $n_3 = (0, 1, -1)$ of the faces opposite $p_0, \dots, p_3$. Each is orthogonal to the edges of its face, and $n_k \cdot (p_k - q) < 0$ for a vertex $q$ of that face, so it points away from $p_k$. The dihedral angle at the edge $p_i p_j$ has $\cos\theta = -n_k \cdot n_l / (|n_k||n_l|)$, where $k, l$ are the other two indices, and $|n_k|^2 = 2$ for all $k$. At $p_0 p_1$: $-n_2 \cdot n_3 / 2 = -(-1 + 1)/2 = 0$. At $p_2 p_3$: $-n_0 \cdot n_1 / 2 = -(-1 + 1)/2 = 0$. At $p_0 p_2$: $-n_1 \cdot n_3 / 2 = -(-1)/2 = \frac12$. At $p_0 p_3$: $-n_1 \cdot n_2 / 2 = -(-1)/2 = \frac12$. At $p_1 p_2$: $-n_0 \cdot n_3 / 2 = -(-1)/2 = \frac12$. At $p_1 p_3$: $-n_0 \cdot n_2 / 2 = -(-1)/2 = \frac12$. So the black edges have $90°$ and the red edges $60°$.

**Step 2: emptiness and tiling.** Let $q \in \Lambda$ with $|q - o|^2 \le \frac54$, that is $(q_1 - 1)^2 + q_2^2 + (q_3 - \frac12)^2 \le \frac54$. Then $|q_3 - \frac12| \le \frac{\sqrt5}{2} < \frac32$, so $q_3 \in \{0, 1\}$ and $(q_3 - \frac12)^2 = \frac14$, which leaves $(q_1 - 1)^2 + q_2^2 \le 1$. If $q$ is even, $q_1 - 1$ is odd, so $(q_1 - 1)^2 = 1$ and $q_2 = 0$, $q_3 = 0$: these are $p_0$ and $p_1$. If $q$ is odd, $q_1 - 1$ is even, so $q_1 = 1$, and $q_2 = \pm 1$, $q_3 = 1$: these are $p_2$ and $p_3$. So no other lattice point lies inside or on the circumsphere. The nearest ones, such as $(0, 0, 2)$, are at squared distance $\frac{13}{4}$ from $o$.

Every BCC tetrahedron $(p, p + 2e_i, c, c')$ listed by `bcc_tetrahedra` is the image of $T_0$ under a symmetry of $\Lambda$: a translation by an even vector composed with a permutation of the axes and sign changes. So each has a strictly empty circumsphere among the points of $\Lambda$, and each is a cell of the Delaunay subdivision of $\Lambda$. Distinct Delaunay cells have disjoint interiors and meet face to face. For coverage, each even point starts 3 black edges, each with 4 tetrahedra, so there are 12 tetrahedra per even point. Their total volume, $12 \cdot \frac23 = 8$, is the volume of one cell of $2\mathbb{Z}^3$. A periodic family of interior-disjoint tetrahedra whose volume per period equals the volume of a period covers space. So the BCC tetrahedra tile $\mathbb{R}^3$ face to face. (The test file also locates random points and finds each in exactly one tetrahedron.)

**Step 3a: the quadrilateral rule depends only on the face.** A face of a BCC tetrahedron omits one vertex, so it contains the black edge that avoids that vertex and two red edges. Let $a, b$ be positive and $x$ negative on the face, with cut points $c$ on $bx$ and $d$ on $ax$; the diagonals are $ac$ and $bd$. If $ax$ is black the rule takes $bd$, the diagonal at the cut point on $ax$, whichever of $a, b$ is named first, and likewise if $bx$ is black. Otherwise $ab$ is black, along an axis $i$, and $x$ lies on the other lattice with $x_i = \frac{a_i + b_i}{2}$ and $x_k = a_k \pm 1$ for $k \ne i$. Since $c$ lies strictly inside the segment $bx$, $a_k > c_k$ holds exactly when $2 a_k > b_k + x_k$. For $k = i$ this reads $a_i > b_i$; for $k \ne i$ it reads $a_k > x_k$, because $b_k = a_k$. Exchanging $a$ and $b$ (and so $c$ and $d$) changes the test for $k = i$ into $b_i > a_i$ and leaves the others unchanged. Exactly one of $a_i > b_i$ and $b_i > a_i$ holds, so the two counts have opposite parity. On the cubic lattice, the rule for $(a, b)$ takes $ac$ if its count is odd and $bd$ if it is even, and the rule for $(b, a)$ takes $bd$ if its count is odd and $ac$ if it is even; with opposite parities they agree. On the shifted lattice both choices are reversed, and they still agree.

**Step 3b: the stencils agree on every face.** Let $F$ be a common face of two BCC tetrahedra, with the values after warping. A zero vertex is a vertex of the output, and an edge with a zero end has no cut point. If $F$ has a positive vertex, inspection of L8 to L12 shows that the output of a tetrahedron $\tau \supset F$ meets $F$ in: all of $F$ if $F$ has no negative vertex; the triangle of the positive vertex and the zero vertices or cut points on its other two edges if $F$ has one positive and some negative vertex; and the quadrilateral $a, b, c, d$ of Step 3a, split by the rule, if $F$ has two positive vertices and one negative. (In L10, for instance, the face $abd$ carries the split quadrilateral, the faces $azd$ and $bzd$ the triangles $a z d'$ with $d'$ the cut point, and the face $abz$ is whole.) These triangles depend only on $F$ and its values, so both tetrahedra produce the same ones. If $F$ has no positive vertex, the output of $\tau$ meets $F$ in a set of dimension at most one, except when all three vertices of $F$ are zero and the fourth vertex of $\tau$ is positive: then $F$ is a face of the output. So either both sides output $F$ as a face, or one side does and $F$ is a boundary face with its three vertices on the surface.

**Step 3c: the prisms split.** A prism with bottom $b_0 b_1 b_2$, top $t_0 t_1 t_2$ and a diagonal on each quadrilateral $b_i b_{i+1} t_{i+1} t_i$ splits into three tetrahedra with these diagonals if some vertex $v$ is the end of two of them: take the tetrahedron from $v$ to the opposite triangle and the pyramid from $v$ over the third quadrilateral, split by its diagonal. If no vertex is the end of two diagonals, each of the six vertices is the end of exactly one, the diagonals form a cycle around the prism, and no split exists, as in Schönhardt's twisted prism.

In L12, with $a, b, c$ positive and $d$ negative, $d$ lies on one black edge $dy$ of $\tau$ with $y \in \{a, b, c\}$. Both faces of $\tau$ that contain $dy$ take the diagonal at the cut point on $dy$, which is then the end of two diagonals.

In L11, with $a, b$ positive and $c, d$ negative, the faces $abc$ and $abd$ carry two of the diagonals. If $ab$ is red, the black edges of $\tau$ are $ac, bd$ or $ad, bc$; say $ac$ and $bd$. Then $abc$ takes $b$ to the cut point on $ac$, and $abd$ takes $a$ to the cut point on $bd$: no common end. If $ab$ is black along axis $i$, so is $cd$, along an axis $m \ne i$, with $c_m = a_m \pm 1$, $d_m = a_m \mp 1$ and $c_k = d_k$ for $k \ne m$. By Step 3a, the counts of $a$ for the faces $abc$ and $abd$ differ only at the coordinate $m$, where exactly one of $a_m > c_m$ and $a_m > d_m$ holds. So the counts have opposite parity, one face takes the diagonal from $a$ and the other the diagonal from $b$, and again there is no common end. In both cases the third quadrilateral lies on the surface inside $\tau$, so its diagonal appears in no other tetrahedron. Its corners are the four cut points. Of its two diagonals, one joins the two cut points that end the face diagonals, and the other joins the two cut points that end neither; the first makes a vertex the end of two diagonals, the second leaves a cycle. L11 tries both and takes the first, so the prism splits.

**Step 3d: conclusion.** Before warping, each piece lies in its BCC tetrahedron, and the pieces of one tetrahedron tile the part of it that the stencil keeps. By Step 2 the BCC tetrahedra tile space face to face, and by Steps 3b and 3c the pieces of neighbors match on every common face. Warping moves only lattice points whose value becomes zero, and moves each to a single position shared by all pieces that contain it, so the combinatorial structure does not change, and the pieces still meet face to face as long as each keeps positive volume. Theorem 1 of Labelle and Shewchuk gives the positive volumes and the angle bounds. Every boundary face of the output has only zero vertices and cut points as vertices, and all of these lie on the surface. $\blacksquare$
