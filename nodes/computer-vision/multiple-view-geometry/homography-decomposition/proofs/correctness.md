---
title: The decomposition returns the true motion and plane, and every candidate it returns reproduces the homography
proves: [correctness]
status: full
---

We use the notation of the page. The true motion is a rotation $R_0$, a vector $\mathbf{t}_0 = \mathbf{t}/d$ and a unit normal $\mathbf{n}_0$, and the input is $G = \mu K_2(R_0 + \mathbf{t}_0\mathbf{n}_0^{\mathsf T})K_1^{-1}$ with an unknown real $\mu \ne 0$. The given pairs are images of points $\mathbf{X}_1$ of the plane $\mathbf{n}_0^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{X}_1 = d > 0$ with positive depths $z_1$ in camera 1 and $z_2$ in camera 2, so $\mathbf{X}_1 = z_1\mathbf{y}_1$ and $\mathbf{X}_2 = R_0\mathbf{X}_1 + \mathbf{t} = z_2\mathbf{y}_2$, where $\mathbf{y}_k = K_k^{-1}\mathbf{x}_k$ has last entry 1. Both cameras are on the same side of the plane. Write $H_0 = R_0 + \mathbf{t}_0\mathbf{n}_0^{\mathsf T}$.

**Claim.** If $\mathbf{t}_0 \ne \mathbf{0}$, DECOMPOSE-HOMOGRAPHY returns a list of triples $(R, \mathbf{t}', \mathbf{n})$ such that (a) each has $R$ a rotation, $\|\mathbf{n}\| = 1$ and $R + \mathbf{t}'\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T} = H_0$; (b) each has $\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{y}_1 > 0$ for the given points; (c) $(R_0, \mathbf{t}_0, \mathbf{n}_0)$ is in the list; (d) the list has at most two distinct triples. If $\mathbf{t}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, it returns $R_0$.

**Lemma 1 (the middle singular value).** For every rotation $R$, vector $\mathbf{t}$ and unit vector $\mathbf{n}$, the matrix $M = R + \mathbf{t}\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T}$ has singular values $s_1 \ge s_2 = 1 \ge s_3$.

*Proof.* Let $\mathbf{u} = R^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{t}$. Then $M^{\mathsf T}M = I + \mathbf{n}\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf T} + \mathbf{u}\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T} + \|\mathbf{u}\|^2\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T} = I + \mathbf{a}\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T} + \mathbf{n}\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf T}$ with $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{u} + \frac12\|\mathbf{u}\|^2\mathbf{n}$. The symmetric matrix $S = \mathbf{a}\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T} + \mathbf{n}\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf T}$ is 0 on the vectors orthogonal to both $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{n}$. If $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{n}$ are independent, its other two eigenvalues are $\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{n} \pm \|\mathbf{a}\|$, as a direct check on the vectors $\mathbf{n} \pm \mathbf{a}/\|\mathbf{a}\|$ shows; one is $\ge 0$ and one is $\le 0$. If $\mathbf{a} = \alpha\mathbf{n}$, then $S = 2\alpha\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T}$ has eigenvalues $2\alpha, 0, 0$. In both cases the eigenvalues of $M^{\mathsf T}M = I + S$, sorted, are $s_1^2 \ge 1 \ge s_3^2$ with the middle one equal to 1. $\square$

**Lemma 2 (scale and sign).** After line L3, $H = H_0$.

*Proof.* After line L1, $H = \mu H_0$. By Lemma 1 the middle singular value of $H$ is $|\mu|$, so after line L2, $H = \pm H_0$. For a given pair, $H_0\mathbf{y}_1 = (R_0\mathbf{X}_1 + \mathbf{t}\,\mathbf{n}_0^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{X}_1/d)/z_1 = \mathbf{X}_2/z_1 = (z_2/z_1)\mathbf{y}_2$, using $\mathbf{n}_0^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{X}_1 = d$. So $\mathbf{y}_2^{\mathsf T}H_0\mathbf{y}_1 = (z_2/z_1)\|\mathbf{y}_2\|^2 > 0$ for every pair, every vote of line L3 has the sign of the factor $\pm 1$, and the line makes it $+1$. $\square$

From now on $H = H_0$, and $l_1 = s_1^2$, $l_3 = s_3^2$, with right singular vectors $v_1, v_2, v_3$, so $H^{\mathsf T}H v_k = s_k^2 v_k$ and $s_2 = 1$.

**Lemma 3 (pure rotation).** $l_1 = l_3$ if and only if $H^{\mathsf T}H = I$. Under the same side condition, this holds if and only if $\mathbf{t}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, and then $H = R_0$.

*Proof.* By Lemma 1, $l_1 = l_3$ means that all eigenvalues of $H^{\mathsf T}H$ are 1. In the notation of Lemma 1 this is $S = 0$. Applying $S$ to $\mathbf{n}$ gives $\mathbf{a} + (\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{n})\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{0}$, so $\mathbf{a} = -(\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{n})\mathbf{n}$, and taking the inner product with $\mathbf{n}$ gives $\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{n} = 0$ and then $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}$. So $\mathbf{u} = -\frac12\|\mathbf{u}\|^2\mathbf{n}$: either $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$, or $\|\mathbf{u}\| = 2$ and $\mathbf{u} = -2\mathbf{n}$. In the second case $H = R_0(I - 2\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T})$ has determinant $-1$. But $\det H = 1 + \mathbf{n}_0^{\mathsf T}R_0^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{t}_0$, and the center of camera 2, $\mathbf{C}_2 = -R_0^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{t}$, is on the side of camera 1 exactly when $\mathbf{n}_0^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{C}_2 < d$, that is, when $\det H > 0$. So under the same side condition only $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$ remains, which is $\mathbf{t}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, and $H = R_0$ is orthogonal; its orthogonal factor, returned by line L5, is $R_0$ itself. $\square$

Assume now $\mathbf{t}_0 \ne \mathbf{0}$, so $l_1 > l_3$ and line L6 is well defined: $1 - l_3 \ge 0$ and $l_1 - 1 \ge 0$ by Lemma 1.

**Lemma 4 (the planes on which $H$ keeps lengths).** Let $Q(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{w}^{\mathsf T}(H^{\mathsf T}H - I)\mathbf{w}$. A 2-dimensional subspace $P$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$ has $Q = 0$ on all of $P$ if and only if $P$ is spanned by $v_2$ and one of the two vectors $u$ of line L6.

*Proof.* In the coordinates $\mathbf{w} = a v_1 + b v_2 + c v_3$, $Q(\mathbf{w}) = (l_1 - 1)a^2 - (1 - l_3)c^2$, with both coefficients $\ge 0$ and not both 0. If both are positive, $Q = 0$ exactly on the two planes $\sqrt{l_1 - 1}\,a = \pm\sqrt{1 - l_3}\,c$, which contain $v_2$ and the vectors $\sqrt{1 - l_3}\,v_1 \pm \sqrt{l_1 - 1}\,v_3$, the vectors of line L6 up to a positive factor. If $l_1 = 1$, $Q = -(1 - l_3)c^2$ vanishes only on the plane $c = 0$, spanned by $v_2$ and $v_1$; line L6 then gives $u = v_1$ twice. If $l_3 = 1$, the zero set is the plane $a = 0$, spanned by $v_2$ and $v_3$, and line L6 gives $u = \pm v_3$. So the zero set of $Q$ is a union of at most two planes, each of the stated form. A 2-dimensional subspace inside a union of two planes through the origin is one of them, since a vector space is never the union of two proper subspaces. Conversely, $Q$ vanishes on each stated plane. $\square$

**Lemma 5 (the candidates).** Let $u$ be one of the vectors of line L6, and let $R$, $\mathbf{n}$ and $\mathbf{t}$ be as in lines L7 and L8. Then $\|u\| = 1$, $u \perp v_2$, $R$ is a rotation, $\|\mathbf{n}\| = 1$, and $R + \mathbf{t}\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T} = H$. The same holds for $(R, -\mathbf{t}, -\mathbf{n})$.

*Proof.* $\|u\|^2 = ((1 - l_3) + (l_1 - 1))/(l_1 - l_3) = 1$, and $u$ is a combination of $v_1$ and $v_3$, so $u \perp v_2$. So $U = [v_2, u, v_2 \times u]$ is a rotation. Next, $\|Hu\|^2 = u^{\mathsf T}H^{\mathsf T}Hu = (l_1(1 - l_3) + l_3(l_1 - 1))/(l_1 - l_3) = 1$, $\|Hv_2\|^2 = 1$, and $(Hv_2)^{\mathsf T}(Hu) = v_2^{\mathsf T}H^{\mathsf T}Hu = 0$, because $H^{\mathsf T}Hu$ is a combination of $v_1$ and $v_3$. So $W = [Hv_2, Hu, Hv_2 \times Hu]$ is also a rotation, and $R = WU^{\mathsf T}$ is a rotation with $Rv_2 = Hv_2$ and $Ru = Hu$. The vector $\mathbf{n} = v_2 \times u$ is a unit vector orthogonal to $v_2$ and $u$. Let $\mathbf{t} = (H - R)\mathbf{n}$. Then $(R + \mathbf{t}\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T})$ agrees with $H$ on $v_2$ and on $u$, because $\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T}v_2 = \mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T}u = 0$, and on $\mathbf{n}$, because $R\mathbf{n} + \mathbf{t} = H\mathbf{n}$. These three vectors are a basis, so $R + \mathbf{t}\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T} = H$. Finally $R + (-\mathbf{t})(-\mathbf{n})^{\mathsf T} = R + \mathbf{t}\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T}$. $\square$

**Lemma 6 (the true triple is a candidate).** $(R_0, \mathbf{t}_0, \mathbf{n}_0)$ is one of the four triples of line L9.

*Proof.* For $\mathbf{w} \perp \mathbf{n}_0$, $H\mathbf{w} = R_0\mathbf{w}$, so $\|H\mathbf{w}\| = \|\mathbf{w}\|$ and $Q = 0$ on the plane $P_0 = \mathbf{n}_0^{\perp}$. By Lemma 4, $P_0$ is spanned by $v_2$ and one vector $u$ of line L6. For this $u$, $R_0$ and the matrix $R$ of line L8 agree on the orthonormal basis $v_2, u$ of $P_0$ (both equal $H$ there), and they are rotations, so they also agree on $v_2 \times u$: $R = R_0$. The normal of $P_0$ is $\mathbf{n}_0 = \pm(v_2 \times u) = \pm\mathbf{n}$. If $\mathbf{n}_0 = \mathbf{n}$, then $\mathbf{t}_0 = (H - R_0)\mathbf{n}_0 = \mathbf{t}$; if $\mathbf{n}_0 = -\mathbf{n}$, then $\mathbf{t}_0 = -\mathbf{t}$. Either way the true triple is one of the two triples that line L9 adds for this $u$. $\square$

**Proof of the claim.** The case $\mathbf{t}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ is Lemma 3. Otherwise, by Lemma 2 the algorithm works with $H = H_0$. Part (a) is Lemma 5 for every candidate. Line L10 keeps only triples with a majority of positive values $\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{y}_1$. For the true triple, $\mathbf{n}_0^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{y}_1 = \mathbf{n}_0^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{X}_1/z_1 = d/z_1 > 0$ for every pair, so it is kept, which with Lemma 6 gives (c). Every other kept triple $(R, \mathbf{t}, \mathbf{n})$ satisfies $R + \mathbf{t}\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T} = H_0$, so the plane it describes is $\mathbf{n}^{\perp}$ for one of the two planes of Lemma 4; the votes for $\mathbf{n}$ and $-\mathbf{n}$ are opposite, so of each pair of line L9 at most one is kept, which gives (b) and (d): two vectors $u$, one triple each. $\blacksquare$

**Remark (why two remain).** The second triple is a genuine other motion and plane that produce the same $H$, so no test on the two images can remove it in general. In the example of the page it passes every test above. A further test, such as positive depth in camera 2 for points computed with the second plane, removes it only in some configurations.
