---
title: An essential matrix has exactly four factorizations into a rotation and a unit translation, and exactly one of them puts a given point in front of both cameras
proves: [correctness]
status: full
---

We use the notation of the page. Poses follow $\mathbf{X}_2 = R\mathbf{X}_1 + \mathbf{t}$, a point in normalized coordinates is $\mathbf{y} = (u, v, 1)^{\mathsf T}$, and the depth of a point in a camera is its $Z$ coordinate in that camera. $SO(3)$ is the set of rotations, $[\mathbf{a}]_\times$ is the matrix with $[\mathbf{a}]_\times\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}$, and
$$W = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Z = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = [\mathbf{e}_3]_\times .$$

**Claim.** Let $E = s[\mathbf{t}_0]_\times R_0$ with $s \ne 0$, $\|\mathbf{t}_0\| = 1$ and $R_0 \in SO(3)$. (a) The four pairs of line L4 are exactly the pairs $(R, \mathbf{t}) \in SO(3) \times \mathbb{R}^3$ with $\|\mathbf{t}\| = 1$ and $[\mathbf{t}]_\times R = \lambda E$ for some real $\lambda \ne 0$, and $(R_0, \mathbf{t}_0)$ is one of them. (b) Let a point have positive depths $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ in the true pose $(R_0, \mathbf{t}_0)$, and let it not lie on the line through the two camera centers. Then, for the four candidates, the depths of line L6 are $(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)$, $(-\lambda_1, -\lambda_2)$, $(-\lambda_1/D, \lambda_2/D)$ and $(\lambda_1/D, -\lambda_2/D)$, with $D = 1 + 2\lambda_1\,\mathbf{t}_0^{\mathsf T}R_0\mathbf{y}_1$, where the last two need $D \ne 0$. So only the true pose has both depths positive. (c) On exact data in which at least one point satisfies (b), RECOVER-POSE returns $(R_0, \mathbf{t}_0)$.

**Lemma 1 (rotations and the cross product).** For $U \in SO(3)$ and $\mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, $[U\mathbf{a}]_\times = U[\mathbf{a}]_\times U^{\mathsf T}$.

*Proof.* For every $\mathbf{b}$, $U[\mathbf{a}]_\times U^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{b} = U(\mathbf{a} \times U^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{b}) = U\mathbf{a} \times UU^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{b} = U\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}$, because a rotation keeps cross products: $U\mathbf{p} \times U\mathbf{q} = U(\mathbf{p} \times \mathbf{q})$ when $\det U = 1$. $\square$

**Lemma 2 (the SVD with rotations).** The singular values of $E$ are $(|s|, |s|, 0)$. After line L2, $E = U\operatorname{diag}(|s|, |s|, 0)V^{\mathsf T}$ with $U, V \in SO(3)$, and the third column $\mathbf{u}_3$ of $U$ spans the null space of $E^{\mathsf T}$.

*Proof.* $EE^{\mathsf T} = s^2[\mathbf{t}_0]_\times[\mathbf{t}_0]_\times^{\mathsf T} = s^2(I - \mathbf{t}_0\mathbf{t}_0^{\mathsf T})$, whose eigenvalues are $s^2, s^2, 0$. Line L1 gives an SVD with orthogonal $U$ and $V$. Line L2 changes the sign of the third column of $U$ or of $V$ when the determinant is $-1$. The third singular value is 0, so the third columns do not enter the product, and the product stays $E$. Now $\det U = \det V = 1$. Finally $E^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{u}_3 = V\operatorname{diag}(|s|, |s|, 0)U^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{u}_3 = V\operatorname{diag}(|s|, |s|, 0)\mathbf{e}_3 = \mathbf{0}$, and the null space has dimension 1. $\square$

**Proof of part (a).** Let $[\mathbf{t}]_\times R = \lambda E$ with $\|\mathbf{t}\| = 1$ and $\lambda \ne 0$. Then $E^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{t} = \lambda^{-1}R^{\mathsf T}[\mathbf{t}]_\times^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{t} = -\lambda^{-1}R^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf{t} \times \mathbf{t}) = \mathbf{0}$, so by Lemma 2, $\mathbf{t} = \pm\mathbf{u}_3$. Write $\mathbf{t} = \epsilon\mathbf{u}_3 = \epsilon U\mathbf{e}_3$ with $\epsilon = \pm 1$. By Lemma 1, $[\mathbf{t}]_\times = \epsilon UZU^{\mathsf T}$. Put $Q = U^{\mathsf T}RV \in SO(3)$. The equation becomes $\epsilon UZQV^{\mathsf T} = \lambda|s|\,U\operatorname{diag}(1, 1, 0)V^{\mathsf T}$, that is,
$$ZQ = \mu\operatorname{diag}(1, 1, 0), \qquad \mu = \epsilon\lambda|s| \ne 0 .$$
The rows of $ZQ$ are $(-\mathbf{q}_2^{\mathsf T}, \mathbf{q}_1^{\mathsf T}, \mathbf{0}^{\mathsf T})$, where $\mathbf{q}_j^{\mathsf T}$ is row $j$ of $Q$. So $\mathbf{q}_1 = \mu\mathbf{e}_2$ and $\mathbf{q}_2 = -\mu\mathbf{e}_1$. Rows of an orthogonal matrix are unit vectors, so $\mu = \pm 1$, and $\mathbf{q}_3$ is the unit vector that makes $\det Q = 1$, which is $\mathbf{e}_3$ in both cases. For $\mu = 1$, $Q = W^{\mathsf T}$; for $\mu = -1$, $Q = W$. So $R = UWV^{\mathsf T}$ or $R = UW^{\mathsf T}V^{\mathsf T}$, and $(R, \mathbf{t})$ is one of the four pairs of lines L3 and L4.

Conversely, each of the four pairs has $R \in SO(3)$ (a product of rotations) and $\|\mathbf{t}\| = 1$, and the computation above, read backwards, gives $[\mathbf{t}]_\times R = \epsilon UZ Q V^{\mathsf T} = \pm U\operatorname{diag}(1, 1, 0)V^{\mathsf T} = \pm E/|s|$. The two rotations differ, because $W \ne W^{\mathsf T}$, so the four pairs are distinct. The true pose satisfies $[\mathbf{t}_0]_\times R_0 = E/s$, so it is one of them. $\square$

**Lemma 3 (the twisted pair).** $UW^{\mathsf T}V^{\mathsf T} = R_\pi\,UWV^{\mathsf T}$, where $R_\pi = 2\mathbf{u}_3\mathbf{u}_3^{\mathsf T} - I$ is the rotation by $\pi$ about $\mathbf{u}_3$.

*Proof.* $W^{\mathsf T}W^{\mathsf T} = \operatorname{diag}(-1, -1, 1)$, so $UW^{\mathsf T}V^{\mathsf T} = U\operatorname{diag}(-1, -1, 1)U^{\mathsf T}\,UWV^{\mathsf T}$, and $U\operatorname{diag}(-1, -1, 1)U^{\mathsf T} = 2\mathbf{u}_3\mathbf{u}_3^{\mathsf T} - I$. This matrix keeps $\mathbf{u}_3$ and reverses every vector orthogonal to it. $\square$

Since $\mathbf{t}_0 = \pm\mathbf{u}_3$, also $R_\pi = 2\mathbf{t}_0\mathbf{t}_0^{\mathsf T} - I$. By part (a) and Lemma 3, the four candidates are $(R_0, \mathbf{t}_0)$, $(R_0, -\mathbf{t}_0)$, $(R_\pi R_0, \mathbf{t}_0)$ and $(R_\pi R_0, -\mathbf{t}_0)$.

**Lemma 4 (the depths of line L6).** For a pose $(R, \mathbf{t})$ and a pair $(\mathbf{y}_1, \mathbf{y}_2)$, let $\mathbf{a} = R\mathbf{y}_1$ and assume that $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{y}_2$ are linearly independent. Then the function depths returns the unique minimizer $(\mu_1, \mu_2)$ of $\|\mu_1\mathbf{a} - \mu_2\mathbf{y}_2 + \mathbf{t}\|$. If $\mu_2\mathbf{y}_2 = \mu_1\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{t}$ has a solution, this minimizer is that solution, and $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ are the depths of the point $\mu_1\mathbf{y}_1$ in the two cameras.

*Proof.* The function is the norm of $N\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{t}$ with $N = [\mathbf{a}, -\mathbf{y}_2]$, a $3 \times 2$ matrix of rank 2. The normal equations $N^{\mathsf T}N\boldsymbol{\mu} = -N^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{t}$ have a positive definite matrix, with determinant $\|\mathbf{a}\|^2\|\mathbf{y}_2\|^2 - (\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{y}_2)^2 > 0$ by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and the code solves them by Cramer's rule. A solution of the equation makes the norm 0, so it is the minimizer. The point $\mathbf{X}_1 = \mu_1\mathbf{y}_1$ has third entry $\mu_1$, and $\mathbf{X}_2 = R\mathbf{X}_1 + \mathbf{t} = \mu_2\mathbf{y}_2$ has third entry $\mu_2$. $\square$

**Proof of part (b).** The true pose gives $\lambda_2\mathbf{y}_2 = \lambda_1\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{t}_0$ with $\mathbf{a} = R_0\mathbf{y}_1$. The point is not on the line through the centers, so $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{t}_0$ are independent, and then $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{y}_2$ are independent too. The rotated ray of a candidate is $\mathbf{a}$ or $R_\pi\mathbf{a} = 2(\mathbf{t}_0^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{a})\mathbf{t}_0 - \mathbf{a}$. Since $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{t}_0$ are independent, $R_\pi\mathbf{a}$ is a multiple $c(\lambda_1\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{t}_0)$ of $\mathbf{y}_2$ exactly when $-1 = c\lambda_1$ and $2\mathbf{t}_0^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{a} = c$, that is, when $D = 0$. So Lemma 4 applies to all four candidates when $D \ne 0$.

For $(R_0, \mathbf{t}_0)$ the solution is $(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)$. For $(R_0, -\mathbf{t}_0)$ the equation $\mu_2\mathbf{y}_2 = \mu_1\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{t}_0$ is solved by $(-\lambda_1, -\lambda_2)$.

For $(R_\pi R_0, \mathbf{t}_0)$ we need $\mu_2\mathbf{y}_2 = \mu_1R_\pi\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{t}_0 = -\mu_1\mathbf{a} + (1 + 2\mu_1\mathbf{t}_0^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{a})\,\mathbf{t}_0$. Since $\lambda_2\mathbf{y}_2 = \lambda_1\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{t}_0$ and $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{t}_0$ are independent, the right side is a multiple $k\lambda_2\mathbf{y}_2$ exactly when $-\mu_1 = k\lambda_1$ and $1 + 2\mu_1\mathbf{t}_0^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{a} = k$. Then $k(1 + 2\lambda_1\mathbf{t}_0^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{a}) = 1$, so $k = 1/D$, $\mu_1 = -\lambda_1/D$ and $\mu_2 = k\lambda_2 = \lambda_2/D$. For $(R_\pi R_0, -\mathbf{t}_0)$, the solution is the negative of this one, $(\lambda_1/D, -\lambda_2/D)$. In both cases $\mu_1\mu_2 = -\lambda_1\lambda_2/D^2 < 0$. If $D = 0$, the rays of the two twisted candidates are parallel, as shown above. The function depths then finds a zero determinant and returns $(0, 0)$, so neither twisted candidate counts the point. $\square$

**Proof of part (c).** By part (b), the true pose counts every point that satisfies (b), and each other candidate counts none of them. A point on the line through the centers gives parallel rays for all candidates, and the code gives it depths $(0, 0)$, so no candidate counts it. So on exact data the true pose has the strictly largest count, and line L8 returns it. $\blacksquare$

**Remark (noise).** With noisy points the depths of Lemma 4 are least squares values, the depths of the [midpoint method](/midpoint-triangulation/). A point far away or near the baseline can then get the wrong sign in the true pose, and a few points can get two positive depths in another pose. The majority rule of line L8 is a rule for this case, not a theorem: it picks the right pose when most points are well inside the region where the sign of the depths is stable.
