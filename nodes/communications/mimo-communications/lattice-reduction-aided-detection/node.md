Lattice reduction aided detection is an approximate method for the [MIMO detection](/mimo-detection/) problem. For QAM and PAM, the transmitted vector is a shifted and scaled vector of integers, so the noiseless received vectors are points of a lattice generated by the columns of the channel matrix. ZF and SIC detection work badly when these columns are long and nearly parallel, because the noise is then amplified in some directions. The detector first changes the basis of the same lattice to short, nearly orthogonal vectors with the [LLL algorithm](/lll-algorithm/). Then it runs ZF ([Babai rounding](/babai-rounding/)) or SIC ([Babai nearest plane](/babai-nearest-plane/)) in the new basis, where the noise is much less amplified. A unimodular integer matrix maps the decision back to the original coordinates, and a last step clips it to the constellation. The cost is close to that of ZF or SIC, and the error rate is much closer to ML.

## In plain words

The receiver sees a point on a regular grid of possible signals, moved a little by noise, and it must find the nearest grid point. A simple receiver describes the grid with the directions given by the antennas. When two of these directions are almost the same, a small move by the noise looks like a large change of the signal, and the simple receiver makes many errors. The same grid can be described with other, better directions that are short and close to perpendicular. The receiver first finds such directions, then does the simple detection in them, and at the end translates the answer back. The translation is exact because the new directions describe the same grid.

## The problem

The model is $y = H x + w$ with $H \in \mathbb{C}^{m_c \times n_c}$ known, $x$ a vector of square QAM or PAM points, and $w \sim \mathcal{CN}(0, N_0 I)$. In the real-valued form, $y_r = (\mathrm{Re}\, y, \mathrm{Im}\, y)$, and for QAM

$$H_r = \begin{pmatrix} \mathrm{Re}\, H & -\mathrm{Im}\, H \\ \mathrm{Im}\, H & \mathrm{Re}\, H \end{pmatrix}, \qquad x_r = (\mathrm{Re}\, x, \mathrm{Im}\, x),$$

so $\|y - H x\|^2 = \|y_r - H_r x_r\|^2$ (a closed form). For PAM, $H_r$ is $\mathrm{Re}\, H$ above $\mathrm{Im}\, H$ and $x_r = \mathrm{Re}\, x$. Let $H_r$ be $m \times n$. Each entry of $x_r$ takes $q$ values $(2 z - (q - 1)) / s$ with $z \in \{0, \ldots, q - 1\}$, where $q = \sqrt M$ and $s = \sqrt{2 (M - 1) / 3}$ for $M$-QAM, and $q = M$ and $s = \sqrt{(M^2 - 1) / 3}$ for $M$-PAM. With $y' = (s\, y_r + (q - 1) H_r \mathbf{1}) / 2$,

$$y' = H_r z + w', \qquad z \in \{0, \ldots, q - 1\}^n, \qquad w' = \tfrac{s}{2} w_r.$$

So detection is a [closest vector problem](/closest-vector-problem/) in the lattice $\{H_r z : z \in \mathbb{Z}^n\}$, with a box constraint on $z$. The detector returns a vector of constellation indices.

## The algorithm

```pseudo id=main
LR-DETECT(y, H, S, method)
L1  (y_r, H_r) ← REAL-VALUED MODEL(y, H)
L2  y' ← (s y_r + (q - 1) H_r 1) / 2              ▷ y' = H_r z + w' with integer z
L3  (B, T) ← LLL(columns of H_r, δ = 3/4)          ▷ B = H_r T, T integer with |det T| = 1
L4  if method = ZF: u ← round(B⁺ y')
L5  else (SIC): for i ← n down to 1: u_i ← round(⟨r, b*_i⟩ / ‖b*_i‖²), r ← r - u_i b_i, starting from r = y'
L6  z ← T u
L7  clip each z_i to {0, ..., q - 1}
L8  return the constellation points (2 z - (q - 1)) / s, as indices
```

The LLL algorithm of line L3 keeps the basis size-reduced ($|\mu_{kj}| \le 1/2$ for the Gram-Schmidt coefficients) and makes a swap of $b_{k-1}$ and $b_k$ whenever the [Lovász condition](/lovasz-condition/) $\|b^*_k\|^2 \ge (\delta - \mu_{k,k-1}^2) \|b^*_{k-1}\|^2$ fails. Every operation on the basis is applied to the columns of $T$ as well, so $B = H_r T$ holds at all times. Line L5 is SIC in the reduced basis: it detects the coordinate along the last Gram-Schmidt vector first and cancels it. With $T = I$ (no reduction), lines L4 and L5 are plain ZF and plain ZF-SIC in a fixed order.

The MMSE form replaces $H_r$ by $\begin{pmatrix} H_r \\ \sigma I \end{pmatrix}$ and $y'$ by $\begin{pmatrix} y' \\ \sigma \frac{q - 1}{2} \mathbf{1} \end{pmatrix}$, with $\sigma^2$ the ratio of the noise variance to the symbol variance in one real dimension ($\sigma^2 = N_0$ for unit-energy QAM). The metric is then $\|y' - H_r z\|^2 + \sigma^2 \|z - \frac{q - 1}{2} \mathbf{1}\|^2$ (a closed form), as for the [MMSE detector](/mmse-mimo-detector/).

## Example

Take BPSK ($q = 2$, $s = 1$) over $H = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $y = (0.5, 0.6)$, the example of the [ZF page](/zero-forcing-mimo-detector/), sent with $x = (+1, -1)$, so $z = (1, 0)$. Then $y' = (y + H \mathbf 1) / 2 = (1.75, 1.3)$, and $w' = y' - H z = (-0.25, 0.3)$.

Plain ZF computes $H^{-1} y' = (0.45, 0.85)$, which rounds to $z = (0, 1)$, so $x = (-1, +1)$: both symbols are wrong. The reason is the noise after the inverse, $H^{-1} w' = (-0.55, 0.85)$, which is larger than $1/2$ in both entries.

LLL on the columns $(2, 1)$ and $(1, 1)$: the size reduction subtracts the first column from the second and gives $(-1, 0)$. Now $\|b^*_2\|^2 = 0.2$ is far below $(0.75 - 0.16) \cdot 5$, so the two vectors are swapped. A second size reduction gives $(0, 1)$, and the basis $B = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is reduced, with

$$T = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad H T = B, \qquad \det T = -1.$$

In the reduced basis, $B^{-1} y' = (-1.75, 1.3)$ rounds to $u = (-2, 1)$, and $z = T u = (1, 0)$, so $x = (+1, -1)$, the transmitted vector and the ML decision. The noise in the reduced basis is $B^{-1} w' = (0.25, 0.3)$, inside $(-1/2, 1/2)$ in both entries. Here SIC gives the same decisions as ZF, in both bases. The tests check every number of this example.

## Correctness

The proof is in [proofs/correctness.md](proofs/correctness.md). It shows that the shift of line L2 gives $y' = H_r z + w'$ with $z$ an integer vector (a closed form), that LLL terminates and returns $B = H_r T$ with $T$ unimodular, size-reduced and satisfying the Lovász condition (a theorem), and that the detector is exact without noise (a theorem). It shows that LR-SIC returns the transmitted vector whenever $\|w'\| < \frac12 \min_i \|b^*_i\|$, and that after LLL with $\delta = 3/4$ this holds whenever $\|w'\| < 2^{-(n-1)/2} \lambda_1 / 2$, with $\lambda_1$ the length of the shortest nonzero lattice vector (a theorem). The unbounded lattice ML decision is guaranteed only for $\|w'\| < \lambda_1 / 2$, so LR-SIC loses at most a factor $2^{(n-1)/2}$ in this noise radius, a factor that does not depend on the channel. Without reduction, $\min_i \|b^*_i\|$ can be arbitrarily small compared with $\lambda_1$, so plain ZF-SIC has no such bound.

Taherzadeh, Mobasher and Khandani (2007) proved that LLL-aided ZF and SIC reach the receive diversity of ML for lattice decoding, that is, when the box constraint of line L7 is ignored (a theorem of the reference, not proved here). With $m_c$ receive antennas and $n_c$ streams, plain ZF over i.i.d. Rayleigh fading has diversity $m_c - n_c + 1$, by the [ZF page](/zero-forcing-mimo-detector/), against $m_c$ for ML.

## Complexity

The real-valued model and the shift cost $O(n m)$. Each LLL iteration costs $O(n m)$ for the size reduction, and the implementation recomputes the Gram-Schmidt vectors after a swap in $O(n^2 m)$, so LLL with $S$ swaps costs $O((S + 1) n^2 m)$. ZF solves an $n \times n$ system in $O(n^2 m + n^3)$, SIC costs $O(n^2 m)$, and the mapping to points costs $O(n M)$. The total is $O((S + 1) n^2 m + n^3 + n M)$ in the [real RAM](/real-ram/) (a closed form). For real bases, $S$ has no bound in terms of $n$ alone, because it depends on how badly the channel is conditioned. The proof shows that $S$ is finite. For 2x2 4-QAM over i.i.d. Rayleigh fading (4 real dimensions), the test with seed 1805 measured 2.34 swaps per channel on average over 1000 channels, and at most 10 (a measurement). The reduction depends only on $H$, so on a channel that stays the same for many vectors its cost is spread over all of them.

## Properties

- **Exact without noise.** $z$ is recovered exactly from $y' = H_r z$ (theorem).
- **Same lattice.** $B$ and $H_r$ generate the same lattice, because $T$ and $T^{-1}$ are integer matrices (theorem).
- **Noise radius.** LR-SIC is correct whenever $\|w'\| < 2^{-(n-1)/2} \lambda_1 / 2$ (theorem).
- **Measured error rates.** For 2x2 4-QAM over i.i.d. Rayleigh fading at $E_s / N_0 = 20$ dB, the test with seed 1805 runs 1000 vectors. Plain ZF made 11 vector errors and LR-SIC made 1 (a measurement).
- **Not ML.** The box constraint is applied only at the end, by clipping, so a lattice point outside the box is moved to the nearest box point, which need not be the ML vector.
- **Only for lattice constellations.** The method needs a constellation that is a shifted and scaled part of $\mathbb{Z}^n$, such as square QAM and PAM. PSK has no such form.

## Variants and improvements

- **MMSE-based reduction.** Reduce the extended matrix of the MMSE form instead of $H_r$. Wübben, Böhnke, Kühn and Kammeyer described this form in 2004. The implementation uses it when $N_0 > 0$ is given.
- **Complex LLL.** Run the reduction on the complex matrix $H$ with Gaussian integers instead of the real matrix $H_r$, which halves the dimension.
- **Other reductions.** [Seysen reduction](/seysen-reduction/) reduces the primal and the dual basis together. Stronger reductions such as [HKZ reduction](/hkz-reduction/) give better bases at a higher cost. [Deep LLL](/deep-lll/) is between the two.
- **Sorted QR.** Compute a sorted QR decomposition first, as in [V-BLAST](/v-blast/), so that LLL starts from a basis that is already partly ordered and needs fewer swaps.
- **Precoding.** The same reduction, applied at the transmitter, gives lattice-reduction-aided precoding, a relative of [Tomlinson-Harashima precoding](/tomlinson-harashima-precoding/).
- **List and soft output.** Search a small neighbourhood of the LR decision for a list of candidates, from which LLRs can be computed as in the [list sphere decoder](/list-sphere-decoder/).

## When to use it

Use LR-aided detection for square QAM or PAM when ZF or MMSE detection loses too much against ML, the number of streams is too large for a sphere search, and the channel stays the same for many symbol vectors, so the reduction is paid once per channel. When the channel changes for each vector and $n$ is small, a [K-best detector](/k-best-detector/) can be cheaper. For the exact ML vector, use [Schnorr-Euchner enumeration](/schnorr-euchner-enumeration/), which also gains from a reduced basis.

## Pitfalls

- **Rounding in the wrong domain.** Rounding needs the shifted and scaled integer vector $z$, not the constellation values. For QAM, the shift by $(q - 1)/2$ per real dimension and the scale $s / 2$ must match the constellation exactly.
- **Forgetting to clip.** $T u$ can lie outside $\{0, \ldots, q - 1\}^n$, because the reduced basis does not see the box. Without line L7, the result is not a constellation vector.
- **Mismatched $T$.** Every swap and size reduction of LLL must be applied to $T$ as well. If $T$ is updated in a different order from $B$, the mapping back is wrong.
- **Round half to even.** Python's built-in round sends 0.5 to 0 and 1.5 to 2. A detector should use one fixed rule; the implementation uses $\lfloor v + 1/2 \rfloor$.
- **A singular channel.** With $n > m$ in the real model, the columns of $H_r$ are linearly dependent and LLL as written fails. The MMSE extension makes the basis full rank.

## History

The idea of using a reduced lattice basis for decoding comes from Babai, who in 1986 analysed rounding and the nearest plane method on an LLL-reduced basis. Lenstra, Lenstra and Lovász had published the LLL algorithm in 1982. Yao and Wornell proposed LR-aided linear and SIC detection for MIMO in 2002, and Windpassinger and Fischer applied LLL to it in 2003. Taherzadeh, Mobasher and Khandani proved in 2007 that LLL reduction gives the receive diversity of lattice decoding. Later work studied complex LLL, MMSE-based reduction and hardware designs.
