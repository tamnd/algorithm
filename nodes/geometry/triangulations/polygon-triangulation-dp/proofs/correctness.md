---
title: The interval dynamic program finds a minimum weight triangulation of a simple polygon
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

**Claim.** Let $P$ be a simple polygon with $n \ge 3$ distinct vertices (consecutive collinear vertices allowed) and let $w$ be any real weight on triangles. POLYGON-TRIANGULATION-DP$(P, w)$ stops and returns the least value of $\sum_{t \in T} w(t)$ over all triangulations $T$ of $P$, together with a triangulation that attains it.

**Setup.** After line L1 the vertices $v_0, \ldots, v_{n-1}$ are in counterclockwise order, so the interior lies to the left of each edge. A *diagonal* of a simple polygon is a segment between two of its vertices whose relative interior lies in its interior. For $0 \le i < j \le n - 1$ call the pair $(i, j)$ *admissible* if $j = i + 1$, or $(i, j) = (0, n - 1)$, or $v_i v_j$ is a diagonal of $P$. For an admissible pair with $j \ge i + 2$, let $P(i, j)$ be the closed curve $v_i, v_{i+1}, \ldots, v_j$ followed by the segment $v_j v_i$. Note $P(0, n-1) = P$.

**Lemma 1 (the diagonal test).** For non-adjacent $i, j$, IS-DIAGONAL$(i, j)$ returns true exactly when $v_i v_j$ is a diagonal of $P$.

*Proof.* Write $s = v_i v_j$, $a = v_i$, $p = v_{i-1}$, $q = v_{i+1}$. Near $a$, the interior of $P$ is the open wedge swept counterclockwise from the direction of $q$ to the direction of $p$. If the corner is convex ($\mathrm{orient}(p, a, q) > 0$), this wedge is the intersection of the open half-planes to the left of the lines $p \to a$ and $a \to q$; otherwise (reflex or flat corner) it is their union. Line L10 tests exactly whether $v_j$ lies in this open wedge, that is, whether the points of $s$ close to $a$ are interior points.

($\Rightarrow$) If $s$ is a diagonal, its relative interior contains no boundary point, so no other vertex lies on $s$ and no edge crosses it properly, and its points near $a$ are interior, so line L10 accepts. Note that the end points $v_i, v_j$ are not in the relative interior, and the closed segment holds a vertex $v_k \ne v_i, v_j$ only in its relative interior.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose an edge $e$ meets the relative interior of $s$ at a point $x$. If $x$ is an end point of $e$, a vertex other than $v_i, v_j$ lies on $s$, which line L9 rejects. Otherwise $x$ is in the relative interiors of both segments. If they are not collinear, the crossing is proper and line L9 rejects it. If they are collinear and overlap, an end point of one lies in the other: an end point of $e$ on $s$ is rejected by line L9, and $v_i$ or $v_j$ in the relative interior of an edge contradicts simplicity. So the relative interior of $s$ misses the boundary. It is connected, and by line L10 its points near $a$ are interior, so all of it lies in the interior. $\square$

**Lemma 2 (sub-polygons).** Let $(i, j)$ be admissible with $j \ge i + 2$. Then $P(i, j)$ is a simple counterclockwise polygon, and for $i \le a < b \le j$ with $(a, b) \ne (i, j)$, the segment $v_a v_b$ is a diagonal of $P(i, j)$ exactly when it is a diagonal of $P$.

*Proof.* If $(i, j) = (0, n - 1)$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise the diagonal $d = v_i v_j$ splits $P$ into two simple polygons, $P(i, j)$ and the other one, $P'$, with disjoint interiors whose union with the relative interior of $d$ is the interior of $P$; $P(i, j)$ keeps the counterclockwise order of its vertices. A diagonal of $P(i, j)$ has its relative interior inside $P(i, j)$, hence inside $P$. Conversely, let $s = v_a v_b$ be a diagonal of $P$. Its relative interior $\sigma$ is connected and lies in the interior of $P$. Suppose $\sigma$ meets the relative interior of $d$ at a point $x$. If $s$ and $d$ are collinear, then $s \supseteq d$ and $s \ne d$ puts $v_i$ or $v_j$, which are boundary points, in $\sigma$, which is impossible. Otherwise $s$ crosses $d$ at $x$; just beyond $x$ it enters the interior of $P'$, and it stays there up to its end point, because it cannot leave $P'$ through the boundary of $P$ (which $\sigma$ misses) nor through $d$ (which a segment not collinear with $d$ meets only once). So that end point is a vertex of $P(i, j)$ in the closure of $P'$, which forces it to be $v_i$ or $v_j$. Then $s$ meets the line of $d$ at two points, $x$ and that end point, and is collinear with $d$, a contradiction. So $\sigma$ avoids $d$. So $\sigma$ lies in the union of the two disjoint open interiors and, being connected, in one of them. If it lay in the interior of $P'$, both end points of $s$ would be vertices of $P'$ and of $P(i, j)$, so $s = d$, which is excluded. So $\sigma$ lies in the interior of $P(i, j)$. $\square$

**Lemma 3 (the recurrence).** Let $(i, j)$ be admissible with $j \ge i + 2$. In every triangulation $T$ of $P(i, j)$, the side $v_i v_j$ lies in exactly one triangle, $v_i v_k v_j$ with $i < k < j$, and the pairs $(i, k)$ and $(k, j)$ are admissible. The other triangles of $T$ form a triangulation of $P(i, k)$ (none if $k = i + 1$) and one of $P(k, j)$ (none if $k = j - 1$). Conversely, for any such $k$ and any triangulations of $P(i, k)$ and $P(k, j)$, adding $v_i v_k v_j$ gives a triangulation of $P(i, j)$.

*Proof.* The side $v_i v_j$ is on the boundary of $P(i, j)$, so exactly one triangle of $T$ has it as a side; its third corner is a vertex $v_k$ of $P(i, j)$, so $i < k < j$. Its sides $v_i v_k$ and $v_k v_j$ are polygon edges or diagonals of $P(i, j)$, hence by Lemma 2 edges or diagonals of $P$: the pairs are admissible. The diagonal $v_i v_k$ (if $k > i + 1$) splits $P(i, j)$, and the triangles on its far side triangulate $P(i, k)$; the same holds for $v_k v_j$. Conversely, $P(i, k)$, the triangle and $P(k, j)$ have disjoint interiors and union $P(i, j)$, because the two diagonals split $P(i, j)$ into these three pieces; the triangles of the three parts meet edge to edge. $\square$

**The invariant.** After the pass of line L4 with value $d$, for every admissible pair $(i, j)$ with $2 \le j - i \le d$, $\mathrm{cost}[i][j]$ is the least weight $\mathrm{OPT}(i, j)$ of a triangulation of $P(i, j)$, and the triangle $v_i v_{\mathrm{split}[i][j]} v_j$ is the side-$v_i v_j$ triangle of an optimal one. Also $\mathrm{cost}[i][i+1] = 0 = \mathrm{OPT}(i, i+1)$, the empty triangulation (line L3).

*Maintenance.* Fix $d$ and an admissible $(i, j)$ with $j - i = d$. Line L2 sets $\mathrm{ok}$ to admissibility, by Lemma 1. Line L6 ranges over all $k$ with $(i, k)$ and $(k, j)$ admissible; their lengths are less than $d$, so their costs are already optimal by the invariant. By Lemma 3, every triangulation of $P(i, j)$ has weight $W_1 + W_2 + w(v_i v_k v_j)$ for some such $k$ and triangulations of weights $W_1 \ge \mathrm{OPT}(i, k)$ and $W_2 \ge \mathrm{OPT}(k, j)$, so its weight is at least the minimum computed in line L6. Conversely, the minimizing $k$ with optimal triangulations of the two parts is a triangulation of $P(i, j)$ of exactly that weight. So the minimum equals $\mathrm{OPT}(i, j)$. At least one $k$ qualifies, because $P(i, j)$ has a triangulation (two ears theorem) and Lemma 3 applies to it.

*Exit.* After $d = n - 1$, $\mathrm{cost}[0][n-1] = \mathrm{OPT}(0, n - 1)$, the optimum for $P$. Line L7 starts from $(0, n - 1)$; for each pair with $j - i \ge 2$ it outputs the triangle with corner $\mathrm{split}[i][j]$ and continues with $(i, k)$ and $(k, j)$. By Lemma 3 and induction on $j - i$, the triangles output for a pair form an optimal triangulation of $P(i, j)$. Each pair is pushed once and each triangle output once, so line L7 stops after $n - 2$ triangles.

**Termination.** All loops are bounded: $d$ runs over $n - 2$ values, $i$ over at most $n$, $k$ over at most $n$, and line L7 handles at most $2n$ pairs. $\blacksquare$

**Weights.** The proof only uses that weights are real numbers added and compared exactly. With Euclidean lengths, which are square roots, an exact comparison of two sums of square roots is not known to be possible in polynomial time on a bit model; the dynamic program is exact on the [real RAM](/real-ram/).
