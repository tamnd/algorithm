---
title: Chew's second algorithm stops with a constrained Delaunay mesh of quality B
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

**Setting.** The domain $\Omega$ is a strictly convex polygon, given counterclockwise, whose interior angles are all at least $60°$. Inside it lie finitely many segments and points that touch neither the boundary nor each other. The **features** are the boundary edges, the interior segments, the interior points, and the end points of all of these. The **segments** are the boundary edges and the interior segments. At every moment each segment is cut into **subsegments** by the vertices on it. The **input vertices** are the corners of $\Omega$, the end points of the interior segments and the interior points. A vertex is a **segment vertex** if it is a midpoint inserted at line L8, and a **free vertex** if it is a circumcenter inserted at line L5. The bound is $B^2 \ge 5/4$. A triangle is **skinny** when its circumradius $R$ and shortest edge $\ell$ satisfy $R > B \ell$. Let $\theta = \arcsin(1/(2B))$, so $0 < \theta \le \arctan(1/2)$ and $B \cos \theta = \sqrt{B^2 - 1/4} \ge 1$.

A point $u$ **sees** a vertex $w$ when the open segment $uw$ meets no subsegment other than $uw$ itself. A triangle $t$ of the mesh is **constrained Delaunay** when no vertex that is seen from a point of the interior of $t$ lies in the open disk bounded by the circumcircle of $t$.

The **local feature size** $\mathrm{lfs}(x)$ of a point $x \in \Omega$ is the radius of the smallest closed disk centered at $x$ that meets two disjoint features. It is positive and continuous on the compact set $\Omega$, so $\lambda = \min_\Omega \mathrm{lfs} > 0$. Two distinct input vertices are disjoint features, so they are at distance at least $\lambda$.

## Invariants

**(I1) The mesh is a triangulation of $\Omega$ whose edges include all subsegments, and all its triangles are constrained Delaunay.** The code keeps a triangulation and changes it in four ways: splitting a triangle or an edge at a new point (line L11), deleting a vertex and triangulating the hole by ear clipping (line L13), recovering an interior segment by removing the triangles it crosses and ear clipping the two sides (line L1), and flipping an edge (line L12). Each change keeps a triangulation of $\Omega$ with the subsegments as edges; the split of a subsegment at line L8 also replaces it by its two halves among the constrained edges. Every edge of a triangle created by a change is pushed on a stack, and an edge is flipped only when it is not a subsegment and the opposite vertex lies strictly inside the circumcircle, in which case the quadrilateral is strictly convex. Each flip increases the sorted vector of angles in lexicographic order, so the flips stop. An edge not on the stack has the same two triangles as when it was last checked, so at the end every edge that is not a subsegment is locally Delaunay. By the constrained Delaunay lemma (Chew 1989; see Cheng, Dey and Shewchuk, Chapter 2), a triangulation in which every edge that is not a constrained edge is locally Delaunay has only constrained Delaunay triangles.

**(I2) The subsegments partition the segments, and the vertices on a segment are exactly the end points of its subsegments.** Initially the subsegments are the segments, and the only vertices on them are their end points, because the input features are disjoint. A split replaces $ab$ by $am$ and $mb$. A free vertex lies on no subsegment (Lemma 4), and only free vertices are deleted.

**(I3) The queue is complete.** Every triangle created by a change is queued, a triangle whose circumcenter is rejected is queued again, and entries that are no longer triangles of the mesh are skipped. So every skinny triangle of the mesh is in the queue.

**(I4) Visibility only shrinks.** Subsegments are only split, never removed, so their union never changes except by growing when interior segments are recovered at line L1. If the open segment $uw$ meets a subsegment $\sigma$ at some moment, it meets a piece of $\sigma$ at every later moment. That piece is $uw$ itself only if $u$ and $w$ are vertices on $\sigma$, and then by (I2) they were already the end points of a subsegment. So if two vertices see each other at some moment, they saw each other at every earlier moment at which both were present.

## Lemma 1: the sweep

Let $u$ be a point that is a vertex or lies on no subsegment, let $w \ne u$ be a vertex, and let $y$ be a point. Suppose no subsegment meets the open segment $(u, y)$, and either (A) $y$ lies on no subsegment and no subsegment meets the open segment $(y, w)$, or (B) $u, y, w$ are not collinear and $[y, w]$ is contained in one subsegment $\sigma_0$ with $y$ in its relative interior. Then $u$ sees some vertex $z \ne u$ of the closed triangle $\Delta = uyw$.

*Proof.* If $u, y, w$ are collinear, we are in case (A), and the open segment $uw$ is contained in $(u, y) \cup \{y\} \cup (y, w)$ or in $(y, w)$, so $u$ sees $w$. Otherwise, for a point $p \in \Delta \setminus \{u\}$ let $\varphi(p)$ be the angle between the rays $uy$ and $up$. Among the finitely many vertices in $\Delta \setminus \{u\}$, which include $w$, choose $z$ with the smallest $\varphi$, and among those the nearest to $u$. If $\varphi(z) = 0$, then $z \in (u, y]$, and $z \ne y$ in case (B) because $y$ is inside a subsegment, so $(u, z) \subseteq (u, y)$ and $u$ sees $z$.

Let $\varphi(z) > 0$ and suppose a subsegment $\sigma \ne uz$ meets the open segment $(u, z)$. If $\sigma$ lies on the line $uz$, it does not contain $u$ in its relative interior, so it has an end point on $(u, z)$, or it is $uz$, or it contains $z$ in its relative interior; the first contradicts the choice of $z$ and the other two are excluded by (I2) and $\sigma \ne uz$. Otherwise $\sigma$ crosses the line $uz$ at one point $v \in (u, z)$. Let $\Delta_1 = \{p \in \Delta : \varphi(p) \le \varphi(z)\}$, the triangle with corners $u$, $y$ and the point $z''$ where the ray $uz$ meets $[y, w]$. Near $v$, the points of $\sigma$ on the side of $y$ lie in the interior of $\Delta_1$. Follow $\sigma$ from $v$ to that side. It cannot meet the line $uz$ again, it cannot pass through $u$ (then $u$ would be an end point and $\sigma$ would lie on the line $uz$), it cannot meet $(u, y)$ by hypothesis, and it cannot meet $[y, z'')$: in case (A) by hypothesis, and in case (B) because a point of $[y, z'')$ is in the relative interior of $\sigma_0$, so $\sigma = \sigma_0$, which lies on the line $yw$ and does not meet $(u, z)$. So $\sigma$ ends inside $\Delta_1$, at a vertex $e$ with $\varphi(e) < \varphi(z)$. This contradicts the choice of $z$. $\square$

## Lemma 2: the circumcenter test

Let $t$ be a triangle of the mesh with circumcenter $c$, open circumdisk $D$ and centroid $x$, and suppose (I1).

(a) If no subsegment meets the closed segment $[x, c]$, then $c$ lies in $\Omega$, on no subsegment, at no vertex, and every vertex that $c$ sees is at distance at least $R$ from $c$.

(b) Otherwise, let $y$ be the point of $[x, c]$ nearest to $x$ that lies on a subsegment. Then $y$ lies in the relative interior of exactly one subsegment $ab$, the vertices $a$ and $b$ are not in $D$, and all three vertices of $t$ lie in the closed half disk $H$ bounded by the circle with diameter $ab$ on the side of $x$.

*Proof.* (a) $\Omega$ is convex and its boundary is covered by subsegments, so the segment from $x \in \Omega$ to $c$ stays in $\Omega$. If $c$ were a vertex, it would be a vertex in $D$ seen from $x$, against (I1). If $c$ saw a vertex $w \in D$, Lemma 1 (A) with $u = x$ and $y = c$ would give a vertex $z$ of the triangle $xcw \subset D$ seen from $x$, again against (I1).

(b) The points $x$ and $c$ lie in $D$, so $y \in D$. If $y$ were a vertex, $x$ would see it. So $y$ is in the relative interior of a subsegment $ab$, and only of one, since two subsegments meet only at a common end point. The point $x$ is not on the line $L$ through $a$ and $b$: otherwise $(x, y)$ would contain points of $ab$ near $y$, because $x$, an interior point of a triangle, is not on the edge $ab$. If $a \in D$, Lemma 1 (B) with $u = x$, $w = a$ and $\sigma_0 = ab$ gives a vertex of the triangle $xya \subset D$ seen from $x$, a contradiction; the same holds for $b$. So the line $L$ meets the closed disk $\bar D$ in a chord $[l_1, l_2]$ that contains $y$ and lies inside $[a, b]$. The triangle $t$ lies in $\bar D$, so $t \cap L \subseteq [a, b]$, and the interior of $t$ does not meet the edge $ab$. So $t$ lies in the closed half plane of $L$ that contains $x$, and its vertices lie on the arc of the circumcircle on that side. The point $c$ lies on the other side of $L$ or on it, so that arc is at most a semicircle, and every point of it sees $[l_1, l_2]$ at an angle of at least $90°$. So the arc lies in the closed disk with diameter $[l_1, l_2]$, which lies in the closed disk with diameter $ab$. $\square$

## Lemma 3: neighbors of a segment

Let $ab$ be a subsegment of a segment $S$ with midpoint $m$ and half length $h$, and let $p \notin \{a, b\}$ be an input vertex or a segment vertex with $|mp| \le h$. Then either $p$ lies on a feature disjoint from $S$, and $|mp| \ge \mathrm{lfs}(m)$, or $S$ is a boundary edge, $p$ lies on a boundary edge $S'$ that meets $S$ at a corner $o \ne p$ with angle $\alpha \in [60°, 90°)$, and $|mp| \ge |op|$.

*Proof.* The closed disk with diameter $ab$ meets the line of $S$ only in $[a, b]$, which contains no vertex but $a$ and $b$ by (I2), so $p \notin S$. The input features are disjoint, so if $p$ lies on a feature that meets $S$, then $S$ and that feature are boundary edges meeting at a corner $o$, with $o \in S$ and so $o \ne p$. In the first case the disk around $m$ of radius $|mp|$ meets $S$ and a disjoint feature. In the second, put $s = |om|$, $r = |mp|$, and let $\alpha$ be the angle of $\Omega$ at $o$. The end point of $ab$ nearer to $o$ is at distance $s - h \ge 0$ from $o$, so $r \le h \le s$. In the triangle $omp$, the side $r$ is opposite the angle $\alpha$ at $o$ and the side $s$ is opposite the angle at $p$, so the angle at $p$ is at least $\alpha$, and the angle at $m$ is at most $180° - 2\alpha$. For $\alpha \ge 90°$ this is impossible. For $60° \le \alpha < 90°$, the law of sines gives $|op| / r = \sin \angle m / \sin \alpha \le \sin(180° - 2\alpha) / \sin \alpha = 2 \cos \alpha \le 1$. $\square$

## Correctness of the output

Lemma 2 (a) shows that an accepted circumcenter lies in $\Omega$ and on no subsegment, so line L11 always finds a triangle, and a midpoint lies on its subsegment, which is an edge by (I1). When the loop of line L2 ends, the queue is empty, so by (I3) no triangle is skinny. By (I1) and (I2), the mesh is a triangulation of $\Omega$ made of constrained Delaunay triangles, every segment is a chain of subsegments that are mesh edges, and the input vertices are vertices of the mesh, since only free vertices are deleted. Every triangle has $R \le B \ell$, and since $\ell = 2 R \sin \theta_{\min}$ by the law of sines, its smallest angle satisfies $\sin \theta_{\min} \ge 1/(2B)$. For $B^2 = 5/4$ this is $\theta_{\min} \ge \arcsin(1/\sqrt 5) \approx 26.57°$. Line L9 returns this mesh.

## Termination

The **insertion radius** $r_v$ of a vertex $v$ inserted by the loop is the distance from $v$ to the nearest vertex that $v$ sees just after its insertion. For an input vertex, $r_v$ is its distance to the nearest other input vertex, so $r_v \ge \lambda$.

**Short edges have young end points.** If $pq$ is an edge of the mesh at some moment and $q$ was inserted after $p$, then $|pq| \ge r_q$. Indeed $p$ was present when $q$ was inserted, the two see each other now, so they did then by (I4), and $r_q$ is a minimum over the vertices $q$ saw. For two input vertices, $|pq| \ge \lambda$.

**Deletions keep vertices away from new midpoints.** Let $q$ be a free vertex and let $a$ be a midpoint inserted after $q$, at the split of a subsegment of half length $g$. If $q$ is still present and sees $a$, then $|qa| \ge g$. Indeed, at the split $q$ saw $a$ by (I4), and line L7 deleted every free vertex strictly inside the diametral circle that saw the midpoint. The two halves have length $g$, and every later subsegment with end point $a$ lies in one of them.

We show by induction on the order of insertion that every segment vertex has $r_v \ge \lambda$ and every free vertex has $r_v \ge \mu$, where $\mu = \lambda / \cos \theta \ge \lambda$.

*A circumcenter $c$ inserted at line L5.* Let $\ell$ be the shortest edge of its triangle and $p$ its younger end point. By Lemma 2 (a), $r_c \ge R > B \ell$. If $p$ is an input vertex, $\ell \ge \lambda$; otherwise $\ell \ge r_p \ge \lambda$. So $r_c > B \lambda \ge \lambda / \cos \theta = \mu$.

*A midpoint $m$ of a subsegment $ab$ of a segment $S$, split at line L8 because of a skinny triangle $t$.* Let $h = |ab| / 2$. By Lemma 2 (b), the vertices of $t$ lie in $H$. First we show $h \ge \lambda$.

- If a vertex $p \notin \{a, b\}$ of $t$ is an input vertex or a segment vertex, then $|mp| \le h$, and Lemma 3 gives $h \ge |mp| \ge \mathrm{lfs}(m) \ge \lambda$ or $h \ge |mp| \ge |op| \ge r_p \ge \lambda$. Here $r_p \le |op|$ because $o$ is an input vertex on the segment of $p$, and the nearest vertex towards $o$ on that segment was seen by $p$ along a subsegment.
- Otherwise every vertex of $t$ other than $a$ and $b$ is free. Order the vertices of $t$ along its arc in $H$ as $v_1, v_2, v_3$. The arc is at most a semicircle, so the angle at $v_2$ is at least $90°$, the longest edge is $v_1 v_3$, and $|v_1 v_3| \le 2h$, the diameter of $H$. The triangle is skinny, so its smallest angle, say $\beta$ at $v_1$, is less than $\theta$, and the shortest edge is $\ell = |v_2 v_3|$. With $\gamma$ the angle at $v_3$, we have $2 \beta \le \beta + \gamma \le 90°$, so $\ell = |v_1 v_3| \sin \beta / \sin(\beta + \gamma) \le |v_1 v_3| \sin \beta / \sin 2\beta = |v_1 v_3| / (2 \cos \beta) < h / \cos \theta$. Let $p$ be the younger end point of the shortest edge. If $p$ is free, then $h > \ell \cos \theta \ge r_p \cos \theta \ge \mu \cos \theta = \lambda$. If $p$ is not free, then $p \in \{a, b\}$, say $p = a$, and the other end point $q$ is free, because $ab$ is the longest edge when it is an edge of $t$. Then $a$ is a midpoint inserted after $q$, $q$ sees $a$ along the edge $qa$, and the subsegment $ab$ lies in a half of length $g$ of the subsegment that $a$ split, so $|qa| \ge g \ge 2h$. But $q \in H$ and $q \ne b$, so $|qa| < 2h$. This case cannot happen.

Now the vertices that $m$ sees after the deletions of line L7 are of three kinds. Those at distance at least $h$, including $a$ and $b$, are at distance at least $\lambda$. Free vertices strictly inside the diametral circle that see $m$ were deleted. Input vertices and segment vertices strictly inside are at distance at least $\lambda$ by Lemma 3, as in the first item. So $r_m \ge \lambda$, and the induction is complete. We also proved that every subsegment that is split has half length $h \ge \lambda$.

**Only finitely many splits and deletions.** Every subsegment ever created has length at least $\lambda$, so a segment of length $L$ is never cut into more than $L / \lambda$ pieces, and each split adds a piece. So there are finitely many splits, and since deletions happen only at splits, finitely many deletions.

**Only finitely many vertices at a time.** Cut the plane by a grid of squares of side $\lambda / 2$ and by the lines that carry the segments. The cells that meet $\Omega$ are finitely many closed convex polygons $K$ of diameter less than $\lambda$, and no subsegment meets the interior of a cell. Suppose two vertices $u \ne w$ present at the same moment lie in the same cell $K$. If no subsegment meets the open segment $uw$, they see each other. Otherwise a subsegment meets $uw \subset K$ on the boundary of $K$, so $uw$ lies on a side of $K$, on the line of that subsegment, and every subsegment meeting $uw$ lies on that line. Then $u$ sees the vertex $z$ on $(u, w]$ nearest to $u$. In both cases two vertices less than $\lambda$ apart see each other, and by (I4) the younger one saw the older one when it was inserted, so its insertion radius is less than $\lambda$. This contradicts the induction. So at every moment there are at most as many vertices as cells.

Between two splits no vertex is deleted, so at most that many circumcenters are inserted. With finitely many splits, the loop inserts finitely many vertices. Every other iteration removes a queue entry, and each change adds finitely many entries, so the loop stops. $\square$

**Remark.** The bound $B^2 \ge 5/4$ is used only in the circumcenter case, through $B \cos \theta \ge 1$: a circumcenter whose parent is a segment vertex must be far enough from it that a later midpoint, which may lose a factor $\cos \theta$, is still at distance $\lambda$. The input angle bound of $60°$ is used only in Lemma 3, where it gives $2 \cos \alpha \le 1$. This is the analysis of Shewchuk (2002, Section 4.2), with the deletion argument made explicit. Chew's own bound of $30°$ uses a different choice of the inserted point.
