---
title: The stack algorithm triangulates a monotone polygon, and the sweep makes the pieces monotone
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

**Claim.** (a) Let $M$ be a simple polygon with $m \ge 3$ vertices that is monotone in the sweep order. TRIANGULATE-MONOTONE$(M)$ outputs $m - 2$ triangles with corners among the vertices of $M$, with pairwise disjoint interiors and union $M$. (b) For a simple polygon $P$, the sweep MAKE-MONOTONE adds non-crossing diagonals that cut $P$ into monotone pieces, so running (a) on each piece triangulates $P$.

**The sweep order.** Write $p \succ q$ ($p$ is *above* $q$) if $p_y > q_y$, or $p_y = q_y$ and $p_x < q_x$. This is a strict total order on distinct points. It is the order of the linear function $f_\varepsilon(p) = p_y - \varepsilon p_x$ for every small enough $\varepsilon > 0$ on the finite set of vertices, that is, the order of heights after a tiny clockwise rotation. Because of this, every statement below may be read for a rotated polygon in which no two vertices have the same height, and horizontal edges need no special care. A polygon is *monotone* if its boundary, from the highest vertex to the lowest, consists of two chains along which the order $\succ$ decreases strictly: the *left chain* (counterclockwise from the top) and the *right chain*. Every horizontal line (of the rotated picture) meets a monotone polygon in a segment, a point, or not at all.

## Part (a): the stack algorithm

Let $u_1 \succ u_2 \succ \cdots \succ u_m$ be the vertices in sweep order (line L1); $u_1$ is the top, $u_m$ the bottom, and both are counted on both chains. For a vertex $u$ let $\mathrm{chain}(u)$ be its chain.

**Reflex chains.** A sequence $s_1, \ldots, s_t$ of vertices on one chain, consecutive in sweep order on that chain (except possibly $s_1$, see below), is *reflex in $R$* for a polygon $R$ if each consecutive pair is joined by an edge of $R$ and, for $2 \le i \le t - 1$, the angle of $R$ at $s_i$ is at least $\pi$. On the left chain, traversed downward, this means $\mathrm{orient}(s_{i-1}, s_i, s_{i+1}) \le 0$; on the right chain, $\ge 0$.

**Invariant.** Before handling $u_j$ ($3 \le j \le m - 1$; line L3), with the stack $S = s_1, \ldots, s_t$ from bottom to top:

1. $t \ge 2$, $s_t = u_{j-1}$, and $s_2, \ldots, s_t$ lie on one chain $A$; $s_1$ is the lowest vertex handled so far on the other chain $B$ (possibly $u_1$).
2. The triangles output so far have disjoint interiors, lie in $M$, and together with a polygon $R$ cover $M$ with disjoint interiors. $R$ is simple and monotone, its boundary runs $s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_t$, then the vertices of $A$ below $s_t$, then $u_m$, then the vertices of $B$ below $s_1$ upward to $s_1$. So the vertices of $R$ are the stack and $u_j, \ldots, u_m$.
3. $s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_t$ is reflex in $R$.

*Initialization.* Line L2 sets $S = u_1, u_2$ and $R = M$. Then $u_2$ is adjacent to $u_1$, both conditions hold trivially, and $s_1 = u_1$ is on $B$.

**A visibility fact.** Let $R_j$ be the part of $R$ that lies (in the rotated picture) on or above the horizontal line through $u_j$. All vertices of $R$ above that line are stack vertices, by item 2. Since $R$ is monotone, $R_j$ is bounded by the boundary of $R$ from the left crossing point of the line to the right crossing point, through $s_1, \ldots, s_t$, and by the segment on the line between the crossing points; one of those crossing points is $u_j$ itself, which lies on the line.

*Case 1: $u_j$ on $B$* (line L4 finds different chains). Then $u_j$ follows $s_1$ on $B$, so $s_1 u_j$ is an edge of $R$, and $R_j$ is the polygon $u_j, s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_t, y$, where $y$ is the point where the edge of $A$ leaving $s_t$ downward meets the line through $u_j$. The corners $s_2, \ldots, s_{t-1}$ are reflex or flat, so the boundary path $s_1, \ldots, s_t$ is a convex curve bulging into $R_j$, and its two end segments are joined to the line through $u_j$ by straight segments. A polygon whose boundary consists of a concave chain and three straight segments, one of them on a supporting line through the remaining vertex $u_j$, is star-shaped from $u_j$: for each edge $s_i s_{i+1}$ of the chain, the whole region $R_j$, and so $u_j$, lies on the inner side of its supporting line, because the chain is convex toward $R_j$ and the straight edge $s_1 u_j$ and the line through $u_j$ close it from the other side. Therefore every segment $u_j s_i$ lies in $R_j$, and the triangles $u_j s_i s_{i+1}$ for $1 \le i \le t - 1$ (lines L5 and L6) have disjoint interiors and cover the part of $R_j$ between the chain and the segment $u_j s_t$. Each has positive area: $u_j$ is strictly below $s_i$ and $s_{i+1}$ in the rotated picture, and the line through $s_i s_{i+1}$ meets the line through $u_j$ outside $R_j$ unless $i = t - 1$ and the segment continues to $y$; in that last case $u_j \ne y$ because $u_j$ is on $B$ and $y$ on $A$. After the cut, the new $R$ has boundary $s_t = u_{j-1}, u_j, \ldots$ on the left of the cut: the stack $u_{j-1}, u_j$ satisfies items 1 to 3 with the chains exchanged, $s_1 = u_{j-1}$ now being the lowest handled vertex of the old chain $A$.

*Case 2: $u_j$ on $A$.* Then $s_t u_j$ is an edge of $R$. Line L7 pops $s_t$ as *last*. Line L8 tests whether the triangle $u_j, \textit{last}, s_{i}$ (with $s_i$ the new top) is a counterclockwise ear of the current $R$ at the tip *last*: its neighbors in $R$ are $s_i$ and $u_j$, and the test asks for a strictly convex corner. If the corner is strictly convex, no vertex of $R$ lies in the closed triangle: the vertices below $u_j$ are below all three corners, and the stack vertices $s_1, \ldots, s_{i-1}$ lie on the outer side of the supporting line of the reflex chain at $s_i$, hence outside. By the ear test (Lemma 1 of the [ear clipping proof](/ear-clipping/)), *last* is an ear, and cutting it leaves a simple monotone polygon in which $s_i$ is adjacent to $u_j$. The loop repeats this while the corner at the new *last* is strictly convex. When it stops, either the stack is empty or the corner at *last* between its stack neighbor and $u_j$ is reflex or flat. Line L9 pushes *last* and $u_j$. The new stack is reflex in the new $R$: the earlier part was reflex, and the corner at *last* is not strictly convex. Items 1 and 2 hold with $s_t = u_j$.

**Exit.** After $j = m - 1$, $u_m$ is adjacent in $R$ to $s_t = u_{m-1}$ and to $s_1$ (both chains end at $u_m$), so $R$ is the polygon $u_m, s_1, \ldots, s_t$ and the reasoning of Case 1 applies with $u_m$ in the role of $u_j$: line L10 outputs the fan from $u_m$, which triangulates $R$. All triangles have corners among the vertices and disjoint interiors, and they cover $M$. Since a triangulation of a polygon with $m$ vertices has $m - 2$ triangles, the count is $m - 2$.

**Termination.** Each vertex is pushed at most twice (once as $u_j$, once more in line L6 or line L9), and each pass of a pop loop removes one entry, so the total work is $O(m)$.

## Part (b): the monotone partition

Classify each vertex $v$ by its two neighbors in the sweep order: *start* (both neighbors below, interior angle less than $\pi$), *split* (both below, angle more than $\pi$), *end* (both above, angle less than $\pi$), *merge* (both above, angle more than $\pi$), and *regular* otherwise. An angle of exactly $\pi$ makes the two neighbors lie on opposite sides of $v$ in the sweep order, so a flat vertex is regular.

**Lemma (de Berg et al., Lemma 3.4).** A simple polygon without split and merge vertices is monotone. *Proof.* If it is not monotone, some horizontal line (in the rotated picture) meets it in more than one component. Walk along the boundary from a point where the line enters the polygon with the interior above (or below) the line, until the boundary meets the line again; the extreme vertex of that boundary path, on the side away from the line, has both neighbors on the line side and its interior angle exceeds $\pi$, so it is a split or merge vertex. $\square$

**The sweep.** MAKE-MONOTONE processes the vertices in sweep order and keeps, in the status, the edges of $P$ that cross the sweep line and have the interior of $P$ immediately to their right; each such edge $e$ has a *helper*, the lowest vertex above the sweep line that sees $e$ horizontally. At a split vertex $v$, it adds the diagonal from $v$ to the helper of the edge directly to its left; at a merge vertex, it sets $v$ as the helper of that edge and of the edge ending at $v$, and the diagonal is added from the next vertex that replaces that helper. de Berg et al. prove (Lemma 3.5) that every added segment is a diagonal of $P$ that crosses no other added diagonal: the segment from $v$ to the helper lies in the trapezoid between $e$, the edge directly to the right of $v$, and the horizontal lines through $v$ and the helper, which contains no vertex inside because the helper is the lowest vertex seen so far between those edges. Each added diagonal removes the split or merge character of both end points in the pieces, so by the lemma every piece is monotone. The tilted order makes these arguments valid when vertices share a $y$ coordinate.

**Faces.** The polygon edges and the $k$ added diagonals form a plane graph whose bounded faces are the $k + 1$ monotone pieces. Tracing each half-edge to the next one clockwise around its head, with exact angle comparisons, lists every bounded face once, counterclockwise. Part (a) triangulates each piece, and the union of the triangulations is a triangulation of $P$. $\blacksquare$
