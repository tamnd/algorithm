---
title: Deleting inessential diagonals gives a convex partition with at most 2r + 1 pieces
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

We use the pseudocode of the page. $P$ is a simple polygon in counterclockwise order with $r$ reflex vertices, and $T$ is a triangulation of $P$ into $n - 2$ triangles with positive area, whose sides are the edges of $P$ and a set $D_0$ of $n - 3$ diagonals that meet only at endpoints. For a set $D \subseteq D_0$, the *pieces* of $D$ are the closures of the connected components of the interior of $P$ minus the diagonals in $D$; each is the union of the triangles of $T$ that it contains. For a piece $A$ with a vertex $w$, the *angle of $A$ at $w$* is its interior angle there. For three points, $\operatorname{orient}(x, w, y) > 0$, $= 0$ or $< 0$ according as the boundary turns left, goes straight or turns right at $w$; so if $x, w, y$ are consecutive on the counterclockwise boundary of a polygon whose angle $\theta$ at $w$ lies in $(0, 2\pi)$, then $\operatorname{orient}(x, w, y) \ge 0$ if and only if $\theta \le \pi$.

**Claim.** The loop runs $n - 3$ times. The output is the set of pieces of the set $D$ of diagonals that were not deleted; each piece is convex and is listed counterclockwise; every diagonal in $D$ is essential; and $|D| \le 2r$, so there are at most $2r + 1$ pieces.

**Step 1 (two pieces meet in one diagonal).** Let $A$ and $B$ be two convex pieces of some $D$ on the two sides of a diagonal $uv \in D$. Then $A \cap B = uv$. Indeed $A \cap B$ is convex and lies on the boundary of both, so it is contained in a line, and it contains $uv$. If it were longer than $uv$, it would contain a point $w$ beyond $u$, say, on the line through $uv$; near $u$, the two pieces would then cover both sides of the line, so $u$ would be an interior point of $A \cup B \subseteq P$, but $u$ is a vertex of $P$.

**Step 2 (invariant).** Before each iteration of line L2, with $D$ the diagonals not yet deleted: (a) the cycles of $\operatorname{next}$ are exactly the boundaries of the pieces of $D$, each traversed counterclockwise, (b) every piece is convex, and (c) for each $\{u, v\} \in D$, the half-edges $u \to v$ and $v \to u$ exist and lie on the cycles of the two pieces on the two sides of $uv$. After line L1, the pieces of $D_0$ are the triangles of $T$, and (a), (b), (c) hold: each triangle is a cycle of three half-edges in counterclockwise order, a triangle is convex, and each diagonal is a side of exactly two triangles, one on each side, which traverse it in opposite directions.

**Step 3 (one iteration).** Let $\{u, v\} \in D$, with $h = u \to v$ on the piece $A$ and $t = v \to u$ on the piece $B$. Write $x = \operatorname{tail}(\operatorname{prev}(h))$, the vertex before $u$ on $A$; $y = \operatorname{head}(\operatorname{next}(t))$, the vertex after $u$ on $B$; $x' = \operatorname{tail}(\operatorname{prev}(t))$, the vertex before $v$ on $B$; and $y' = \operatorname{head}(\operatorname{next}(h))$, the vertex after $v$ on $A$. The pieces of $D \setminus \{uv\}$ are those of $D$ with $A$ and $B$ replaced by $M = A \cup B$. By Step 1, the boundary of $M$ is the boundary of $A$ without $h$ followed by the boundary of $B$ without $t$: the cycle $\ldots, x, u, y, \ldots, x', v, y', \ldots$, which is what line L6 builds. This is a simple closed curve, as the two boundaries meet only in $uv$. At every vertex other than $u$ and $v$, the angle of $M$ equals the angle of $A$ or of $B$. At $u$, the angle of $M$ is the sum $\theta_u$ of the angles of $A$ and $B$ at $u$, as the two pieces lie on opposite sides of $uv$; this sum is part of the angle of $P$ at $u$, so $0 < \theta_u < 2\pi$, and likewise at $v$. Hence $\alpha \ge 0$ if and only if $\theta_u \le \pi$, and $\beta \ge 0$ if and only if $\theta_v \le \pi$.

If the test at line L5 holds, every angle of $M$ is at most $\pi$. A simple polygon whose angles are all at most $\pi$ is convex (see [convex polygon](/convex-polygon/), conditions 1 and 2), so (b) holds for $D \setminus \{uv\}$. Line L6 makes $\operatorname{next}(\operatorname{prev}(h)) = \operatorname{next}(t)$ and $\operatorname{next}(\operatorname{prev}(t)) = \operatorname{next}(h)$ and deletes $h$ and $t$, so the cycle of $M$ is as described and is counterclockwise, being made of counterclockwise pieces of the boundaries of $A$ and $B$, which gives (a). The other diagonals keep their half-edges, now on $M$ where they were on $A$ or $B$, which gives (c). If the test fails, nothing changes. So the invariant holds after the iteration, whichever way the test goes.

**Step 4 (termination and output).** Line L2 visits each of the $n - 3$ diagonals once, and each iteration does a constant amount of work, so the loop stops. By the invariant, line L7 returns the counterclockwise boundaries of the pieces of the final $D$, all convex. A partition of the simple polygon $P$ by $|D|$ diagonals that do not cross has $|D| + 1$ pieces.

**Step 5 (kept diagonals stay essential).** A diagonal $uv \in D$ is *essential at $u$* if the sum of the angles at $u$ of the two pieces on its sides is greater than $\pi$, and it is essential if it is essential at $u$ or at $v$. When line L5 rejects $uv$, it is essential at $u$ or at $v$ by Step 3. Later iterations only replace pieces by larger ones: each piece of a later $D$ contains the pieces of the earlier $D$ that it was merged from. The two final pieces on the sides of $uv$ contain the two pieces of the time of the test, and both have $u$ as a vertex, so their angles at $u$ are at least as large. Hence $uv$ is still essential at the same endpoint at the end, and every diagonal of the final $D$ is essential.

**Step 6 (counting).** If $w$ is not a reflex vertex of $P$, no diagonal is essential at $w$: the two pieces at a diagonal cover part of the angle of $P$ at $w$, which is at most $\pi$. Let $w$ be reflex, with angle $\theta < 2\pi$ in $P$, and let $d_1, \ldots, d_k$ be the final diagonals at $w$ in counterclockwise order. They split the angle at $w$ into sectors $s_0, s_1, \ldots, s_k > 0$ with $s_0 + \cdots + s_k = \theta$, where $s_{i-1}$ and $s_i$ are the angles at $w$ of the two pieces on the sides of $d_i$. So $d_i$ is essential at $w$ exactly when $s_{i-1} + s_i > \pi$. Suppose three of them, $d_i, d_j, d_l$ with $i < j < l$, were essential at $w$. The pairs $\{s_{i-1}, s_i\}$ and $\{s_{l-1}, s_l\}$ are disjoint, as $i < l - 1$, so $\theta \ge (s_{i-1} + s_i) + (s_{l-1} + s_l) > 2\pi$, a contradiction. So at most two final diagonals are essential at each reflex vertex. Every final diagonal is essential at one of its endpoints, which is then reflex, so $|D| \le 2r$ and there are at most $2r + 1$ pieces.

**Step 7 (the factor four).** In any partition of $P$ into convex pieces by diagonals, each reflex vertex $w$ is an endpoint of some diagonal, as otherwise the piece containing the whole angle at $w$ would not be convex. A diagonal has two endpoints, so at least $\lceil r/2 \rceil$ diagonals and $\lceil r/2 \rceil + 1$ pieces are needed. As $4(\lceil r/2 \rceil + 1) \ge 2r + 4 > 2r + 1$, the output has fewer than four times the optimal number of pieces. $\blacksquare$
