---
title: Merging the edge vectors by angle gives the boundary of the Minkowski sum
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

We use the pseudocode of the page. Write $\theta(d) \in [0, 2\pi)$ for the angle of a nonzero vector $d$, so that $d \preceq d'$ means $\theta(d) \le \theta(d')$, and "same direction" means $\theta(d) = \theta(d')$. For a nonzero vector $d$ let $d^\perp = (d_y, -d_x)$, which is $d$ turned clockwise by $90°$; for a counterclockwise polygon it points out of the polygon on an edge with direction $d$. For a compact convex set $K$ and a vector $u$ let $F_K(u) = \{x \in K : u \cdot x = \max_{y \in K} u \cdot y\}$, the face of $K$ in direction $u$. Let $K$ also denote the convex region of a convex polygon (its convex hull, which may be a segment or a point).

**Claim.** The loop stops after $|E| + |F|$ iterations, and the output $R$ is the boundary of $P \oplus Q$ in counterclockwise order from its lowest point, with no straight vertices; it is a single point or the two endpoints when the sum is a point or a segment.

**Step 1 (the edge function).** For a compact convex polygonal set $K$ and an angle $\theta$ with unit vector $d = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, the face $F_K(d^\perp)$ lies on a line perpendicular to $d^\perp$, that is, parallel to $d$. It is a segment or a point, so $F_K(d^\perp) = [a, a + \lambda d]$ for a unique point $a$ and a unique $\lambda \ge 0$. Define $g_K(\theta) = \lambda d$. Then $g_K(\theta) \ne 0$ for finitely many $\theta$ only.

**Step 2 (the face of a sum is the sum of the faces).** For every $u$, $F_{P \oplus Q}(u) = F_P(u) \oplus F_Q(u)$. Indeed $\max_{p \in P, q \in Q} u \cdot (p + q) = \max_P u \cdot p + \max_Q u \cdot q$, and $u \cdot (p + q)$ reaches this value if and only if both terms reach their maxima. The sum of $[a, a + \lambda d]$ and $[b, b + \mu d]$ is $[a + b, a + b + (\lambda + \mu) d]$. Hence

$$g_{P \oplus Q}(\theta) = g_P(\theta) + g_Q(\theta) \quad \text{for every } \theta.$$

The same argument for the lexicographic order on $(y, x)$, which is also additive, shows that the lowest point of $P \oplus Q$ is the sum of the lowest points of $P$ and $Q$.

**Step 3 (a convex polygon is its lowest point plus its edge function in angle order).** Let $K$ be the region of a convex polygon, $z$ its lowest point, and $\theta_1 < \cdots < \theta_k$ the angles with $g_K(\theta) \ne 0$. We show that the boundary of $K$ in counterclockwise order, with straight vertices dropped, is $z, z + g_1, z + g_1 + g_2, \ldots$, where $g_t = g_K(\theta_t)$, and that $g_1 + \cdots + g_k = 0$.

If $K$ is a point, $k = 0$. If $K$ is a segment $[z, w]$, its faces are the whole segment for the two normals of $w - z$ and the single endpoints otherwise, so $g$ is nonzero at the angles of $w - z$ and $z - w$, and as $z$ is the lowest point, $\theta(w - z) \in [0, \pi)$ comes first; the list is $z, w$. Otherwise $K$ has positive area. Let its boundary, from $z$ in counterclockwise order with straight vertices removed, have edge vectors $c_1, \ldots, c_k$. By the page [convex polygon](/convex-polygon/), each turn $c_t \times c_{t+1}$ is positive and the angles of the edges turn once around, so they strictly increase from $\theta(c_1)$ to $\theta(c_1) + 2\pi$. The first edge goes from $z$ to a point that is higher, or equally high and further right, as $z$ is the leftmost of the lowest points, so $\theta(c_1) \in [0, \pi)$. The last edge comes to $z$ from a point that is higher, so $\theta(c_k) \in (\pi, 2\pi)$: if that point were equally high and further right, the edge would point in direction $(-1, 0)$, and the region, which lies to the left of each edge, would lie below $z$. So $0 \le \theta(c_1) < \cdots < \theta(c_k) < 2\pi$. For the edge $c_t$ with unit direction $d$, the region lies in the closed half-plane left of the edge line, which is $\{x : d^\perp \cdot x \le d^\perp \cdot a\}$ for a point $a$ of the edge, so the edge lies in $F_K(d^\perp)$. Conversely $F_K(d^\perp)$ is a segment of the boundary on the edge line; it contains no other edge, as two edges on one line with strictly increasing angles would have opposite directions and the region would then lie on both sides of the line, so it is that edge. So $g_K(\theta(c_t)) = c_t$. For every other angle $\theta$, $F_K(d^\perp)$ cannot contain an edge, so it is a single vertex and $g_K(\theta) = 0$. Hence $(\theta_t, g_t) = (\theta(c_t), c_t)$, and the edges close up, so their sum is $0$.

**Step 4 (lines L1 and L2).** After line L1, $P[0]$ is the lowest vertex of $P$. The vertices of $P$ in counterclockwise order are the boundary with possibly some straight or repeated vertices added, so the nonzero vectors $E$ are the vectors $c_t$ of Step 3, each split into one or more pieces with its direction. So the angles in $E$ never decrease, and for each angle $\theta$ the vectors in $E$ with angle $\theta$ are consecutive and sum to $g_P(\theta)$. The same holds for $F$ and $Q$. (For a point, $E$ is empty. For a segment $z, w$, possibly with points inside it, $E$ consists of pieces of $w - z$ followed by pieces of $z - w$.)

**Step 5 (the merge, and termination).** Each iteration of lines L4 to L7 takes the first unused vector of $E$ or of $F$ and increases $i$ or $j$ by one, so the loop runs exactly $|E| + |F|$ times. It takes from $E$ when $F$ is used up or when $\theta(E[i]) \le \theta(F[j])$, and from $F$ otherwise. As both lists are sorted by angle, this is the merge of two sorted lists: the vectors are taken in an order in which the angle never decreases, so all vectors with one angle $\theta$ are taken consecutively. Line L7 adds a vector to the last element of $D$ exactly when it has the same angle as that element. So at the end, $D$ has one element for each angle $\theta$ for which $E$ or $F$ has a vector, in increasing order of $\theta$, and that element is the sum of those vectors, which by Step 4 is $g_P(\theta) + g_Q(\theta) = g_{P \oplus Q}(\theta)$ by Step 2. Both $g_P(\theta)$ and $g_Q(\theta)$ are nonnegative multiples of the unit vector of $\theta$, so the sum is nonzero exactly when one of them is. Hence $D$ is the list $g_1, \ldots, g_k$ of Step 3 for $K = P \oplus Q$.

**Step 6 (line L8).** By Step 2 the lowest point of $P \oplus Q$ is $P[0] + Q[0]$. Line L8 starts there and adds $g_1, \ldots, g_{k-1}$. By Step 3 the points produced are the boundary of $P \oplus Q$ in counterclockwise order from the lowest point, with no straight vertices. The omitted last vector $g_k$ leads back to the start, since $g_1 + \cdots + g_k = 0$. If $k = 0$ the sum is a point and $R$ is that point; if $k = 2$ the sum is a segment and $R$ is its two endpoints. $\blacksquare$
