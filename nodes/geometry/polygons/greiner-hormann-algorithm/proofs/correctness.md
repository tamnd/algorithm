---
title: The Greiner-Hormann walk traces the boundary of the intersection, union or difference
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

We use the pseudocode of the page. $S$ and $C$ are simple polygons. For a polygon $P$ we write $\partial P$ for its boundary and $\operatorname{int} P$ for its interior. By the [Jordan curve theorem](/jordan-curve-theorem/), every point of the plane is in $\operatorname{int} P$, on $\partial P$ or in the unbounded exterior, and a connected set that misses $\partial P$ lies entirely inside or entirely outside $P$. The result $R$ is the closed region $S \cap C$, $S \cup C$ or the closure of $S \setminus C$.

**Claim.** If no vertex of either polygon lies on the boundary of the other, the algorithm terminates and returns rings such that a point not on any ring lies in $R$ exactly when it is inside an odd number of rings. Otherwise line L1 reports a degenerate input.

**Step 1 (line L1 finds exactly the crossings, or reports a touch).** Two closed segments $ab$ and $pq$ with $d = (b - a) \times (q - p) \ne 0$ meet at most at one point, $a + \alpha (b - a) = p + \beta (q - p)$, with the parameters of the page. That point is on both segments exactly when $\alpha, \beta \in [0, 1]$. If $\alpha$ or $\beta$ is $0$ or $1$, a vertex of one polygon lies on an edge of the other. If $d = 0$, the segments are parallel, and they share a point only when they are collinear and their projections overlap. Then an endpoint of one lies on the other. Conversely, if a vertex lies on an edge of the other polygon, one of these cases occurs for an edge at that vertex. So line L1 rejects exactly the degenerate inputs. Otherwise every common point of $\partial S$ and $\partial C$ is a *crossing*: a single point interior to an edge of each polygon, where the edges are not parallel. Because the edges of a simple polygon have disjoint interiors, each crossing is found exactly once, for one pair of edges. From now on we assume the input is not degenerate, and we let $k$ be the number of crossings.

**Step 2 (the case $k = 0$, line L2).** Then $\partial S$ and $\partial C$ are disjoint. The connected curve $\partial S$ lies entirely inside or entirely outside $C$, and the first vertex of $S$ decides which. If $\partial S$ is inside $C$, then $S \subseteq C$, because $C$ contains every bounded component of the complement of $\partial S$ that it surrounds. The same holds with the roles exchanged, and the two cases cannot both happen. If neither happens, the interiors are disjoint. In the six resulting cases, the rings returned by line L2 are $S$ or $C$ for the intersection; $C$, $S$ or both for the union; and nothing, both, or $S$ for the difference. In each case the even-odd rule gives exactly $R$. In particular, $S \setminus C$ with $C$ inside $S$ is $S$ with a hole $C$. From now on $k \ge 1$.

**Step 3 (line L3 builds the boundaries).** Each crossing on an edge of $S$ is inserted after the edge's first vertex, in increasing order of its parameter, which is the order along the edge. So the list of $S$ is the cyclic sequence of the vertices of $S$ and the crossings in the order of $\partial S$. The same holds for $C$. The two nodes of a crossing are neighbors of each other.

**Step 4 (arcs and the flags of line L4).** The crossings cut $\partial S$ into $k$ *arcs*, each running from one crossing to the next in the forward direction. An open arc misses $\partial C$, so by the Jordan curve theorem it lies entirely inside or entirely outside $C$. At a crossing $x$, the boundary $\partial C$ is locally a straight segment that $\partial S$ crosses at a positive angle, so the arcs of $\partial S$ before and after $x$ lie on opposite sides of $\partial C$. Hence the arcs alternate between inside and outside $C$, and $k$ is even. The first vertex of $S$ is not on $\partial C$, so line L4 knows the status of the arc through it. Walking forward, every crossing switches the status. So after line L4, $\operatorname{entry}(x)$ holds exactly when the arc that starts at $x$ lies inside $C$, and the arc that ends at $x$ lies outside. The same holds for $C$ and $S$ exchanged.

**Step 5 (the chosen arc of a node).** For a crossing node $x$ on $\partial P$, call its *chosen arc* the arc after $x$ if $\operatorname{entry}(x)$ holds and the arc before $x$ otherwise. This is the arc that lines L8 and L9 walk. By Step 4, after line L4 the chosen arc of every node is the arc at $x$ that lies inside the other polygon. Negating all flags of $P$ in line L5 makes it the arc outside the other polygon. So after line L5 the chosen arcs are as follows.

- Intersection: the arcs of $\partial S$ inside $C$ and the arcs of $\partial C$ inside $S$.
- Union: the arcs of $\partial S$ outside $C$ and the arcs of $\partial C$ outside $S$.
- Difference: the arcs of $\partial S$ outside $C$ and the arcs of $\partial C$ inside $S$.

Call these arcs the *selected arcs* $A$. At each crossing, exactly one of the two arcs of $\partial S$ and exactly one of the two arcs of $\partial C$ is selected, because the two arcs of the same boundary at a crossing lie on opposite sides of the other boundary.

**Step 6 (the selected arcs form the boundary of $R$).** Let $p$ be a point of an open arc of $\partial S$, so $p \notin \partial C$, and take a small disk $D$ around $p$ that misses $\partial C$. If $p \in \operatorname{int} C$, then $R \cap D$ equals $S \cap D$ for the intersection, and $D \subseteq R$ for the union. For the difference, $R \cap D$ is empty. If $p$ is outside $C$, then $R \cap D$ is empty for the intersection, and equals $S \cap D$ for the union and the difference. So $p \in \partial R$ exactly when the arc of $p$ is selected, and then $R$ lies locally on exactly one side of the arc. The same argument works for the arcs of $\partial C$, with the roles of the polygons as in the list of Step 5. The crossings are limits of selected arcs. Hence $\partial R$ is the union of the selected arcs, and crossing a selected arc away from the crossings moves a point into or out of $R$.

**Step 7 (one ring is one cycle).** Consider the graph whose vertices are the crossings and whose edges are the selected arcs. By Step 5 every crossing has degree two in it: one selected arc of $\partial S$ and one of $\partial C$. So the graph is a disjoint union of cycles, and along each cycle the arcs alternate between the two boundaries. Start the walk at the $S$ node of a crossing $x$. Line L8 or L9 follows the chosen arc of $x$, which is the selected $S$-arc at $x$, appending its vertices up to its other end $y$. Line L10 moves to the $C$ node of $y$, whose chosen arc is the selected $C$-arc at $y$, the other edge of the cycle at $y$. Continuing, the walk follows the cycle through $x$ and marks each crossing on it visited, both nodes at once in line L7. In a cycle the first crossing that is reached a second time is the start, so line L11 stops exactly when the walk returns to $x$. The ring is the cycle through $x$, listed in order, once.

**Step 8 (all cycles, line L6).** Every crossing lies on exactly one cycle, and every cycle contains a crossing. Line L6 tries every crossing node of $S$ and skips the visited ones, so it starts the walk on each cycle exactly once. The rings are therefore the cycles of Step 7. They are simple closed curves, since distinct arcs share only crossings and each crossing lies on one cycle. Different rings are disjoint.

**Step 9 (the even-odd rule gives $R$).** Let $p$ be a point on no ring, and take a ray from $p$ that avoids every vertex and crossing. It crosses the rings finitely many times, each time transversally at an interior point of a selected arc. By Step 6, each such crossing moves the ray into or out of $R$. Far away the ray is outside $R$, which is bounded. So $p \in R$ exactly when the total number of crossings is odd. That number is the sum over the rings of the crossings with each ring. The crossings with one ring are odd exactly when $p$ is inside that ring. So $p \in R$ exactly when $p$ lies inside an odd number of rings.

**Step 10 (termination and time).** Line L1 runs $nm$ times. Line L3 inserts $2k$ nodes. Line L4 walks each list once. In lines L6 to L11, each step of lines L8 and L9 moves along a selected arc, and by Step 8 each selected arc is walked exactly once. So the inner loops take $O(n + m + k)$ steps in total, and the outer loop of line L6 runs $k$ times. Every loop is bounded, so the algorithm terminates. $\blacksquare$
