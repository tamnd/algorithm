---
title: Bentley-Ottmann reports every point that lies on two or more segments
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

**Claim.** Let $S$ be a finite list of $n$ closed segments with rational end points, with no restriction (vertical segments, segments of length zero, shared end points, several segments through one point and collinear overlaps are allowed). Let $X$ be the finite set of all end points of segments together with every point $x$ such that some pair of segments meets exactly in $\{x\}$. Let $R = \{p \in X : p \text{ lies on at least two segments}\}$. Then BENTLEY-OTTMANN$(S)$ processes exactly the points of $X$, each once and in increasing lexicographic order, and it reports exactly the points of $R$, each with the set of all segments that contain it.

**Notation.** As on the algorithm page: $p < q$ is the lexicographic order, $l(s) \le r(s)$ are the end points of $s$, $U(p)$ is the set of non-degenerate segments with $l(s) = p$, and $T$ is the status. For an event $p$, the *block* is $T[i \ldots j)$ of line L5, and $N$ is the sequence of line L9.

**Step 1: reduction to a sweep without vertical segments.** For $\delta > 0$ let $\sigma_\delta(x, y) = (x + \delta y, y)$, a shear of determinant 1. Choose $\delta$ so small that the first coordinate of $\sigma_\delta$ is injective on $X$ and orders $X$ lexicographically. This is possible because $X$ is finite: for two points with $p_x < q_x$ we need $\delta |p_y - q_y| < q_x - p_x$, and for $p_x = q_x$, $p_y < q_y$ the first coordinates differ by $\delta (q_y - p_y) > 0$. The shear maps segments to segments and keeps all incidences, so $X$ and $R$ are mapped to the corresponding sets of the image. It keeps every value of orient, because its determinant is 1. A forward direction $u$ (one with $u_x > 0$, or $u_x = 0$ and $u_y > 0$) is mapped to a direction with a positive first coordinate when $\delta$ is small, and for two forward directions the sign of $u \times w$ does not change, so the order of line L9 by slope (vertical last) is the order by slope in the image. So the algorithm makes the same decisions on $S$ and on $\sigma_\delta(S)$. In the image, no segment of positive length is vertical, and the points of $X$ have distinct $x$-coordinates. From now on we work in the image and write $x$ and $y$ for its coordinates.

**Step 2: the order between events.** For a real $c$ that is not the $x$-coordinate of a point of $X$, let $T(c)$ be the sequence of segments of positive length that cross the line $x = c$, sorted by the $y$-coordinate $y_t(c)$ of the crossing, with ties broken by index. Two segments with $y_s(c) = y_t(c)$ share a point that is not in $X$, so they do not meet in a single point: they overlap, and then they have the same $y$ on all of their common $x$-range. Two segments of $T(c)$ cannot change their relative order on an interval of $c$ that contains no $x$-coordinate of a point of $X$: by the intermediate value theorem, a change would give a common point, which is either a single crossing (in $X$) or part of an overlap (where they tie for all such $c$). So $T(c)$ is the same sequence for all $c$ between two consecutive points of $X$.

**Invariant.** Let $x_1 < x_2 < \cdots < x_m$ be the points of $X$. We prove by induction on $k$ that the $k$-th iteration of the loop of line L3 processes $x_k$, and that after it:

- (A) $T$ is the sequence $T(c)$ for $c$ between $x_k$ and $x_{k+1}$ (for $k = m$, $T$ is empty);
- (B) the queue holds every end point greater than $x_k$, it holds only points of $X$, and it holds no point twice.

Before the first iteration, $T$ is empty, which is $T(c)$ for $c$ to the left of all points, and the queue holds the end points.

**The next event is in the queue.** Assume (A) and (B) after iteration $k$, and let $q = x_{k+1}$. We show that $q$ is in the queue. If $q$ is an end point, (B) gives this. Otherwise $q$ is the single common point of some pair, and it is not an end point. Let $A$ be the set of segments that contain $q$. Each has $q$ in its relative interior, so for $c$ just left of $q_x$ all of $A$ is in $T(c)$, and $y_t(c) \to q_y$ as $c \to q_x$ for $t \in A$. A segment $w \notin A$ in $T(c)$ has $y_w(q_x) \ne q_y$ (it is defined at $q_x$ because its right end point is in $X$, hence at or after $q$, and not at $q$), so for $c$ close to $q_x$ it is not between two members of $A$. Hence $A$ is a block of consecutive segments of $T$. Inside the block the segments are sorted by $y_t(c) = q_y - \mathrm{slope}(t)(q_x - c)$, that is by decreasing slope. Since some pair of $A$ meets only at $q$, $A$ holds two different slopes, so two consecutive members $s, t$ of the block have different slopes, and then $s \cap t = \{q\}$.

The segments $s$ and $t$ are adjacent in $T$ after iteration $k$. Let $x_j$, $j \le k$, be the last iteration at which they became adjacent. At an iteration for $p$, the pairs of neighbors that are new are the pair across the place of the removed block when $N$ is empty, the two pairs at the ends of $N$, and pairs inside $N$. Two members of $N$ both contain $p$, so they meet only at $p$ or they overlap; they have no single common point after $p$, so $s, t$ is not such a pair. So $s, t$ is one of the pairs tested by line L11 or L12, and line L13 found their single common point $q > x_j$. It is put into the queue unless it was seen before, and a point that was seen before and is greater than the current event is still in the queue because every popped point is at most $x_k < q$. So $q$ is in the queue.

**Iteration $k + 1$.** The queue holds only points of $X$ (by (B)), all of them greater than $x_k$, and it holds $q = x_{k+1}$, the smallest point of $X$ after $x_k$. So line L4 pops $p = q$.

*Line L5 finds the segments through $p$.* By (A), $T = T(c)$ for $c$ just left of $p_x$. Each $t \in T$ is defined at $p_x$ (its right end point is in $X$, so it is at or after $p$), and by continuity $y_t(p_x)$ does not decrease along $T$. By the formula $\mathrm{orient}(l(t), r(t), p) = (r(t)_x - l(t)_x)(p_y - y_t(p_x))$, the value $\mathrm{side}(t) = -\mathrm{orient}(l(t), r(t), p)$ is the sign of $y_t(p_x) - p_y$, so it does not decrease along $T$. The two binary searches therefore return the block of the segments $t \in T$ with $y_t(p_x) = p_y$, that is, the segments of $T$ that contain $p$.

*Lines L6 and L7 report the right set.* A segment that contains $p$ is a degenerate segment at $p$, or has $l(s) = p$ and is in $U(p)$, or has $l(s) < p \le r(s)$; in the last case it crosses $x = c$ for $c$ just left of $p_x$, so it is in $T$ and in the block. Conversely every segment of these three sets contains $p$. So the set of line L6 is exactly the set of segments through $p$, and line L7 reports $p$ exactly when $p \in R$.

*Lines L8 to L10 restore (A).* For $c$ just right of $p_x$, $T(c)$ holds the segments of $T$ that do not end at $p$, plus $U(p)$. A segment of $T$ outside the block has $y_t(p_x) \ne p_y$; it keeps its position relative to the other segments outside the block, and it is below all segments through $p$ when $y_t(p_x) < p_y$ and above them otherwise, for $c$ close to $p_x$. The segments through $p$ that go on to the right of $p$ are ordered, for $c$ just right of $p_x$, by $y = p_y + \mathrm{slope} \cdot (c - p_x)$, that is by increasing slope, with ties among overlapping segments broken by index. This is the sequence $N$ of line L9, and line L10 puts it in the place of the old block. So after line L10, $T = T(c)$ for $c$ between $x_{k+1}$ and $x_{k+2}$.

*Line L13 keeps (B).* It inserts only a single common point of two segments, which is in $X$, only when it is greater than $p$, and only once, because of the set of seen points.

**Conclusion.** By induction, the loop processes $x_1, \ldots, x_m$ in this order and then stops, because the queue holds only points of $X$ greater than the last event. Each point of $R$ is reported once, with the set of segments through it, and no other point is reported. Mapping back by $\sigma_\delta^{-1}$ gives the claim for $S$.

**Termination.** $|X| \le 2n + \binom{n}{2}$, each point enters the queue at most once, and each iteration does a finite amount of work. $\blacksquare$
