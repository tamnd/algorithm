---
title: When the straight, visibility and stochastic walks end, and why they answer correctly
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

**Setting.** $K$ is a convex polygon, tiled edge to edge by $T$ triangles with counterclockwise corners: two triangles meet in a common edge, a common vertex, or not at all. An edge $ab$ of a triangle $t$, in counterclockwise order, *faces* $q$ if $\mathrm{orient}(a, b, q) < 0$. A triangle edge is *interior* if two triangles share it, and *boundary* otherwise. The circumcircle of $t$ has center $o_t$ and radius $r_t$, and $\pi_t(x) = |x - o_t|^2 - r_t^2$ is the power of $x$ with respect to it. An interior edge $uw$ with triangles $uwc$ and $wud$ is *locally Delaunay* if $d$ is not strictly inside the circumcircle of $uwc$, that is, $\mathrm{incircle}(u, w, c, d) \le 0$.

**Claim.** (1) The straight walk ends after at most $T$ steps, with the correct answer. (2) Whenever the visibility walk ends, its answer is correct. (3) If every interior edge is locally Delaunay, the visibility walk, with any rule for choosing among the facing edges, never visits a triangle twice, so it ends after at most $T$ steps. (4) The stochastic walk ends with probability $1$, and its expected number of steps is at most $T \cdot 3^T$. (5) In the pinwheel of the page, the deterministic visibility walk from triangle 1 to $q = (9, 2)$ never ends.

**Step 1 (the answers).** (a) No edge of $t$ faces $q$ if and only if $q$ is in the closed triangle $t$, by the definition of the closed triangle. Then the answer is correct by the argument of the [Kirkpatrick correctness proof](/kirkpatrick-point-location/proofs/correctness/), Step 6: a corner equal to $q$ gives the vertex; otherwise a zero orientation puts $q$ in the relative interior of that edge, which is the only edge with this property; otherwise $q$ is in the open triangle $t$, which is the only triangle whose interior contains $q$. (b) If a boundary edge $ab$ of $t$ faces $q$, then $q \notin K$. Indeed, suppose a point $y \in K$ has $\mathrm{orient}(a, b, y) < 0$. Let $m$ be the midpoint of $ab$ and $z$ a point inside $t$ near $m$. By convexity, the segment from $z$ to $y$ lies in $K$, and as $z \to m$, it contains points of $K$ arbitrarily close to $m$ strictly on the right of $ab$. But near $m$, the right side of $ab$ is covered by no triangle: a triangle that contains points arbitrarily close to $m$ contains $m$, so it has $ab$ as an edge (edge-to-edge tiling), and the only such triangle is $t$, which is on the left. So $y$ cannot exist, and $q \notin K$. Parts (a) and (b) prove (2), since the visibility walk ends only in lines L8 and L9.

**Step 2 (the moved segment).** Let $s$ be the centroid of the start triangle $t_0$, $d = q - s \ne 0$ (the case $q \in t_0$ ends in line L1), and $\nu = (d_y, -d_x)$, which points to the right of $d$. For $\varepsilon > 0$ let $s_\varepsilon = s + \varepsilon \nu$ and $q_\varepsilon = q + \varepsilon \nu$, and let $\ell_\varepsilon$ be the line through them. For every point $p$, with $\times$ the cross product,

$$d \times (p - s_\varepsilon) = d \times (p - s) - \varepsilon\, d \times \nu = \mathrm{orient}(s, q, p) + \varepsilon |d|^2,$$

using $d \times \nu = -|d|^2$, where $\mathrm{orient}$ here is the signed determinant. So a vertex with $\mathrm{orient}(s, q, p) = 0$ lies strictly to the left of $\ell_\varepsilon$, and for $\varepsilon$ below some $\varepsilon_0 > 0$ the other vertices keep their sides. Hence, for $0 < \varepsilon < \varepsilon_0$, the side function of the code is the exact side with respect to $\ell_\varepsilon$, and no vertex lies on $\ell_\varepsilon$. Make $\varepsilon_0$ also so small that $s_\varepsilon$ is inside $t_0$, and that $q_\varepsilon$ is outside every closed triangle that does not contain $q$ (these are finitely many closed sets that miss $q$).

**Step 3 (the straight walk follows the moved segment, 1).** Fix $0 < \varepsilon < \varepsilon_0$ and let $\sigma = s_\varepsilon q_\varepsilon$. For a counterclockwise edge $uw$ with $u$ strictly right and $w$ strictly left of $\ell_\varepsilon$, the line crosses $uw$ at a point $x$ that is not a vertex, and $(w - u) \times d = -(d \times (w - s_\varepsilon) - d \times (u - s_\varepsilon)) < 0$, so the direction $d$ points to the right of $uw$, out of the triangle on its left. So $\sigma$, moving forward, leaves that triangle through $x$. Now line L2: the corners of $t_0$ are not all on one side of $\ell_\varepsilon$, since $s_\varepsilon$ is inside $t_0$, so exactly one counterclockwise edge goes from right to left, and $\sigma$ leaves $t_0$ there, before $q_\varepsilon$, which is outside $t_0$. Lines L4 to L6: suppose $\sigma$ enters the triangle $t = (w, u, v)$ through the edge $wu$, with $u$ right and $w$ left. If $q \in t$, the walk stops correctly by Step 1. Otherwise $q_\varepsilon \notin t$, so $\sigma$ leaves the convex set $t$ at a second boundary point, on the edge whose ends are on opposite sides: $uv$ if $v$ is left, and $vw$ if $v$ is right, which is the edge chosen by line L6, again in the order (right end, left end). So the walk visits the triangles met by $\sigma$ in the order of $\sigma$, until it stops. A segment meets a convex triangle in one interval, so no triangle is visited twice, and the walk takes at most $T$ steps.

It remains to treat the return in line L4. The segment leaves $K$ through a boundary edge at a point before $q_\varepsilon$. Since $K \cap \sigma$ is an interval that starts at $s_\varepsilon \in K$, $q_\varepsilon \notin K$. Suppose that $q \in K$ nonetheless. The triangles $t_0, \ldots, t_m$ visited are the same for all $\varepsilon < \varepsilon_0$, since the walk depends only on the signs, and none of them contains $q$ in its closure, so their union $U$ is a closed set at a distance $\delta > 0$ from $q$. Let $y$ be the point of the segment $sq$ at distance $\delta / 2$ from $q$. Since $s$ is interior to $K$ and $q \in K$, $y$ is interior to $K$, so for small $\varepsilon$ the point $y + \varepsilon \nu$, which lies on $\sigma$, is in $K$, hence in $U$, and at distance less than $\delta$ from $q$. This contradicts the choice of $\delta$. So $q \notin K$, and "outside" is correct. This proves (1).

**Step 4 (power decreases across a Delaunay edge).** Let $t = uwc$ and $t' = wud$ share the locally Delaunay edge $uw$, and let $q$ be strictly on the side of $d$, so that $uw$ faces $q$ in $t$. Then $\pi_{t'}(q) \le \pi_t(q)$, with equality only if the two circumcircles are the same. Proof: $f = \pi_t - \pi_{t'}$ is affine, since the quadratic terms cancel: $f(x) = 2 x \cdot (o_{t'} - o_t) + \mathrm{const}$. Both circles pass through $u$ and $w$, so $f(u) = f(w) = 0$. If $o_t = o_{t'}$, the radii are equal too, the circles are the same, and $f = 0$. Otherwise $f$ is a nonconstant affine function that vanishes exactly on the line $uw$. Since $d$ is on the circle of $t'$ and not strictly inside the circle of $t$, $f(d) = \pi_t(d) \ge 0$, and $d$ is not on the line $uw$, so $f(d) > 0$. So $f > 0$ on the whole open side of $d$, which contains $q$.

**Step 5 (no cycle in a Delaunay triangulation, 3).** Suppose the visibility walk visits $t_0, t_1, \ldots, t_k = t_0$ with $k \ge 1$, each step crossing an interior edge that faces $q$. By Step 4, $\pi_{t_0}(q) \ge \pi_{t_1}(q) \ge \cdots \ge \pi_{t_k}(q) = \pi_{t_0}(q)$, so all are equal, and by Step 4 consecutive triangles have the same circumcircle. So all $t_i$ have one circle $C$. Let $e = uw$ be the edge from $t_0$ to $t_1$; $q$ is strictly on the side $B$ of the line of $e$ that contains $t_1$, and $t_0$ is on the side $A$. Every triangle with all its corners on $C$ lies in one closed side of the line of $e$: if two of its corners were strictly on opposite sides, the edge between them would cross the line of $e$ at a point strictly inside the disk, which is the relative interior of the chord $e$, and two triangle edges would cross. The walk goes from $t_1$ (side $B$) back to $t_0$ (side $A$), so some step goes from a triangle $t_i$ on side $B$ to a triangle $t_{i+1}$ on side $A$, across a shared edge $f$. Then $f$ lies in both closed sides, so on the line of $e$, and since $f$ is a chord of $C$ on that line, $f = e$. The only triangles with edge $e$ are $t_0$ and $t_1$, so this step goes from $t_1$ to $t_0$ across $e$, which needs $q$ strictly on side $A$. This contradicts $q$ on side $B$. So the walk never returns to a triangle. It moves to a new triangle at each step, so it ends after at most $T - 1$ moves between triangles and at most one more step out of $K$. This proves (3), and covers cocircular points.

**Step 6 (the stochastic walk, 4).** Let $t$ be a triangle where the walk does not stop, so $q \notin t$. Choose a point $p$ inside $t$ that is on no line through $q$ and a vertex other than $q$; there are finitely many such lines, and the interior of $t$ is open. Follow the segment $pq$ from $p$, as in Step 3 with no perturbation needed: it passes through no vertex except possibly $q$, so it leaves each triangle it enters through the relative interior of an edge at a point $x \ne q$, unless the triangle contains $q$. At such an exit, the line of the edge meets the line $pq$ only at $x$, and $q$ comes after $x$, so $q$ is strictly beyond the edge: the edge faces $q$. So the triangles met by $pq$ give a path of at most $T$ visibility steps from $t$ that ends with a stop in line L8 or a step out of $K$ in line L9. At each triangle, the stochastic walk tests first the facing edge of this path with probability at least $1/3$, independently of the past, and then takes it. So from any triangle the walk ends within $T$ steps with probability at least $3^{-T}$. By the Markov property, it has not ended after $jT$ steps with probability at most $(1 - 3^{-T})^j$, which tends to $0$, and the expected number of steps is at most $\sum_{j \ge 0} T (1 - 3^{-T})^j = T \cdot 3^T$. This proves (4).

**Step 7 (the pinwheel, 5).** For $q = (9, 2)$, the three orientations $\mathrm{orient}(t[k], t[k+1], q)$ for $k = 0, 1, 2$ of the stored triangles are: triangle 1: $3, -17, 42$; triangle 2: $-1, 17, -11$; triangle 3: $1, -15, 28$; triangle 4: $-5, 15, -5$; triangle 5: $5, -33, 126$; triangle 6: $-3, 33, -14$. The deterministic walk takes the first negative one. In triangle 1 it is edge 1, $(5,7)(14,0)$, shared with triangle 2; in triangle 2 edge 0, $(12,1)(14,0)$, shared with triangle 3; in triangle 3 edge 1, $(12,1)(0,0)$, shared with triangle 4; in triangle 4 edge 0, $(7,1)(0,0)$, shared with triangle 5; in triangle 5 edge 1, $(7,1)(0,14)$, shared with triangle 6; in triangle 6 edge 0, $(5,7)(0,14)$, shared with triangle 1. The walk is a function of the current triangle only, so it repeats the cycle $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$ forever. By Step 5, some interior edge is not locally Delaunay, and indeed $\mathrm{incircle}((12,1), (0,0), (14,0), (7,1)) = 350 > 0$ for the edge $(0,0)(12,1)$.
