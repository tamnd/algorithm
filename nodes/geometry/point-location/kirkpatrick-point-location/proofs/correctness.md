---
title: Kirkpatrick's hierarchy is built correctly and answers every query
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

**Setting.** $ABC$ is a triangle with counterclockwise corners. A *triangulation of $ABC$* is a finite set of triangles, each with three non-collinear vertices, whose union is the closed triangle $ABC$, such that any two of them meet in a common edge, a common vertex, or not at all, and such that every vertex other than $A$, $B$, $C$ lies strictly inside $ABC$. $T_0$ is the input, and $T_{r+1}$ is the set of triangles after round $r + 1$ of KIRKPATRICK-BUILD. For an inner vertex $v$, the *star* of $v$ is the set of triangles with corner $v$, and $\deg(v)$ is their number. A closed triangle contains $q$ if the three orientation tests of $q$ against its edges are at least $0$.

**Claim.** (1) Every $T_r$ is a triangulation of $ABC$ whose vertex set is that of $T_0$ minus the removed vertices. (2) The build terminates, and its last level is $\{ABC\}$. (3) For every point $q$, KIRKPATRICK-QUERY returns "outside" if $q \notin ABC$; otherwise the vertex $q$ if $q$ is a vertex; otherwise the edge of $T_0$ that contains $q$ in its relative interior, if there is one; otherwise the index of the triangle of $T_0$ whose interior contains $q$.

**Step 1 (the link of an inner vertex is a simple polygon).** Let $v$ be an inner vertex of a triangulation $T$. A small disk around $v$ lies in $ABC$, and it meets only triangles of the star of $v$, which cover it, since a triangle that does not have $v$ as a corner and contains points arbitrarily close to $v$ would contain $v$ in an edge or its interior, which edge-to-edge tiling forbids. So the angles of the star triangles at $v$ tile the full angle $2\pi$. Each angle is less than $\pi$, so $\deg(v) \ge 3$. Order the star triangles counterclockwise around $v$ as $v u_i u_{i+1}$ for $i = 0, \ldots, d - 1$ with indices mod $d$. Two consecutive triangles share the edge $v u_{i+1}$. The rays from $v$ through $u_0, \ldots, u_{d-1}$ appear in strictly increasing angle and wind once around $v$, and each edge $u_i u_{i+1}$ lies in the open angle between two consecutive rays, which is less than $\pi$. Two edges in disjoint open angles do not meet, and two consecutive edges meet only at their shared end point, so the link polygon $u_0 u_1 \cdots u_{d-1}$ is simple, counterclockwise, and its closed region is the union of the star. This is the map `nxt` in the code: the triangle $v u w$ in counterclockwise order gives the link edge from $u$ to $w$.

**Step 2 (ear clipping triangulates the hole).** Let $Q$ be a simple counterclockwise polygon with $m \ge 3$ vertices, where three consecutive vertices may be collinear. Call $b$, with neighbors $a$ and $c$, an *ear* if $\mathrm{orient}(a, b, c) > 0$ and no other vertex of $Q$ lies in the closed triangle $abc$.

(a) If $m \ge 4$, an ear exists. A *diagonal* is a segment between two vertices whose relative interior lies in the open interior of $Q$. First, $Q$ has a diagonal. Let $b$ be the lexicographically smallest vertex, with neighbors $a$ and $c$. Both are lexicographically larger, so they are not on opposite sides of $b$ on a line through $b$, and they are not on the same ray from $b$, or the edges $ba$ and $bc$ would overlap. So $\mathrm{orient}(a, b, c) \ne 0$, and since $Q$ is counterclockwise and $b$ is extreme, $\mathrm{orient}(a, b, c) > 0$. If no vertex other than $a, b, c$ lies in the closed triangle $abc$, then no edge of $Q$ meets the relative interior of $ac$: such an edge would cross into the open triangle through $ac$ and would have to leave it again through $ab$ or $bc$, which are edges of $Q$, or end inside it, which puts a vertex there; and $ac$ cannot overlap an edge, as then a vertex would lie on $ac$ or $m = 3$. Points of $ac$ near $a$ lie inside the angle $abc$ at $b$, which is inside $Q$ near $b$, so $ac$ is a diagonal. Otherwise, let $w$ be a vertex in the closed triangle $abc$, other than $a, b, c$, at the largest distance from the line $ac$. No vertex lies on the edges $ab$ or $bc$ except their end points, since $Q$ is simple, so every point of $bw$ other than $w$ is strictly farther from the line $ac$ than $w$, and no vertex lies there. The same crossing argument, in the triangle cut from $abc$ by the parallel to $ac$ through $w$, shows that no edge meets the relative interior of $bw$, so $bw$ is a diagonal. A diagonal splits $Q$ into two simple polygons with fewer vertices, and by induction on $m$, $Q$ has a triangulation by diagonals. Its triangles are non-degenerate: a triangle $xyz$ of it with collinear corners would have its longest side overlap a diagonal or an edge with the other two, so a vertex would lie in the relative interior of a diagonal or an edge. Its dual graph, with one node per triangle and one arc per diagonal, is a tree with $m - 2 \ge 2$ nodes, so it has a leaf. A leaf triangle has two sides on $Q$, which are consecutive edges $ab$ and $bc$, and the diagonal $ac$. It is non-degenerate and counterclockwise, so $\mathrm{orient}(a, b, c) > 0$. No vertex lies in its open interior, which is inside one triangle of the triangulation, nor in the relative interior of $ab$, $bc$ or the diagonal $ac$. So $b$ is an ear.

(b) Removing an ear $b$ leaves a simple polygon $Q'$ with $m - 1$ vertices: $ac$ is a diagonal by the argument of (a), and $Q$ is the union of $Q'$ and the triangle $abc$ with disjoint interiors. When $m = 3$, $Q$ is simple, so its three vertices are not collinear and it is a counterclockwise triangle. By induction, `ear_clip` returns $m - 2$ non-degenerate counterclockwise triangles with vertices among those of $Q$, whose union is the closed region of $Q$ and which meet edge to edge: each is an ear $abc$ with no other vertex in it, cut off along a diagonal.

**Step 3 (each level is a triangulation).** Assume $T_r$ is a triangulation of $ABC$, and let $I$ be the set picked in line L2. No two vertices of $I$ are adjacent, so no triangle has two corners in $I$, and the stars of the vertices of $I$ are pairwise disjoint sets of triangles. So, when the loop of line L3 reaches $v$, the star of $v$ is the same as at the start of the round, and the earlier holes have not touched it. By Step 1 the union of the star is the region of a simple polygon $Q_v$ whose vertices are the neighbors of $v$. By Step 2, line L4 replaces the star by a triangulation of $Q_v$ that uses the same boundary edges and no vertex other than those of $Q_v$. The new triangles meet the triangles outside the star only along the boundary of $Q_v$, which is made of the same edges as before, so the tiling stays edge to edge. The corners are never picked, so they stay. Hence $T_{r+1}$ is a triangulation of $ABC$ whose vertices are those of $T_r$ minus $I$. This proves (1) by induction.

**Step 4 (termination).** While $T_r$ has more than one triangle, it has $N_r \ge 4$ vertices. By the count in [the complexity proof](../complexity/), at least $(N_r - 3)/2 > 0$ inner vertices have degree at most $8$. If $I$ were empty, no vertex would be blocked, and the scan would pick the first of them, so $I \ne \emptyset$. So $N_{r+1} < N_r$, and the loop of line L1 runs at most $n - 3$ times. A triangulation of $ABC$ with $3$ vertices is $\{ABC\}$, so the top triangle is $ABC$. Each round does finitely many steps, because ear clipping removes one vertex per step and raises an error only for non-simple polygons, which Step 1 excludes. This proves (2).

**Step 5 (children cover their parent).** Let $t$ be a new triangle of line L4 in the hole $Q_v$, and $C(t)$ the set of deleted triangles of the star of $v$ whose interiors meet the interior of $t$. The interior of $t$ is inside $Q_v$, which is the union of the star. A star triangle $s \notin C(t)$ meets $t$ in a convex set with empty interior, so in a point or a segment. So the interior of $t$, minus finitely many segments, is covered by the triangles of $C(t)$. The union of $C(t)$ is closed and contains a dense subset of the closed triangle $t$, so it contains $t$. Also, $C(t) \ne \emptyset$, since $t$ has positive area and the star triangles cover it. The test `interiors_overlap` is correct: two convex polygons have disjoint interiors if and only if a line separates them, and that line can be chosen through an edge of one of them; so the interiors of the counterclockwise triangles $s$ and $t$ are disjoint if and only if, for some edge $ab$ of one of them, every corner $p$ of the other has $\mathrm{orient}(a, b, p) \le 0$.

**Step 6 (the query).** If $q$ is not in the closed triangle $ABC$, line L6 returns "outside", which is correct by Step 4. Otherwise, the invariant of the loop of lines L7 and L8 is "$q$ is in the closed triangle $t$". It holds after line L6. If it holds and $t$ has children, they cover $t$ by Step 5, so some child contains $q$ and line L8 finds it. A triangle has children if and only if it was made in line L4, and its children belong to the level just before it was made, so each step goes down at least one level, and the loop ends after at most $R$ steps at a triangle $t$ with no children, which is a triangle of $T_0$ with its input index.

Now $q$ is in the closed input triangle $t$. If $q$ is a corner of $t$, it is the vertex $q$. Otherwise, if $\mathrm{orient}(a, b, q) = 0$ for an edge $ab$ of $t$, then $q$ is on the line of $ab$ and in the closed triangle, so on the segment $ab$, and not at its end points; this is an edge of $T_0$, and it is the only one that contains $q$ in its relative interior, since edges of an edge-to-edge tiling meet only at end points. Otherwise $q$ is in the open triangle $t$, and since open triangles of $T_0$ are disjoint, $t$ is the only input triangle whose interior contains $q$. The edge is reported with its end points in lexicographic order, so the answer does not depend on which closed triangle the descent reached. This proves (3).
