---
title: The layered range tree query reports exactly the points in the box in O(log n + k) time
proves: [correctness, termination, complexity]
status: full
---

**Setting.** The points of $P$, $n \ge 1$, are at positions $0, \dots, n - 1$ of the order of $(p_x, p_y, \text{index})$. The primary tree is the balanced leaf-based tree over these positions; $\beta(v)$ is the block of positions of node $v$. For position $j$ with point $p$, its key is $\kappa(j) = (p_y, p_x, j)$; keys are distinct. $A(v)$ lists $\beta(v)$ in increasing key order, and for an internal node, $\mathrm{left}(v)[i] = |\{\, r < i : A(v)[r] \in \beta(\mathrm{lc}(v)) \,\}|$ for $0 \le i \le |A(v)|$. The query box is $Q = [x_1, x_2] \times [y_1, y_2]$, closed, with $y_1 \le y_2$. The height is $H = \lceil \log_2 n \rceil$. For a node $v$ write $\sigma_v = $ the first index of $A(v)$ with $p_y \ge y_1$ and $\tau_v = $ the first index with $p_y > y_2$ (both $|A(v)|$ if there is none).

**Claim.** The query terminates, reports each point of $P \cap Q$ exactly once and no other point, does exactly two binary searches by $y$, and runs in $O(\log n + k)$ time.

**Step 1 (cascading lemma).** Let $v$ be internal, $c = \mathrm{lc}(v)$, and $0 \le i \le |A(v)|$. The first $i$ entries of $A(v)$ are the entries of $\beta(v)$ with key less than that of $A(v)[i]$ (all of $\beta(v)$ if $i = |A(v)|$), because $A(v)$ is sorted by distinct keys. Since $\beta(c) \subseteq \beta(v)$ and $A(c)$ is sorted by the same keys, the entries of $A(c)$ before index $\mathrm{left}(v)[i]$ are exactly the positions of $\beta(c)$ with key less than that of $A(v)[i]$. In the same way, with $\mathrm{rc}(v)$ and $i - \mathrm{left}(v)[i]$.

**Step 2 (cascading $\sigma$ and $\tau$).** We show $\sigma_c = \mathrm{left}(v)[\sigma_v]$ for $c = \mathrm{lc}(v)$. The entries of $A(v)$ before $\sigma_v$ are exactly those with $p_y < y_1$, since $A(v)$ is sorted by $p_y$ first. So by Step 1 the entries of $A(c)$ before $\mathrm{left}(v)[\sigma_v]$ are the positions of $\beta(c)$ with $p_y < y_1$, and $A(c)$ is sorted by $p_y$ first, so these form a prefix whose length is $\sigma_c$. The same argument with "$p_y \le y_2$" gives $\tau_c = \mathrm{left}(v)[\tau_v]$, and with the right child gives $\sigma_{\mathrm{rc}(v)} = \sigma_v - \mathrm{left}(v)[\sigma_v]$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{rc}(v)} = \tau_v - \mathrm{left}(v)[\tau_v]$.

**Step 3 (the invariant of the walk).** Line L8 sets $(s, t) = (\sigma_{v_s}, \tau_{v_s})$ at the split node $v_s$ by two binary searches. Lines L10, L12, L13 and L15 move from a node to a child and replace $(s, t)$ by the pair Step 2 gives for that child, computed from the counts of the parent before the update. By induction on the depth, whenever the walk is at a node $u$, $(s, t) = (\sigma_u, \tau_u)$. When line L12 or its mirror reports from the other child $w$ of $u$, it uses the pair Step 2 gives for $w$, so the slice is $A(w)[\sigma_w .. \tau_w - 1]$.

**Step 4 (the reported slices).** For any node $w$, $A(w)[\sigma_w .. \tau_w - 1]$ is exactly the set of positions of $\beta(w)$ whose point has $y_1 \le p_y \le y_2$, because $A(w)$ is sorted by $p_y$ first. In particular at a leaf it is its point if the point's $y$ is in range and nothing otherwise.

**Step 5 (which blocks are reported).** The walk visits the same nodes, and reports from the same nodes $w$ and end leaves, as the range tree query, since lines L6, L7, L11 and the branch tests of lines L12 and L13 are the same. By Steps 1 to 5 of the [range tree query proof](/range-tree-query/), these blocks $\beta(w)$ are disjoint and their union is the set of positions from $lo$ to $hi$, which are the points with $x_1 \le p_x \le x_2$. With Step 4, each point with $x_1 \le p_x \le x_2$ and $y_1 \le p_y \le y_2$ is reported exactly once, and no other point. If the split node is a leaf, line L9 reports the slice of Step 4 at that leaf.

**Step 6 (termination and time).** The descent to the split node and each of the two paths take at most $H$ steps, each step moving to a child. Line L6 and line L8 do two binary searches each, in arrays of at most $n$ entries, $O(\log n)$ time; no other line searches. Every other line costs $O(1)$ plus the length of the slices it reports, and each reported point is in exactly one slice. The total is $O(\log n + H + k) = O(\log n + k)$, and $O(\log n)$ for COUNT, which only adds the lengths $t - s$. $\blacksquare$
