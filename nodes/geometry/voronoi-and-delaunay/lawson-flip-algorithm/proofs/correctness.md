---
title: Lawson's flip algorithm terminates with a Delaunay triangulation
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

**Claim.** Let $S$ be a finite set of $n$ points in the plane, not all on one line, and let $T_0$ be a triangulation of $S$ that uses every point of $S$ as a vertex. Then LAWSON started at $T_0$ makes at most $n(n - 1)/2$ flips, terminates, and returns a triangulation of $S$ in which no point of $S$ lies strictly inside the circumcircle of any triangle. No general position is assumed: four or more points may lie on one circle and three or more on one line.

**Notation.** $H$ is the convex hull of $S$. For a triangle $T$ with vertices $u, v, w$ not on one line, $f_T$ is the affine function on the plane with $f_T(u) = |u|^2$, $f_T(v) = |v|^2$, $f_T(w) = |w|^2$. An interior edge $ab$ with triangles $abc$ and $bad$ is *locally Delaunay* when $\mathrm{incircle}(a, b, c, d) \le 0$, where $a, b, c$ is counterclockwise. For a triangulation $T$ of $S$, $F_T : H \to \mathbb{R}$ is the function that equals $f_\Delta$ on each triangle $\Delta$ of $T$.

**Lemma 1 (lifting).** Let $T$ have circumcenter $c$ and circumradius $\rho$. Then $f_T(q) = 2 c \cdot q + \rho^2 - |c|^2$, and $|q|^2 - f_T(q) = |q - c|^2 - \rho^2$. So $q$ is strictly inside the circumcircle of $T$ exactly when $|q|^2 < f_T(q)$, and on it exactly when $|q|^2 = f_T(q)$.

*Proof.* The function $q \mapsto 2 c \cdot q + \rho^2 - |c|^2$ is affine, and $|q|^2 - (2 c \cdot q + \rho^2 - |c|^2) = |q - c|^2 - \rho^2$, which is zero at the three vertices. An affine function is fixed by its values at three points not on one line, so it is $f_T$. $\square$

**Lemma 2 (pencil).** Let $\Delta_1$ and $\Delta_2$ be triangles with a common edge $e$ on the line $L$, with third vertices $w_1$ and $w_2$ on opposite sides of $L$. Then $f_{\Delta_2} - f_{\Delta_1}$ is affine and zero on $L$, and the following are equivalent: (i) $w_2$ is not strictly inside the circumcircle of $\Delta_1$; (ii) $f_{\Delta_2} \ge f_{\Delta_1}$ on the closed side of $L$ that holds $w_2$; (iii) $w_1$ is not strictly inside the circumcircle of $\Delta_2$. In the same way, the following are equivalent: (i') $w_2$ is strictly outside the circumcircle of $\Delta_1$; (ii') $f_{\Delta_2} > f_{\Delta_1}$ on the open side of $L$ that holds $w_2$; (iii') $w_1$ is strictly outside the circumcircle of $\Delta_2$.

*Proof.* Both functions equal $|x|^2$ at the two ends of $e$, so their difference is affine and zero at two points of $L$, hence on $L$. An affine function that is zero on $L$ has one sign on each open side of $L$ and opposite signs on the two sides, or is zero everywhere. At $w_2$ the difference is $|w_2|^2 - f_{\Delta_1}(w_2)$, so by Lemma 1 (i) says that it is $\ge 0$ at $w_2$, which is (ii). At $w_1$ the difference is $f_{\Delta_2}(w_1) - |w_1|^2$, and (iii) says that it is $\le 0$ at $w_1$, which by the sign rule is the same as (ii). The proof of the second list is the same, with strict signs. $\square$

So local Delaunayness of an edge does not depend on which of its two triangles is used, and LAWSON tests it with the triangle on the left of the directed edge.

**Lemma 3 (flips are legal).** Let $ab$ be an interior edge with counterclockwise triangles $abc$ and $bad$, and let $\mathrm{incircle}(a, b, c, d) > 0$. Then $a, d, b, c$ is a strictly convex quadrilateral in counterclockwise order, so $adc$ and $dbc$ are counterclockwise triangles with the same union, and the flip gives a triangulation of $S$.

*Proof.* The point $d$ is strictly inside the open disc $B$ of the circle through $a, b, c$, and $c$ is on its circle. Every point of the segment $cd$ other than $c$ is a strict convex combination of a point of the closed disc and a point of the open disc, so it is in $B$. As $c$ and $d$ are strictly on opposite sides of the line $ab$, the segment $cd$ meets the line $ab$ at a point $x \ne c$, so $x \in B$. The line $ab$ meets $B$ in the open segment between $a$ and $b$, so $x$ is strictly between $a$ and $b$. So the diagonals $ab$ and $cd$ cross at a point inside both, and the quadrilateral is strictly convex. Its order is $a, d, b, c$ counterclockwise because $abc$ is counterclockwise and $d$ is on the other side of $ab$. $\square$

**Lemma 4 (the lifted surface goes down).** In the setting of Lemma 3, let $T$ be the triangulation before the flip and $T'$ after it. Then $F_{T'} \le F_T$ on $H$, with equality outside the quadrilateral, and $F_{T'}(x) < F_T(x)$ for every point $x$ strictly between $a$ and $b$. The new edge $cd$ is locally Delaunay in $T'$, with strict inequality.

*Proof.* Write $Q$ for the quadrilateral. Outside $Q$ the triangles are the same. By Lemma 1, $f_{abc}(d) > |d|^2 = f_{adc}(d)$. The function $f_{abc} - f_{adc}$ is affine and zero on the line $ac$, which is a side of the convex $Q$, and positive at $d$; so it is positive on the open side of $ac$ that holds $d$, which also holds $b$. Then $f_{adc}(b) < f_{abc}(b) = |b|^2$, so by Lemma 1 $b$ is strictly outside the circumcircle of $adc$, and $cd$ is locally Delaunay with strict inequality. By Lemma 2 in its strict form, $f_{dbc} > f_{adc}$ on the open side of $cd$ that holds $b$ and $f_{dbc} < f_{adc}$ on the other side, so on $Q$ we have $F_{T'} = \max(f_{adc}, f_{dbc})$, which is a convex function on $Q$. A convex function is at most its affine interpolation on a triangle, so on the triangle $abc$, $F_{T'} \le f_{abc} = F_T$, since $F_{T'}$ and $f_{abc}$ agree at $a$, $b$ and $c$; the same holds on $bad$. Finally let $x$ be strictly between $a$ and $b$. If $x$ is in the triangle $adc$, then $F_{T'}(x) = f_{adc}(x) < f_{abc}(x) = F_T(x)$, because $x$ is strictly on the side of $ac$ that holds $b$. If $x$ is in $dbc$, the same argument with the line $bc$, on whose open side $x$ and $a$ lie, and $f_{abc}(d) > |d|^2 = f_{dbc}(d)$, gives the strict inequality. $\square$

**Lemma 5 (no edge comes back).** During a run of LAWSON, a segment $ab$ between two points of $S$ is flipped away at most once. So there are at most $n(n - 1)/2$ flips.

*Proof.* Let $T_0, T_1, \ldots$ be the triangulations after each flip. By Lemma 4, $F_{T_0} \ge F_{T_1} \ge \cdots$ on $H$. Suppose $ab$ is flipped away by the flip from $T_k$ to $T_{k+1}$, and let $x$ be the midpoint of $ab$. Then $F_{T_{k+1}}(x) < (|a|^2 + |b|^2)/2$ by Lemma 4. If $ab$ were an edge of a later $T_j$, then $F_{T_j}(x) = (|a|^2 + |b|^2)/2$, because $F_{T_j}$ is affine on each triangle with the edge $ab$ and equals $|\cdot|^2$ at $a$ and $b$. But $F_{T_j}(x) \le F_{T_{k+1}}(x)$, which is a contradiction. So $ab$ is never an edge again and cannot be flipped away twice. Each flip removes one segment, and there are $n(n - 1)/2$ segments. $\square$

**Lemma 6 (the Delaunay lemma).** If every interior edge of a triangulation $T$ of $S$ that uses every point of $S$ is locally Delaunay, then no point of $S$ lies strictly inside the circumcircle of any triangle of $T$.

*Proof.* Let $\Delta$ be a triangle of $T$ and $q \in S$. Pick a point $p$ in the interior of $\Delta$ that lies on no line through two distinct points of $S$; there are finitely many such lines, so such a $p$ exists. The segment from $p$ to $q$ is in $H$, which is convex, and it passes through no point of $S$ other than $q$, so it crosses edges of $T$ only at their interior points and only finitely often. Let $g(t) = F_T(p + t (q - p))$ for $t \in [0, 1]$. It is continuous and piecewise affine. At a crossing at $t_0$ of an edge $e$ from a triangle $\Delta_1$ into a triangle $\Delta_2$, the difference $f_{\Delta_2} - f_{\Delta_1}$ is affine, zero on the line of $e$, and $\ge 0$ on the side of $\Delta_2$ by Lemma 2, because $e$ is locally Delaunay. For $t > t_0$ the point $p + t(q - p)$ is on the side of $\Delta_2$, so the slope of $g$ does not decrease at $t_0$. So $g$ is convex, and as $g(t) = f_\Delta(p + t(q - p))$ for small $t$, $g(t) \ge f_\Delta(p + t(q - p))$ for all $t \in [0, 1]$. At $t = 1$ this gives $|q|^2 = F_T(q) \ge f_\Delta(q)$, because $q$ is a vertex of $T$. By Lemma 1, $q$ is not strictly inside the circumcircle of $\Delta$. $\square$

**Proof of the claim.** Every flip is legal by Lemma 3, so $T$ stays a triangulation of $S$ with the same vertices and the same boundary, the hull edges. By Lemma 5 there are at most $n(n - 1)/2$ flips. Each pass of the loop of line L3 pops one edge, and the stack receives at most $3n$ edges at the start and at most $4$ per flip, so the loop ends.

The invariant is: *every interior edge of $T$ that is not locally Delaunay is on the stack.* It holds at the start, because line L2 puts every interior edge on the stack. When line L4 pops an edge that is no longer an interior edge, or line L5 finds it locally Delaunay, the invariant still holds, as $T$ does not change. When line L6 flips $ab$ to $cd$, the local test of an edge depends only on its two triangles. The only edges whose triangles change are $cd$, which is locally Delaunay by Lemma 4, and the four sides $ad$, $db$, $bc$, $ca$ of the quadrilateral, which line L7 puts on the stack unless they are already there. All other edges keep their two triangles and their state, so the invariant holds again.

When the loop ends, the stack is empty, so every interior edge is locally Delaunay, and by Lemma 6 no point of $S$ lies strictly inside any circumcircle. This is the definition of a Delaunay triangulation of $S$. If no four points of $S$ lie on one circle, the Delaunay triangulation is unique and the output is that triangulation; otherwise the output is one of the Delaunay triangulations, and which one depends on $T_0$ and the stack order. $\blacksquare$
