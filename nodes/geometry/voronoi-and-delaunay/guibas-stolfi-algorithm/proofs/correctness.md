**Claim.** Let $S$ be the distinct points of $P$ after line L1, and assume hypothesis (H): no four points of $S$ lie on one circle. Three or more points on a line are allowed. Then GUIBAS-STOLFI terminates. If $S$ lies on one line it returns the empty list; otherwise it returns the unique Delaunay triangulation of $S$.

**Notation.** $\prec$ is the lexicographic order: $(x, y) \prec (x', y')$ if $x < x'$, or $x = x'$ and $y < y'$. It is compatible with vectors: if $a \prec b$ then $a + v \prec b + v$ and $\lambda a \prec \lambda b$ for $\lambda > 0$. A direction $d \ne 0$ is *positive* if $0 \prec d$. $\mathrm{orient}(a, b, c) > 0$ means a left turn, and $h(z) = \mathrm{orient}(l^*, r^*, z)$ below. For a circle $C$ with center $c$ and radius $\rho$, $f_C(q) = 2c \cdot q + \rho^2 - |c|^2$; then $q$ is strictly inside, on, or strictly outside $C$ exactly when $|q|^2 - f_C(q)$ is negative, zero or positive. For a triangle $t$ with vertices not on a line, $f_t$ is $f_C$ of its circumcircle. Two open segments *cross* if they meet.

**Delaunay edges.** For a finite set $A$, let $\mathrm{Del}(A)$ be the set of pairs $pq$ of points of $A$ for which some circle through $p$ and $q$ has every other point of $A$ strictly outside. Three facts are used.

- (D1) If $A \subseteq A'$, then a pair of points of $A$ in $\mathrm{Del}(A')$ is in $\mathrm{Del}(A)$. A circle that is good for $A'$ is good for $A$.
- (D2) If $A$ lies on one line, $\mathrm{Del}(A)$ is the path through $A$ in $\prec$ order. A circle through two points of a line has the points strictly between them strictly inside and the other points of the line strictly outside.
- (D3) If $A$ satisfies (H) and is not on one line, $A$ has exactly one Delaunay triangulation $DT(A)$, every circumcircle of its triangles has all other points of $A$ strictly outside, and $\mathrm{Del}(A)$ is its edge set. The first two parts are the [uniqueness theorem](/delaunay-triangulation-uniqueness/), which also shows that every pair in $\mathrm{Del}(A)$ is an edge. Conversely, let $pq$ be an edge and $pqc$ a triangle on it, and let $\lambda$ be an affine function that is zero on the line $pq$ and positive at $c$. For small $\varepsilon > 0$ the affine function $f_{pqc} - \varepsilon \lambda$ defines a circle through $p$ and $q$; $c$ is strictly outside it, and every other point stays strictly outside, as it was strictly outside before and there are finitely many points.

**Triangulations.** (T1) A point of $A$ in a closed triangle of a Delaunay triangulation of $A$, other than its vertices, is strictly inside the circumcircle (fact F1 of the [Bowyer-Watson proof](/bowyer-watson-algorithm/)). So under (H) no point of $A$ lies in a closed triangle of $DT(A)$ other than its vertices. (T2) Let $D$ be a triangulation of $A$, and let $a, b \in A$ with no point of $A$ in the open segment $ab$, where $ab$ is not an edge of $D$. Then the open segment $ab$ crosses an edge of $D$. Otherwise it meets no vertex and no edge, so it lies in one open triangle, whose closure holds $a$ and $b$; then $a$ and $b$ are two vertices of that triangle and $ab$ is its edge.

**Quad-edge facts.** (Q) We use the following properties of the quad-edge operations, which follow from the definition of Splice (Guibas and Stolfi, the section on the topological operators). While the structure is a plane straight-line graph whose Onext rings list the edges out of each vertex in counterclockwise order, Lnext$(e)$ is the next edge on the boundary of the face to the left of $e$, and Rprev$(e)$ is the next edge counterclockwise out of the destination of $e$ after $\mathrm{Sym}(e)$. Connect$(a, b)$ adds an edge from $\mathrm{dest}(a)$ to $\mathrm{org}(b)$; if $a$ and $b$ border the same face $F$ and the new segment lies in $F$ and crosses no edge, the result is again a plane straight-line graph with counterclockwise rings. Delete removes an edge and keeps the rings counterclockwise.

**Lemma 1 (BUILD).** Let $s$ be a block of $k \ge 2$ consecutive points of $S$ in $\prec$ order. BUILD$(s)$ returns a plane straight-line graph with counterclockwise rings whose edge set is $\mathrm{Del}(s)$, and two handles. The handle le starts at $\min s$, re starts at $\max s$, the face to the right of le and the face to the left of re are the outer face, and le and re are boundary edges of $\operatorname{conv} s$. If $s$ is not on one line, the faces other than the outer face are the triangles of $DT(s)$.

*Proof.* By induction on $k$. For $k = 2$, line L3 makes one edge $s_0 s_1$ with le $= s_0 \to s_1$ and re $= s_1 \to s_0$. For $k = 3$ on a line, it makes the path $s_0 s_1 s_2$, which is $\mathrm{Del}(s)$ by (D2). For three points not on a line, it makes the triangle, which is the only triangulation and has no fourth point to test; the code returns $s_0 \to s_1$ and $s_2 \to s_1$ if the triple turns left, and $s_0 \to s_2$ and $s_2 \to s_0$ if it turns right, in both cases the counterclockwise hull edge out of $s_0$ and the clockwise hull edge out of $s_2$. For $k \ge 4$, line L4 and line L5 split $s$ into blocks $L$ and $R$ with at least two points each and $L \prec R$ pointwise. By induction they have the stated form, and Lemma 8 below shows that MERGE turns them into the stated form for $s$. $\square$

Fix a merge with $L \prec R$, $|L|, |R| \ge 2$, and the structures $D_L$ and $D_R$ of Lemma 1, and let $U = L \cup R$. Since every point of $\operatorname{conv} L$ is $\preceq \max L \prec \min R \preceq$ every point of $\operatorname{conv} R$, the hulls are disjoint, so no edge of $D_L$ meets an edge of $D_R$.

**Lemma 2 (the bridge and the chains).** Going counterclockwise around the boundary of $\operatorname{conv} U$, there is exactly one place where a point $l^* \in L$ is followed by a point $r^* \in R$. All points of $U$ satisfy $h(z) \ge 0$, and no point of $U$ is in the open segment $l^* r^*$. Let $l^* = a_0 \prec a_1 \prec \cdots \prec a_p = \max L$ be the boundary points of $\operatorname{conv} L$ from $l^*$ counterclockwise to $\max L$, and $\min R = b_0 \prec \cdots \prec b_q = r^*$ those of $\operatorname{conv} R$ from $\min R$ counterclockwise to $r^*$. Then consecutive triples of each sequence do not turn right, and $h(a_j) > 0$ for $j \ge 1$ and $h(b_j) > 0$ for $j < q$. Starting from the handle re of $D_L$, the walk by Lnext visits $a_p, a_{p-1}, \ldots, a_0$ in this order, and starting from le of $D_R$, the walk by Rprev visits $b_0, b_1, \ldots, b_q$.

*Proof.* A line separates $\operatorname{conv} L$ from $\operatorname{conv} R$, so the points of $L$ on the boundary of $\operatorname{conv} U$ form one arc, which gives the unique pair $l^*, r^*$; consecutive boundary points span a boundary edge, which gives $h \ge 0$ and the empty open segment. The line $l^* r^*$ supports $\operatorname{conv} L$ with $L$ on its left and has the positive direction $u = r^* - l^*$. The counterclockwise boundary of $\operatorname{conv} L$ from $\min L$ to $\max L$ has positive edge directions and the arc back from $\max L$ to $\min L$ has negative ones; at a point inside the second arc, every supporting direction with $L$ on the left is a nonnegative combination of two negative directions, so it is negative. So $l^*$ is on the first arc, and the $a_j$ are the part of it from $l^*$ to $\max L$; the same holds for $r^*$ and the $b_j$. Points of a counterclockwise convex boundary do not turn right. If $h(a_j) = 0$ for $j \ge 1$, then $a_j$ is on the line $l^* r^*$ with $l^* \prec a_j$, so it is in the open segment (excluded) or beyond $r^*$, so $r^* \prec a_j$, which is false as $a_j \in L$; the $b_j$ are similar. By Lemma 1 and (Q), Lnext from re walks the outer face of $D_L$ with that face on its left, that is, clockwise around $\operatorname{conv} L$ from $\max L$, which passes $a_p, \ldots, a_0$ first; for $L$ on a line it walks the path from $\max L$ to $\min L$. Rprev from le of $D_R$ steps from an edge $b \to b'$ to the next edge counterclockwise out of $b'$ after $b' \to b$; the angle at $b'$ outside $\operatorname{conv} R$ holds no edge, so this is the next boundary edge counterclockwise. $\square$

**Lemma 3 (the tangent loop).** The loop before line L6 makes at most $|L| + |R|$ steps and stops with $\mathrm{org}(\mathrm{ldi}) = l^*$ and $\mathrm{org}(\mathrm{rdi}) = r^*$.

*Proof.* Let $l = \mathrm{org}(\mathrm{ldi})$, $r = \mathrm{org}(\mathrm{rdi})$, and let $l^-$ and $r^+$ be the destinations of ldi and rdi. The loop moves $l$ to $l^-$ if $\mathrm{orient}(l, l^-, r) > 0$, else moves $r$ to $r^+$ if $\mathrm{orient}(r, r^+, l) < 0$, else stops. By Lemma 2, $l$ starts at $a_p$ and moves along $a_p, a_{p-1}, \ldots$, and $r$ starts at $b_0$ and moves along $b_0, b_1, \ldots$. We show that $l$ never moves from $a_0$ and $r$ never moves from $b_q$; then the number of steps is at most $p + q$.

For positive directions $d, d'$ we have $\mathrm{orient}(0, d, d') > 0$ exactly when $d'$ is counterclockwise from $d$ by less than a half turn; as all positive directions lie in one half-plane that holds no two opposite directions, this is a strict order, and we write $\theta(d) < \theta(d')$.

*$l$ stays at $l^*$.* If $l^* \ne \min L$, then $l^-$ is the point $w$ before $l^*$ on the counterclockwise boundary of $\operatorname{conv} L$, with $w \prec l^*$ and $h(w) \ge 0$. Then $\theta(l^* - w) \le \theta(u) \le \theta(r - l^*)$, where the first inequality is $h(w) \ge 0$ and the second is $h(r) \ge 0$, so $\mathrm{orient}(w, l^*, r) \ge 0$ and $\mathrm{orient}(l^*, w, r) \le 0$: no move. If $l^* = \min L$, then $l$ did not start there (as $|L| \ge 2$) and arrived from $a_1$ by a move at some $r_0$, so $\mathrm{orient}(a_1, l^*, r_0) > 0$. Let $w$ be the clockwise neighbour of $l^*$ on the boundary of $\operatorname{conv} L$. Measured at $l^*$, all other points have positive directions, the move condition says $\theta(r_0 - l^*) < \theta(a_1 - l^*)$, and convexity at $l^*$ gives $\theta(a_1 - l^*) \le \theta(w - l^*)$. So $\theta(r_0 - l^*) < \theta(w - l^*)$, which is $\mathrm{orient}(l^*, w, r_0) < 0$: no move. While $l = l^*$, each move of $r$ from $r_j$ to $r_{j+1}$ has $\mathrm{orient}(l^*, r_j, r_{j+1}) < 0$, so $\theta(r - l^*)$ decreases and stays below $\theta(w - l^*)$. So $l$ never leaves $l^*$.

*$r$ stays at $r^*$.* This is the mirror image: exchange $L$ and $R$, reverse the order $\prec$, and measure directions at $\max R$ by $\theta(\max R - z)$.

*The stop.* At the stop, $l = a_i$ and $r = b_j$, with $\mathrm{orient}(a_i, a_{i-1}, r) \le 0$ if $i \ge 1$ and $\mathrm{orient}(b_j, b_{j+1}, a_i) \ge 0$ if $j < q$. Consider the path $z_0 = a_0, a_1, \ldots, a_i, b_j, \ldots, b_q = z_m$. Its points increase in $\prec$, so all its edge directions $d_1, \ldots, d_m$ are positive. Every turn of the path is a left turn or straight: inside the two chains by Lemma 2, at $a_i$ by the first stop condition, and at $b_j$ by the second. So $\theta(d_1) \le \theta(d_m)$. If $m \ge 2$, the point $z_1$ has $h(z_1) > 0$ and $z_{m-1}$ has $h(z_{m-1}) > 0$ by Lemma 2, so $\theta(d_1) > \theta(u) > \theta(d_m)$, a contradiction. So $m = 1$, $l = l^*$ and $r = r^*$. $\square$

So line L6 makes the edge $e_0 = r^* \to l^*$. The segment $l^* r^*$ is a boundary edge of $\operatorname{conv} U$ with no point inside, so it is in $\mathrm{Del}(U)$.

**Lemma 4 (pencil).** Let $C \ne D$ be circles through two points $l, r$. If some point strictly on one side of the line $lr$ is on $D$ and strictly outside $C$, then every point strictly on the other side that is strictly inside $D$ is strictly inside $C$.

*Proof.* $f_D - f_C$ is affine, not zero, and zero on the line $lr$, so it has opposite strict signs on the two open sides. It is positive at the given point, so it is negative on the other side, where $|q|^2 < f_D(q) < f_C(q)$ for a point strictly inside $D$. $\square$

**The cross edges.** Assume now that $U$ is not on one line, and let $DT = DT(U)$. A line $\ell$ strictly separates $L$ and $R$. The edges of $DT$ from $L$ to $R$ (the *cross edges*) are exactly the edges that cross $\ell$. They do not cross each other, so they meet $\ell$ in different points; order them along $\ell$ as $e_0, \ldots, e_k$. The segment $\ell \cap \operatorname{conv} U$ runs from $e_0 = l^* r^*$ to the other boundary edge of $\operatorname{conv} U$ that crosses $\ell$, which is $e_k$, and it is covered by the triangles of $DT$ that meet $\ell$. Each such triangle has one vertex on one side of $\ell$ and two on the other, so it has two cross edges, and they are consecutive in the order. So for $i < k$ the triangle $t_i$ of $DT$ above $e_i$ (on the side away from $e_{i-1}$) has the cross edges $e_i$ and $e_{i+1}$; let $c_i$ be its third vertex and $C_i$ its circumcircle. Write $e_i$ as the directed edge $r_i \to l_i$ with $r_i \in R$, $l_i \in L$; then $c_i$ is strictly to the right of $r_i \to l_i$, that is, $\mathrm{orient}(l_i, r_i, c_i) > 0$. If $c_i \in L$ then $e_{i+1} = r_i c_i$, and if $c_i \in R$ then $e_{i+1} = c_i l_i$. By (D3), every point of $U$ other than $l_i, r_i, c_i$ is strictly outside $C_i$. No point of $U$ is strictly to the right of $r_k \to l_k$.

**Lemma 5 (invariant of the main loop).** At the start of the $i$-th pass of line L7 ($i = 0, 1, \ldots$):

- (a) basel is $r_i \to l_i$;
- (b) the edges are $e_0, \ldots, e_i$ and the edges of $D_L$ and $D_R$ except some deleted ones, none of which is in $\mathrm{Del}(U)$;
- (c) no edge crosses any of $e_0, \ldots, e_i$, and the structure is a plane straight-line graph with counterclockwise rings.

For $i = 0$ this holds: $e_0$ is a boundary edge of $\operatorname{conv} U$, an edge inside $\operatorname{conv} L$ or $\operatorname{conv} R$ cannot cross it, and $L$ has no point on the line $l^* r^*$ other than $l^*$ by Lemma 2, so no edge of $D_L$ runs along it.

In the pass, write $l = l_i$, $r = r_i$, $c = c_i$, $C = C_i$, and call a point *valid* if it is strictly right of $r \to l$. The candidate lcand is the first edge counterclockwise after $l \to r$ out of $l$, and each deletion of line L8 removes it and takes the next one; so lcand is always the first edge counterclockwise after $l \to r$, and the same holds for rcand clockwise at $r$.

**Lemma 6 (no wrong deletion).** Under Lemma 5, line L8 and line L9 never delete an edge of $\mathrm{Del}(U)$.

*Proof.* Take line L8; line L9 is the mirror image. It deletes $l \to x$ when $x$ is valid and the next edge $l \to x'$ counterclockwise has $\mathrm{incircle}(l, r, x, x') > 0$, that is, $x'$ strictly inside the circle $E$ through $l, r, x$. Assume $lx \in \mathrm{Del}(U)$. Then $x \ne c$ if $c \in L$: otherwise $x' \notin \{l, r, c\}$, as $x'$ is not $c$, is $r$ only if the incircle value is $0$, and is not $l$; so $x'$ is strictly outside $E = C$. If $c \in L$, the edge $l c$ is in $\mathrm{Del}(U)$, so in $D_L$ by (D1), and not deleted by (b); so the first edge after $l \to r$ is at or before $l \to c$, and an $x$ strictly before it makes $lx$ enter the interior of $t_i$, which is not possible for an edge of $DT$. So $c \in R$, or $i = k$; but for $i = k$ no point is valid. So $c \in R$ and $x \ne c$. Edges out of $l$ to points of $R$ are cross edges $e_j$ with $j \le i$, and their ends other than $l$ are not valid, so $x \in L$. Call the side of the line $lr$ without valid points the lower side. For $i = 0$ no point of $U$ is strictly on the lower side. For $i \ge 1$, $x$ is valid, so it is not a vertex of $t_{i-1}$ and is strictly outside $C_{i-1}$; Lemma 4 with $C = C_{i-1}$ and $D = E$ shows that a point strictly on the lower side and strictly inside $E$ is strictly inside $C_{i-1}$, and by (D3) there is no such point of $U$. A point on the line $lr$ is outside the open segment and so outside $E$ by (D2). So $x'$ is valid. Then $x'$ is counterclockwise from $x$ by less than a half turn, so it is strictly left of $l \to x$. So the edge $lx$ of $DT$ has a triangle $\tau' = l x z$ on its left. The function $f_E - f_{\tau'}$ is zero on the line $lx$; at $r$, which is strictly right of $l \to x$, it equals $|r|^2 - f_{\tau'}(r) > 0$ by (D3) ($r \ne z$, as they lie on different sides). So it is negative strictly left of $l \to x$, and $f_E(x') < f_{\tau'}(x') \le |x'|^2$: $x'$ is not strictly inside $E$, and no deletion happens. $\square$

**Lemma 7 (one pass).** Under Lemma 5 with $i < k$: if $c \in L$, then after line L8 lcand is $l \to c$; if $c \in R$, then after line L9 rcand is $r \to c$. Line L10 does not exit, line L11 adds $e_{i+1}$ as the new basel, and Lemma 5 holds for $i + 1$. For $i = k$, line L8 and line L9 delete nothing and line L10 exits.

*Proof.* Let $c \in L$; the other case is the mirror image. As in Lemma 6, $l \to c$ is present, and the edges out of $l$ strictly between $l \to r$ and $l \to c$ counterclockwise go into the open angle $W$ of $t_i$ at $l$. No cross edge is in $W$ (it would enter $t_i$), so these are edges of $D_L$, all valid. Let $l \to x$ be one of them. By (T1), $x$ is not in the closed triangle $t_i$, and the segment $lx$ is not along $lr$ or $lc$, so it leaves $t_i$ through a point $y$ of the open segment $rc$, with $y$ in the open segment $lx$. If $L$ is on one line, every edge of $D_L$ out of $l$ lies on the line $lc$, so none is in $W$; so we can assume that $L$ is not on one line. The point $c$ is strictly left of $l \to x$, so $D_L$ has a triangle $\tau = l x z$ on the left of $l \to x$, where $z$ is the next edge after $x$ out of $l$ in $D_L$, at or before $c$.

*$l \to z$ is still present.* Deletions at $l$ happen only in passes where $l$ is the left end of basel; there basel $e_j$ ($j \le i$) is at or clockwise from $e_i$, and only the first edge counterclockwise after $e_j$ is deleted. Counterclockwise from $e_j$, the edge $l \to x$ comes before $l \to z$, and it is present now, so $l \to z$ was never the first. No cross edge lies between $x$ and $z$, so the next edge after $l \to x$ is now $l \to z$.

*The loop goes on past $x$.* Let $g = f_\tau - f_C$. Then $g(l) = 0$ and $g(x) = |x|^2 - f_C(x) > 0$, so $g(y) > 0$, as $y$ is strictly between $l$ and $x$. And $g(c) = f_\tau(c) - |c|^2 \le 0$, as $D_L$ is Delaunay. As $y$ is strictly between $c$ and $r$, $g(r) > g(y) > 0$, so $f_\tau(r) > f_C(r) = |r|^2$. Let $E$ be the circle through $l, r, x$. Then $f_\tau - f_E$ is zero on the line $lx$ and positive at $r$, so negative at $z$, which is strictly on the other side: $f_E(z) > f_\tau(z) = |z|^2$, and $z$ is strictly inside $E$. So line L8 deletes $l \to x$.

*The loop stops at $c$.* The first edge after $l \to r$ is valid (it is in $W$ or is $l \to c$), so the deletions run, and by the last paragraph they remove every edge in $W$. At $l \to c$, the next endpoint is $r$ or a point other than $l, r, c$, so the incircle value is $0$ or negative. So lcand $= l \to c$.

*The choice.* lcand is valid, so line L10 does not exit. If rcand is not valid, line L11 takes lcand. If rcand $= r \to b$ is valid, then $b \in U \setminus \{l, r, c\}$ is strictly outside $C$, and line L11 tests $\mathrm{incircle}(c, l, r, b)$, which is then negative, so it takes lcand. In the case $c \in R$, rcand $= r \to c$; if lcand $= l \to a$ is valid, let $D$ be the circle through $l, r, a$; then $f_D - f_C$ is affine, zero on the line $lr$ and equal to $|a|^2 - f_C(a) > 0$ at $a$, so it is positive on the valid side, and $f_D(c) > f_C(c) = |c|^2$; so $c$ is strictly inside $D$, the test $\mathrm{incircle}(a, l, r, c)$ is positive and line L11 takes rcand; if lcand is not valid it takes rcand. Line L11 adds $r \to c$ or $c \to l$, which is $e_{i+1}$ directed from $R$ to $L$.

*The invariant.* (a) and (b) hold by Lemma 6. For (c), take an edge $ab$ other than $e_0, \ldots, e_{i+1}$ that crosses $e_{i+1}$ (here $rc$). It meets the interior of $t_i$. It cannot cross $lr$ by (c) for $i$, and it cannot cross $lc$: edges of $D_L$ do not cross each other, edges of $D_R$ do not meet $\operatorname{conv} L$, and cross edges of $DT$ do not cross. Its endpoints are not inside the closed $t_i$ except at vertices by (T1), and $l$, $c$, $r$ are not inside an edge of $\mathrm{Del}$ of their side or in the other hull. So $ab$ has an endpoint at a vertex of $t_i$ and leaves through $rc$: from $c$ it would leave through $lr$, from $r$ it would leave through $lc$, so it starts at $l$, it is in $W$, and it was deleted. The interior of $t_i$ meets no edge, so it lies in one face, which is to the left of $\mathrm{Sym}(\text{basel})$ and of the edge $c \to l$; the new edge is a side of $t_i$, and (Q) gives a plane straight-line graph with counterclockwise rings.

*The last pass.* For $i = k$ no point is valid, so line L8 and line L9 do nothing and line L10 exits. $\square$

**Lemma 8 (MERGE).** MERGE returns a structure with the properties of Lemma 1 for $U$.

*Proof.* If $U$ is on one line, $l^*$ and $r^*$ are $\max L$ and $\min R$, no point is valid, and the result is the path $D_L$, $e_0$, $D_R$, which is $\mathrm{Del}(U)$ by (D2). Otherwise, by Lemmas 5 to 7 the loop ends after $k + 1$ passes with the edges $e_0, \ldots, e_k$, every edge of $\mathrm{Del}(U)$ inside $L$ or inside $R$ (they are in $D_L$ or $D_R$ by (D1) and not deleted by Lemma 6), and possibly other edges of $D_L$ or $D_R$. Such an other edge $ab$ has no point of $U$ in its open segment, so by (T2) it crosses an edge of $DT$, which must be a cross edge; this is excluded by (c). So the edge set is $\mathrm{Del}(U)$, the graph is plane with counterclockwise rings, and its bounded faces are the triangles of $DT$. The handles: if $l^* = \min L$, the counterclockwise hull edge out of $\min U = \min L$ is $l^* \to r^*$, which the code sets as $\mathrm{Sym}(\text{basel})$; otherwise the old handle is on the arc from $\min L$ to $l^*$, which is also part of the boundary of $\operatorname{conv} U$, and boundary edges are never deleted. The handle at $\max R$ is the mirror image. $\square$

**Proof of the claim.** Line L1 sorts and removes duplicates. With fewer than two points the code returns the empty list. Otherwise Lemma 1 for $s = S$ gives the structure of $\mathrm{Del}(S)$; if $S$ is on one line it has no bounded face and the list of triangles is empty, else its bounded faces are the triangles of $DT(S)$, which the code lists. Termination: the tangent loop stops by Lemma 3, the main loop adds a cross edge in each pass and there are at most $3|U|$ of them, each deletion removes one of finitely many edges, and the recursion is on blocks of smaller size. $\blacksquare$

*What is not covered.* With four or more points on a circle, $DT$ is not unique and Lemma 6 and Lemma 7 do not apply as stated. The code uses strict tests, and the tests on grids and on points of one circle find a Delaunay triangulation each time, but this case is observed, not proved here.
