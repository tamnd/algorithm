---
title: BUILD returns a constrained Delaunay triangulation and terminates
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

**Claim.** Let $P$ be a finite set of $n$ distinct points with integer coordinates and let $S$ be a set of segments with end points in $P$, no two of which cross at one point inside both. If the points of $P$ are not all on one line and $n \ge 3$, BUILD stops after at most $n(n-1)/2$ flips and returns a triangulation of $P$ that is a constrained Delaunay triangulation of $(P, S)$. Otherwise it returns no triangles. No general position is assumed.

**Notation.** The pieces, visibility and locally Delaunay edges are as defined on the main page. $F$ is the set of pieces and $C$ is the set of edges chosen by lines L2 to L4. A pair $ab$ is *admissible* if no point of $P$ is strictly inside the segment $ab$. Two admissible pairs *cross* if they meet at one point inside both; two admissible pairs that do not cross meet at most at a common end point, because an overlap along a line would put an end point of one strictly inside the other. For a triangle $t = abc$ with $\mathrm{orient}(a, b, c) > 0$, let $f_t$ be the affine function with $f_t = |z|^2$ at $z = a, b, c$. For every point $q$,

$$\mathrm{incircle}(a, b, c, q) > 0 \iff |q|^2 < f_t(q),$$

since both say that the lifted point $(q, |q|^2)$ is below the plane through the lifted vertices; see the [paraboloid lifting map](/paraboloid-lifting-map/). A point $q$ is strictly inside the circumcircle of $t$ exactly when $|q|^2 < f_t(q)$. For an interior edge $ab$ with triangles $abc$ and $bad$, $\mathrm{incircle}(a, b, c, d) = \mathrm{incircle}(b, a, d, c)$, since the two sides differ by two row swaps of the determinant. So the test of line L8 gives the same answer for both directed forms of an edge, and the locally Delaunay condition does not depend on which triangle is taken first.

**Lemma 1 (the scan is maximal).** Every piece is in $C$, the edges of $C$ are admissible and pairwise do not cross, and every admissible pair not in $C$ crosses an edge of $C$.

*Proof.* Pieces are admissible by line L1, and two pieces do not cross: the segments of $S$ do not cross, and two pieces of segments meet only at end points since each segment is cut at every point on it. Pieces come first in line L2, so each is chosen. Lines L3 and L4 add a pair only if it is admissible and crosses no chosen edge, and the chosen set only grows. A pair rejected at line L3 is not admissible or crosses an edge that stays chosen. $\square$

**Lemma 2 (hull edges).** If $u$ and $v$ are consecutive points of $P$ on the boundary of the convex hull, then $uv \in C$.

*Proof.* $uv$ is admissible since no point of $P$ lies between consecutive boundary points. A pair that crosses $uv$ has its end points strictly on both sides of the line $uv$, but all of $P$ is in the closed half-plane of the hull. By Lemma 1, $uv \in C$. $\square$

**Lemma 3 (faces are empty triangles).** Assume the points are not all on one line. Let $G$ be the union of the edges of $C$ and the points of $P$. Every connected component $\Phi$ of the interior of the convex hull minus $G$ is the open interior of a triangle $wxy$ whose sides are in $C$ and whose closed region holds no point of $P$ other than $w, x, y$.

*Proof.* The closure of $\Phi$ is compact, so its convex hull has a vertex $w$ that is a point of the closure of $\Phi$. The point $w$ is on the boundary of $\Phi$, so on $G$. It is not in the relative interior of an edge $e \in C$: edges meet only at end points, so the face on each side of $e$ is the same along all of $e$, and if $w$ were inside $e$ then all of $e$ would be in the closure of $\Phi$ and $w$ would not be a vertex of its convex hull. So $w \in P$. Near $w$, $\Phi$ fills the open sector between two edges $wx$ and $wy$ of $C$ that are consecutive in the angular order around $w$. This sector has an angle strictly less than $\pi$, because $w$ is a vertex of the convex hull of the closure of $\Phi$. In particular $x \ne y$, $w$ has at least two edges, and no edge of $C$ leaves $w$ into the open sector. Let $\Delta$ be the closed triangle $wxy$; it lies in the closed sector.

*Case A: no point of $P$ other than $w, x, y$ is in $\Delta$.* Then $xy$ is admissible. Assume an edge $uv \in C$ crosses $xy$ at a point $q$. Its end points are not in $\Delta$ except possibly $w$, $x$ or $y$. If $u = w$, the edge leaves $w$ into the open sector, which is false. If $u = x$ or $u = y$, the edge meets the line $xy$ at its end point, not at $q$. Otherwise both end points are outside $\Delta$, and the part of $uv$ after $q$ on the side of $w$ must leave $\Delta$ through $wx$ or $wy$, which it would cross, or through $w$, which is not inside an edge. All cases are false, so by Lemma 1, $xy \in C$. The open triangle $\Delta^\circ$ meets no edge of $C$ and no point of $P$: an edge that enters it must leave it through a side, so it crosses a side or leaves $w$ into the sector or runs from $x$ or $y$ across $\Delta$ to the opposite side, and all of these are excluded. So $\Delta^\circ$ is a connected subset of the complement of $G$ that meets $\Phi$ near $w$, so $\Delta^\circ \subseteq \Phi$. Its boundary is in $G$, so $\Phi = \Delta^\circ$.

*Case B: some point of $P$ other than $w, x, y$ is in $\Delta$.* Such a point is not on $wx$ or $wy$, which are admissible. Let $z$ be one at the largest distance from the line $xy$. Every point of the open segment $wz$ is strictly farther from the line $xy$ than $z$, and the region $\Delta' = \{p \in \Delta : \mathrm{dist}(p, xy) > \mathrm{dist}(z, xy)\}$ holds no point of $P$ except $w$. So $wz$ is admissible. Assume an edge $uv \in C$ crosses $wz$ at a point $q \in \Delta'$. Its end points are points of $P$, so neither is in $\Delta' \setminus \{w\}$, and $uv$ leaves $\Delta'$ in both directions from $q$. A line through an inner point of the convex region $\Delta'$ meets its boundary in two points, and at most one of them is on the side of $\Delta'$ that lies on a parallel to $xy$. So $uv$ meets $wx$ or $wy$ at some point other than an end point of $uv$ that is not $w$. If that point is inside $wx$ or $wy$, two chosen edges cross. If it is $w$, then $w$ is an end point of $uv$ and $uv$ leaves $w$ into the open sector. If it is $x$ or $y$ (possible only when $z$ is on the line $xy$), then $uv$ has that point as an end point and runs into $\Delta^\circ$ from it, and it must leave $\Delta$ through the opposite side $wy$ or $wx$, a crossing, or end on the side $xy$, which does not reach $q$. All cases are false, so by Lemma 1 $wz \in C$. But $wz$ leaves $w$ into the open sector, which is false. So Case B does not happen. $\square$

**Lemma 4 (line L5).** Line L5 returns the triangles of a triangulation of $P$ in which every piece is an edge.

*Proof.* By Lemma 3, every face is the interior of a triangle that line L5 finds. Conversely, a triangle $uvw$ found by line L5 has its three sides in $C$ and no other point in its closed region. As in Case A, no edge of $C$ meets its interior, so the interior is a face. By Lemma 2 the faces fill the convex hull up to $G$, so the triangles cover the hull, with disjoint interiors, and every point of $P$ is a vertex: a point is not inside a face, not inside an edge of $C$, and it lies on the boundary of a face. The pieces are in $C$ by Lemma 1, and every edge of $C$ is a side of a face, since it lies in the hull and has faces on its sides. If the points are on one line or $n < 3$, no triple has $\mathrm{orient} > 0$ and there are no triangles. $\square$

**Lemma 5 (a flip is legal).** Let $abc$ and $bad$ be counterclockwise triangles with $\mathrm{incircle}(a, b, c, d) > 0$. Then the segment $cd$ crosses the open segment $ab$, the quadrilateral $adbc$ is strictly convex, and $adc$ and $dbc$ are counterclockwise triangles with the same union as $abc$ and $bad$.

*Proof.* $c$ and $d$ are strictly on opposite sides of the line $ab$, so $cd$ meets this line at a point $q$ strictly between $c$ and $d$. Let $D$ be the closed circumdisk of $abc$; $d$ is in its interior. The point $q$ is a convex combination of $c \in D$ and $d$ with a positive weight on $d$, so $q$ is in the open disk. The open disk meets the line $ab$ exactly in the open segment $ab$. So $q$ is strictly between $a$ and $b$, and $a$ and $b$ are strictly on opposite sides of the line $cd$. A quadrilateral whose diagonals cross at an inner point of both is strictly convex, and its two diagonals cut it into the two pairs of triangles. With $c$ on the left of $a \to b$ and $d$ on the right, $\mathrm{orient}(a, d, c) > 0$ and $\mathrm{orient}(d, b, c) > 0$. $\square$

**Lemma 6 (the lifted surface goes down).** Let $\Lambda_T$ be the function on the convex hull that equals $f_t$ on each triangle $t$ of the current triangulation $T$. After a flip at line L8, $\Lambda_T$ does not increase at any point, and with $q = ab \cap cd$ the value at $q$ drops from $L_{ab}(q)$ to $L_{cd}(q) < L_{ab}(q)$, where $L_{uv}$ is the linear interpolation of $|u|^2$ and $|v|^2$ along $uv$. The new edge $cd$ is locally Delaunay.

*Proof.* Let $g = f_{abc}$. On $ab$, the old function is $g = L_{ab}$. Write $q = (1-s) c + s d$ with $0 < s < 1$. Then $L_{cd}(q) = (1-s)|c|^2 + s |d|^2 < (1-s) g(c) + s g(d) = g(q) = L_{ab}(q)$, because $|d|^2 < g(d)$. The two diagonals cut the quadrilateral into four triangles with corners $q$ and two of $a, b, c, d$. On each, the old and the new functions are affine; their difference is zero at the corners in $\{a, b, c, d\}$ and negative at $q$, so it is at most zero on that triangle. Outside the quadrilateral nothing changes. For the last statement, let $k = f_{dbc}$ and write $q = (1-u) a + u b$ with $0 < u < 1$. Then $(1-u) k(a) + u |b|^2 = k(q) = L_{cd}(q) < L_{ab}(q) = (1-u)|a|^2 + u |b|^2$, so $k(a) < |a|^2$ and $a$ is strictly outside the circumcircle of $dbc$. $\square$

**Lemma 7 (termination).** Lines L7 to L9 do at most $n(n-1)/2$ flips, and the loop ends.

*Proof.* Assume the pair $ab$ is flipped away at some step, with $q$ as in Lemma 6. After that step $\Lambda_T(q) = L_{cd}(q) < L_{ab}(q)$, and by Lemma 6 $\Lambda_T(q)$ never increases later. If $ab$ were an edge again, $\Lambda_T$ would equal $L_{ab}$ on all of $ab$, so $\Lambda_T(q) = L_{ab}(q)$, which is false. So each of the $n(n-1)/2$ pairs is flipped away at most once. The stack starts with at most $6n$ directed edges and each flip pushes four, so the loop pops at most $6n + 2n(n-1)$ times. $\square$

**Lemma 8 (the loop invariant).** At the start of each pass of line L7: the current triangles form a triangulation of $P$ with every piece as an edge, and every interior edge that is not a piece and is not locally Delaunay has at least one of its directed forms on the stack.

*Proof.* Line L6 pushes every directed edge not in $F$, and Lemma 4 gives the triangulation. A pass that pops a hull edge, a piece, an edge that is no longer present or a locally Delaunay edge changes nothing, and the popped edge needs no entry. A flip at line L8 is legal by Lemma 5 and keeps every piece, since line L7 skips the pieces. It removes $ab$, adds $cd$, which is locally Delaunay by Lemma 6, and changes the opposite vertex only for the four outer edges $ad, db, bc, ca$, which line L9 pushes. All other edges keep their two triangles. $\square$

**Lemma 9 (local implies constrained).** Let $T$ be a triangulation of $P$ in which every piece is an edge and every edge that is not a piece is locally Delaunay. Then $T$ is a constrained Delaunay triangulation of $(P, S)$.

*Proof.* Let $t_0$ be a triangle, $s$ a point inside $t_0$, and $r \in P$ visible from $s$. Let $u$ be the first point of $P$ on the segment from $s$ to $r$; it exists since $r$ is one. The half-open segment $[s, u)$ holds no point of $P$, so it meets the edges of $T$ only at inner points of edges, and it does not run along an edge, since then an end point of that edge would lie on $[s, u)$ or $s$ would be on an edge. Each meeting is a crossing from one triangle into the next. By visibility, none of the crossed edges is a piece, and none is a hull edge, since the segment stays in the convex hull. So the walk from $s$ to $u$ passes through triangles $t_0, t_1, \ldots, t_k$ across edges $e_1, \ldots, e_k$ that are locally Delaunay, and it is finite because the segment meets each of the finitely many edge lines at most once. The last triangle $t_k$ has $u$ as a vertex, since the segment reaches $u$ from inside $t_k$.

For the edge $e_i$ between $t_{i-1}$ and $t_i$, the difference $f_{t_i} - f_{t_{i-1}}$ is affine and zero on the line of $e_i$. At the vertex $d$ of $t_i$ opposite $e_i$ it equals $|d|^2 - f_{t_{i-1}}(d) \ge 0$, since $e_i$ is locally Delaunay. So $f_{t_i} \ge f_{t_{i-1}}$ on the closed side of the line of $e_i$ that holds $t_i$, which holds the rest of the segment after the crossing. By induction, $f_{t_k}(u) \ge f_{t_{k-1}}(u) \ge \cdots \ge f_{t_0}(u)$, and $f_{t_k}(u) = |u|^2$. So $|u|^2 \ge f_{t_0}(u)$ and $u$ is not strictly inside the circumcircle of $t_0$.

If $r = u$ we are done. Otherwise $r$ is on the ray from $s$ through $u$, beyond $u$. The point $s$ is inside the circumdisk, since it is inside $t_0$, and $u$ is not in the open disk. A ray from a point of a convex open set leaves it once and does not come back, so $r$ is not in the open disk. $\square$

**Proof of the claim.** If $n < 3$ or the points are on one line, Lemma 4 gives no triangles and the loop does nothing. Otherwise, by Lemma 7 the loop ends, and then the stack is empty, so by Lemma 8 the final triangles form a triangulation of $P$ that has every piece as an edge and every interior edge that is not a piece is locally Delaunay. Hull edges are locally Delaunay by definition. By Lemma 9 it is a constrained Delaunay triangulation of $(P, S)$. Line L10 lists each triangle once, rotated to start at its smallest vertex, which keeps it counterclockwise. $\blacksquare$
