---
title: Weighted Bowyer-Watson insertion keeps a regular triangulation
proves: [correctness]
status: full
---

**Claim.** Let $S$ be the distinct sites inserted, each with its first weight. If $S$ lies on one line, the structure holds no triangle. Otherwise, after the first triangle exists and after each later insertion, with $S'$ the sites inserted so far, the real triangles are counterclockwise, tile $Q = \operatorname{conv} S'$ edge to edge, have their vertices in $S'$, and satisfy $f_t(q) \le z_q$ for every real triangle $t$ and every $q \in S'$. The properties listed on the page follow.

**Notation.** $z_q = |q|^2 - w_q$. For a counterclockwise real triangle $t$, $f_t$ is the affine function equal to $z$ at its three vertices. For a hull edge $uv$, $h_{uv}$ is the affine function on the line $uv$ equal to $z$ at $u$ and $v$. A triangle in conflict with $p$ is defined on the page; $K$ is the set of such triangles.

**Lemma 0 (power test).** For counterclockwise $a, b, c$ and any $d$, the power test has the sign of $f_t(d) - z_d$, where $t = (a, b, c)$.

*Proof.* The third entry of the row of $a$ is $|a - d|^2 - w_a + w_d = (z_a - z_d) - 2 d \cdot (a - d)$. Subtracting $-2 d_x$ times the first column and $-2 d_y$ times the second leaves the third entry $z_a - z_d$, and the same for $b$ and $c$. Write $f_t(q) = \alpha \cdot q + \beta$. Then $z_a - z_d = \alpha \cdot (a - d) + (f_t(d) - z_d)$, so subtracting $\alpha_x$ times the first column and $\alpha_y$ times the second leaves the constant $f_t(d) - z_d$ in every row of the third column. The determinant is $(f_t(d) - z_d)$ times the determinant with rows $(a - d, 1)$, $(b - d, 1)$, $(c - d, 1)$, which is positive because $a, b, c$ are counterclockwise. $\square$

**Invariant.** (I1) The real triangles are counterclockwise, tile $Q$ with disjoint interiors and meet edge to edge, and their vertex set $V$ is a subset of $S'$. (I2) $f_t(q) \le z_q$ for every real $t$ and every $q \in S'$. (I3) Each counterclockwise boundary edge $v \to u$ of the tiling has exactly one ghost $(u, v, G)$, and each directed edge of a triangle has its reverse in exactly one other triangle. Line L2 makes $S' = \{a, b, c\}$ with $\mathrm{orient}(a, b, c) > 0$ and the three ghosts, so the invariant holds. Fix one later insertion of a site $p \notin S'$.

**Lemma 1 (the old function).** Let $F$ be the function on $Q$ that equals $f_t$ on each real triangle $t$. Then $F = \max_t f_t$ on $Q$, so $F$ is convex and $f_t \le F$ on $Q$ for every $t$. For every hull edge $uv$ and every $q \in S'$ on the line $uv$, $h_{uv}(q) \le z_q$.

*Proof.* $F$ is well defined because the triangles meet edge to edge. Let $x$ be in the real triangle $t_0$, $x = \sum \lambda_i v_i$ a convex combination of its vertices. For any real $t$, $f_t(x) = \sum \lambda_i f_t(v_i) \le \sum \lambda_i z_{v_i} = f_{t_0}(x) = F(x)$ by (I2). For the second part, $q$ is in $Q$ and on the line of a hull edge, so it is on that side of $Q$; the real triangle $t'$ at $uv$ has $f_{t'} = h_{uv}$ on the line, and $f_{t'}(q) \le z_q$ by (I2). $\square$

**Lemma 2 (no conflict).** $K$ is empty exactly when $p \in Q$ and $z_p \ge F(p)$. Then line L4 returns, and the invariant holds for $S' \cup \{p\}$.

*Proof.* If $p \notin Q$, then $p$ is strictly outside some hull edge, and its ghost is in $K$. Let $p \in Q$. For a real $t$, $f_t(p) \le F(p)$, with equality for a triangle that holds $p$, so a real triangle is in conflict exactly when $z_p < F(p)$. A ghost $(u, v, G)$ has $\mathrm{orient}(u, v, p) \le 0$, and it is in conflict only if $p$ is on the line $uv$ and $z_p < h_{uv}(p) = f_{t'}(p) \le F(p)$, with $t'$ as in Lemma 1. So $K$ is empty exactly when $z_p \ge F(p)$. Then $Q$ and the triangles do not change, and $f_t(p) \le F(p) \le z_p$ gives (I2) for $p$. $\square$

From now on $K$ is not empty. If $p \in Q$, then $z_p < F(p)$.

**Lemma 3 (neighbours).** Let $t = (x, y, w)$ and $t' = (y, x, v)$ be real. Then $f_{t'} \ge f_t$ on the closed side of the line $xy$ that holds $v$, and $f_{t'} \le f_t$ on the other closed side.

*Proof.* $f_{t'} - f_t$ is affine and zero on the line $xy$, and at $v$ it is $z_v - f_t(v) \ge 0$ by (I2). $\square$

**Lemma 4 (the ghosts in conflict).** The ghosts in $K$ form one contiguous chain of the boundary cycle of $Q$, and not the whole cycle.

*Proof.* Let $\ell$ be the line of one side of $Q$, with the boundary vertices $x_0, \ldots, x_m$ on it in order, and let $e_j$ be the affine function on $\ell$ through $\hat{x}_j$ and $\hat{x}_{j+1}$, with slope $s_j$. By Lemma 1, $F$ is convex along $\ell$, so $s_j \le s_{j+1}$. Both $e_j$ and $e_{j+1}$ pass through $\hat{x}_{j+1}$, so $e_{j+1}(r) - e_j(r) = (s_{j+1} - s_j)(r - x_{j+1})$ in a coordinate $r$ along $\ell$. So for a fixed point of $\ell$, the values $e_j$ rise up to the edge nearest to that point and then fall. The ghosts on this side that are in conflict with a $p$ on $\ell$ are those with $e_j(p) > z_p$, a contiguous run that holds the edge nearest to $p$, if it is not empty. If $p \notin Q$, the hull edges with $p$ strictly outside form one contiguous chain, a standard fact for a convex polygon, and when $p$ is on the line of a side, the end of that side nearest to $p$ is a corner where this chain ends, because $Q$ turns there. So the strict chain and the runs at its two ends form one chain. If $p \in Q$, only a side whose line holds $p$ can have conflict ghosts, and $p$ is on one side at most, as $p$ is not a corner. The chain is not the whole cycle: if $p \notin Q$, the edges of the side of $Q$ farthest from $p$ have $p$ strictly inside; if $p \in Q$, the other sides have no conflict ghosts. $\square$

**Lemma 5 (the conflict set is connected).** $K$ is connected through shared edges. So line L5 computes $C = K$.

*Proof.* Every ghost $(u, v, G)$ in $K$ with $p$ on the line $uv$ has its real triangle $t'$ in $K$, because $f_{t'}(p) = h_{uv}(p) > z_p$; they share the edge $uv$. By Lemma 4, the other conflict ghosts form a chain whose consecutive members share an edge at $G$. If $p \in Q$, every real triangle whose closure holds $p$ is in $K$, as $f_t(p) = F(p) > z_p$; since $p$ is not a vertex, there are one or two of them, sharing an edge. Now let $t \in K$ be real with $p$ not in its closure. Choose a point $s$ inside $t$ such that the segment $sp$ passes through no vertex. Walk from $s$ toward $p$ through the triangles $t = t_0, t_1, \ldots$, each entered through the inside of an edge, and stop at the first triangle whose closure holds $p$ or at the ghost of the hull edge where the walk leaves $Q$. When the walk crosses an edge from $t_{j-1}$ into a real $t_j$, the crossing point is not $p$, so $p$ is strictly on the side of $t_j$, and Lemma 3 gives $z_p < f_{t_{j-1}}(p) \le f_{t_j}(p)$. When it leaves $Q$, $p$ is strictly outside that hull edge. So the walk stays in $K$ and ends at a triangle holding $p$ or at a ghost of the chain. Joining these pieces gives one connected set. $\square$

**Lemma 6 (visibility).** For every border edge $(x, y)$ of line L6 with $x \ne G$ and $y \ne G$, $\mathrm{orient}(x, y, p) > 0$.

*Proof.* The edge is in a triangle $t \in C$, and the triangle $t'$ across it is not in $C$.

- *Both real.* If $p$ were on the closed side of $t'$, Lemma 3 would give $f_{t'}(p) \ge f_t(p) > z_p$, so $t' \in K$.
- *$t$ real, $t' = (y, x, G)$.* Since $t' \notin K$, $\mathrm{orient}(x, y, p) \ge 0$, and if it is $0$, then $z_p \ge h_{xy}(p) = f_t(p)$, so $t \notin K$.
- *$t = (x, y, G)$, $t'$ real.* Since $t \in K$, either $\mathrm{orient}(x, y, p) > 0$, or $p$ is on the line and $z_p < h_{xy}(p) = f_{t'}(p)$, which puts $t'$ in $K$. $\square$

**Lemma 7 (the new triangles tile the hull).** After lines L7 and L8, (I1) and (I3) hold for $S' \cup \{p\}$, except (I2).

*Proof.* Let $Q' = \operatorname{conv}(S' \cup \{p\})$, let $U$ be the union of the closed real triangles not in $C$, let $R$ be the closure of $Q' \setminus U$, and let $E_r$ be the border edges with two real ends. The interior of a triangle of $U$ does not meet $R$, since it has a neighbourhood inside $U$.

*(a) The boundary of $R$.* Let $\Lambda$ be the set of lines through $p$ that contain a segment of the boundary of $Q'$; there are at most two. Let $e$ be a point of the boundary of $R$, other than $p$, that is not a vertex and not on a line of $\Lambda$. Points of $Q' \setminus Q$ near $e$ would put a neighbourhood of $e$ in $R$ if $e \notin Q$, so $e \in Q$. If $e$ is inside $Q'$, it is on an edge $xy$ of a triangle of $U$, inside the edge, and the other side is not in $U$: either a real triangle of $C$, or the outside of $Q$ across a hull edge with points of $Q'$, so that $p$ is strictly outside $xy$ and the ghost is in $C$. If $e$ is on the boundary of $Q'$, it is on a hull edge $xy$ of $Q$, as the rest of that boundary lies on lines of $\Lambda$. Its real triangle is in $C$, since otherwise a neighbourhood of $e$ in $Q'$ is in $U$; its ghost is not in $C$, since otherwise $p$ is strictly outside $xy$, and $e$ is not on the boundary of $Q'$, or $p$ is on the line $xy$, which is then in $\Lambda$. In all cases $e$ is inside an edge of $E_r$.

*(b) Rays.* Call a ray from $p$ generic if it holds no old vertex and does not lie on a line of $\Lambda$. We show: if a generic ray $\rho$ meets $R$ at a point other than $p$, then $\rho \cap R$ is a segment $[p, b]$, and $b$ is the only point of $\rho$ on an edge of $E_r$. First, $p \in R$: if $p \notin Q$, then $p \notin U$; if $p \in Q$, the triangles whose closure holds $p$ are in $C$. Points of $Q'$ near $p$ are in $R$ for the same reasons. Let $e \ne p$ be a point of $\rho$ on an edge $(x, y)$ of $E_r$. As $\rho$ holds no vertex, $e$ is inside the edge, and by Lemma 6, $p$ is strictly to the left of $x \to y$, so $\rho$ crosses from left to right at $e$. The edge is stored with its triangle of $C$ on the left. Near $e$, the left side is in $R$: it is in a real triangle of $C$, or outside $Q$ but in $Q'$ because $p$ is strictly on that side. The right side is not in $R$: it is inside a triangle of $U$, or outside $Q'$ because $p$ is strictly on the other side. By (a), every point of the boundary of $R$ on $\rho$ other than $p$ is such a point $e$, so there the ray leaves $R$. A closed set on a ray that contains $p$ and that the ray only leaves is one segment $[p, b]$, and $b$ is the only crossing.

*(c) Tiling.* For $(x, y) \in E_r$, the triangle $(x, y, p)$ is counterclockwise by Lemma 6. A generic ray through an inside point of it crosses $xy$ at an inside point, which is then its point $b$, so the ray meets the triangle in $[p, b] \subseteq R$. So the new triangles lie in $R$, and two of them have disjoint interiors, since a generic ray through a common inside point would meet two edges of $E_r$. Conversely, $Q' \setminus U$ is open in $Q'$, so its points on generic rays are dense in $R$, and each of them is in the new triangle of the edge that holds $b$; the new triangles are closed, so they cover $R$. Hence the real triangles after line L8, those of $U$ and the new ones, cover $Q' = U \cup R$ with disjoint interiors.

*(d) Pairing.* Around each vertex $y$, the old triangles, ghosts included, form a cycle, so the border edges at $y$ alternate between leaving and entering $y$, and there are as many of each. At a real $y$, at most one border edge $(x, y)$ with a real $x$ enters $y$: two would give two new triangles $(x_1, y, p)$ and $(x_2, y, p)$ that both hold the points just to the left of $y \to p$ near the middle of the segment $yp$, against (c). In the same way at most one border edge with a real end leaves $y$. There is exactly one border edge $(G, u_0)$ and one $(u_k, G)$ in all, at the two ends of the chain of Lemma 4, and $u_0 \ne u_k$ because the chain is not the whole cycle. So no real $y$ has two edges in: that would be $(x, y)$ and $(G, y)$, then also two edges out, $(y, z)$ and $(y, G)$, and $y = u_0 = u_k$. Hence each vertex of the border has exactly one border edge in and one out, and each new directed edge $(y, p)$ or $(p, y)$ has its reverse in exactly one other new triangle. The other edges keep their partners. The two new ghosts belong to the edges $u_k \to p$ and $p \to u_0$ between $p$ and the ends of the chain.

*(e) Edge to edge.* Suppose a vertex $v$ is inside an edge $ab$ of a real triangle $s$. If the reverse of $(a, b)$ is in a real triangle $s_2$, then a small disc around $v$ minus the line $ab$ lies in the interiors of $s$ and $s_2$, and a triangle with corner $v$ has inside points in that disc, against (c). If the reverse is in a ghost, then, by the same argument with a point inside $ab$, $ab$ is on the boundary of $Q'$, and the triangle at $v$ overlaps $s$. So the triangles meet edge to edge, and the edges with a ghost are exactly the boundary edges of $Q'$. The vertices are in $S' \cup \{p\}$. $\square$

**Lemma 8 (locally regular edges).** For every edge between two new real triangles $s_1$ and $s_2$, the vertex of $s_2$ opposite the edge is not strictly below the plane of $s_1$, and the same holds for an edge between a new and an old real triangle.

*Proof.* *Old and new.* Let the edge be $xy$, with the new $s = (x, y, p)$ and the old $t' = (y, x, v)$, not in $C$. Then $f_s - f_{t'}$ is zero on the line $xy$, and at $p$ it is $z_p - f_{t'}(p) \ge 0$. So it is $\le 0$ at $v$, on the other side: $f_s(v) \le z_v$.

*Two new triangles* $(w, x, p)$ and $(x, y, p)$. First we find an affine $\gamma$ with $\gamma(x) = z_x$, $\gamma(p) = z_p$, and $\gamma \le z$ on $S'$. We need an affine $f$ with $f(x) = z_x$, $f \le z$ on $S'$ and $f(p) > z_p$. If $x$ is a vertex of a real triangle $t \in C$, take $f = f_t$. Otherwise $x$ is the end of a hull edge $uv$ whose ghost is in $C$ with $p$ strictly outside, since a ghost in $C$ with $p$ on its line has its real triangle in $C$. Let $\Delta(q)$ be the orientation determinant of $(u, v, q)$, which is $\le 0$ on $S'$, zero at $x$ and positive at $p$, and take $f = f_{t'} + \mu \Delta$ for the real triangle $t'$ at $uv$ and a large $\mu$. We also need an affine $\tau$ with $\tau(x) = z_x$, $\tau \le z$ on $S'$ and $\tau(p) \le z_p$. The edge $(x, y)$ is a border edge with the triangle on its other side not in $C$. If that triangle is a real $t'$, take $\tau = f_{t'}$. If it is a ghost not in $C$, the edge $xy$ is a hull edge of $Q$ with its real triangle $t$ in $C$, and $p$ is strictly inside its line by Lemma 6; take $\tau = f_t + \mu \Delta'$ with the orientation determinant $\Delta'$ of the hull edge, which is $\le 0$ on $S'$, zero at $x$ and negative at $p$, and $\mu$ large. Then $\lambda \mapsto ((1 - \lambda) f + \lambda \tau)(p) - z_p$ is positive at $0$ and $\le 0$ at $1$, so it is zero at some $\lambda \in (0, 1]$; let $\gamma = (1 - \lambda) f + \lambda \tau$.

Now let $\sigma_1 = f_{(w, x, p)}$. Then $\sigma_1 - \gamma$ is zero on the line $px$, since both equal $z$ at $p$ and at $x$, and at $w$ it is $z_w - \gamma(w) \ge 0$. The vertex $y$ is on the other side of the line $px$, since the two triangles have disjoint interiors, so $\sigma_1(y) \le \gamma(y) \le z_y$. $\square$

**Lemma 9 (local to global).** Let the real triangles tile a convex polygon $Q'$ edge to edge, let $F'$ equal $f_s$ on each triangle $s$, and let every interior edge be locally regular as in Lemma 8. Then $F'$ is convex on $Q'$ and $f_s \le F'$ on $Q'$ for every $s$.

*Proof.* $F'$ is continuous. Take a segment in $Q'$ that passes through no vertex and does not lie on the line of an edge. Along it, $F'$ is piecewise affine, and at a crossing of an edge from $s_1$ into $s_2$, $f_{s_2} - f_{s_1}$ is zero on the edge line and $\ge 0$ on the side of $s_2$, by local regularity at the opposite vertex. So the slope does not drop, and $F'$ is convex on the segment. Such segments are dense among all segments in $Q'$, and the inequality of convexity passes to limits by continuity. For the second part, let $x \in Q'$ and $y$ inside $s$. On the segment from $y$ to $x$, $F'$ is convex and equals $f_s$ near $y$, so it is $\ge f_s$ on the whole segment. $\square$

**Lemma 10 (the new function is lower).** Let $F'$ be the new function on $Q'$. Then $F' \le F$ on $Q$.

*Proof.* On $U$ both are equal. Let $x \in Q$ be in a new triangle $s = (a, b, p)$ with $(a, b) \in E_r$. If the triangle of $C$ at $(a, b)$ is a ghost, then $p$ is strictly outside the hull edge $ab$, and $s \cap Q$ is the segment $ab$, where $F' = F$. Otherwise it is a real $t \in C$. Then $f_t - f_s$ is zero on the line $ab$ and positive at $p$, as $f_t(p) > z_p$, so it is $\ge 0$ on the closed side of $p$, which holds $s$. With Lemma 1, $F(x) \ge f_t(x) \ge f_s(x) = F'(x)$. $\square$

**The step keeps (I2).** By Lemmas 7, 8 and 9, $F'$ is convex and $f_s \le F'$ on $Q'$ for every new real $s$. For a vertex $q$, $F'(q) = z_q$. A site $q \in S'$ that is not a vertex is in $Q$, and $z_q \ge F(q)$ by Lemma 1 and (I2), so $z_q \ge F(q) \ge F'(q)$ by Lemma 10. In both cases $f_s(q) \le F'(q) \le z_q$. The site $p$ is a vertex. So (I2) holds, and with Lemma 7 the invariant holds for $S' \cup \{p\}$.

**Proof of the claim.** Line L2 builds no triangle while the sites are on one line. Once it does, the invariant holds, and every insertion keeps it, by Lemma 2 when $K$ is empty and by the steps above otherwise. Each insertion ends, because line L5 adds each of finitely many triangles at most once.

**The properties on the page.** Let $T$ satisfy (I1) and (I2) for the set $P$, with function $F$, and let $L(x) = \sup \{g(x) : g \text{ affine}, g \le z \text{ on } P\}$, the lower hull; $L_q$ is the same with $q$ left out.

- *Lower hull.* Every $f_t$ is such a $g$, and $F = \max_t f_t$, so $F \le L$. On a triangle $t$, any such $g$ is at most the interpolation $f_t$ of its values at the vertices, so $L \le F$.
- *Vertices.* A site $q$ outside the hull of the others is a corner of the hull and so a vertex. If $q$ is not a vertex, $q$ lies in a triangle whose vertices are other sites, so $L_q(q) \le F(q) \le z_q$, and $q$ is not strictly below the hull of the others. If $q$ is a vertex, $z_q = F(q) = L(q) \le L_q(q)$, so $q$ is not redundant.
- *Uniqueness.* Let $T_1$ and $T_2$ be regular, and let $t_1 \in T_1$. Some $t_2 \in T_2$ meets the inside of $t_1$ in an open set, where $f_{t_1} = L = f_{t_2}$. So the two planes are equal. The vertices of $t_2$ are sites on this plane, and if no four lifted sites lie on one plane, the plane holds only the three vertices of $t_1$, so $t_2 = t_1$.
- *Duality.* Write $f_t(y) = 2 x_t \cdot y + \beta$. For a site $q$, $f_t(q) \le z_q$ means $2 x_t \cdot q - |q|^2 + w_q \le -\beta$, that is, $|x_t - q|^2 - w_q \ge |x_t|^2 + \beta$, with equality at the three vertices of $t$. So the three vertices have the smallest power distance at $x_t$.
- *Delaunay case.* With all weights equal to $c$, $z_q = |q|^2 - c$, and $f_t(q) - z_q$ is the same as for weights $0$, which has the sign of $\mathrm{incircle}$ by Lemma 0. For a site $q$, the affine $g(y) = 2 q \cdot y - |q|^2 - c$ has $z_y - g(y) = |y - q|^2 > 0$ for every other site $y$, so $g + \varepsilon \le z$ on the other sites for a small $\varepsilon > 0$, and $L_q(q) \ge z_q + \varepsilon$. So no site is redundant, and every site is strictly below the hull of the others, so it is a vertex. $\blacksquare$
