The conic hull of a set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ is the set of all nonnegative combinations of vectors of $S$. It is the smallest convex cone that contains $S$: think of a flashlight at the origin whose beam is just wide enough to light every point of $S$. Conic hulls are where linear programming meets geometry. The system $Ax = b$, $x \ge 0$ has a solution exactly when $b$ lies in the conic hull of the columns of $A$, and [Farkas' lemma](/farkas-lemma/) describes what happens when it does not.

## Definition

A *conic combination* (or nonnegative combination) of vectors $x_1, \ldots, x_m \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is $\sum_{i=1}^m \mu_i x_i$ with all $\mu_i \ge 0$. There is no condition on the sum of the weights.

A set $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ is a *convex cone* if it is nonempty and $\mu x + \nu y \in K$ for all $x, y \in K$ and $\mu, \nu \ge 0$. Equivalently, $K$ is [convex](/convex-set/), contains 0, and is closed under multiplication by nonnegative scalars. The *conic hull* of $S$ is

$$\mathrm{cone}(S) = \bigcap \{ K : K \text{ is a convex cone and } S \subseteq K \} = \Big\{ \sum_{i=1}^m \mu_i s_i : m \ge 0,\ s_i \in S,\ \mu_i \ge 0 \Big\},$$

where the empty sum ($m = 0$) gives the origin, so $\mathrm{cone}(\emptyset) = \{0\}$. The two descriptions agree by the argument used for the [convex hull](/convex-hull/): the set of conic combinations is a convex cone that contains $S$, and a convex cone contains every conic combination of its elements.

**Relation to the convex hull.** Each conic combination with $\sum_i \mu_i > 0$ is a positive multiple of a convex combination, so

$$\mathrm{cone}(S) = \{0\} \cup \{ t\, x : t > 0,\ x \in \mathrm{conv}(S) \}.$$

In the other direction, *homogenization* turns hulls into cones. Lift each point $s$ to $(s, 1) \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}$. Then $x \in \mathrm{conv}(S)$ if and only if $(x, 1) \in \mathrm{cone}(\{(s, 1) : s \in S\})$, because the last coordinate of a conic combination of lifted points is $\sum_i \mu_i$, and it equals 1 exactly when the combination is convex. Many results about polytopes are proved for cones first and then read off at height 1.

**Facts for finite $S$.** Let $S = \{a_1, \ldots, a_n\}$ be the columns of a matrix $A$, so $\mathrm{cone}(S) = \{ A \mu : \mu \ge 0 \}$.

1. *Carathéodory for cones.* Every nonzero $x \in \mathrm{cone}(S)$ is a positive combination of at most $d$ linearly independent vectors of $S$. The proof is on the [Carathéodory's theorem](/caratheodory-theorem/) page.
2. *Closedness.* $\mathrm{cone}(S)$ is closed. By fact 1 it is the union of the finitely many cones spanned by linearly independent subsets of $S$, and each of those is the image of the closed orthant under an injective linear map, hence closed.
3. *[Farkas' lemma](/farkas-lemma/).* For each $b \in \mathbb{R}^d$ exactly one of the following holds: $b \in \mathrm{cone}(S)$, or there is $y \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with $y \cdot a_i \ge 0$ for all $i$ and $y \cdot b < 0$. The second case is a certificate, a hyperplane through the origin that has $S$ on one side and $b$ strictly on the other. It follows from fact 2 and the [separating hyperplane theorem](/separating-hyperplane-theorem/).
4. *Finitely generated equals polyhedral.* A cone is $\mathrm{cone}(S)$ for a finite $S$ if and only if it is $\{x : Bx \le 0\}$ for a finite matrix $B$. This is the cone version of the [Minkowski-Weyl theorem](/minkowski-weyl-theorem/), and homogenization turns it into the statement that a [convex polytope](/convex-polytope/) is a bounded [polyhedron](/polyhedron/).

**The dual cone.** The dual cone of $K$ is $K^* = \{ y : y \cdot x \ge 0 \text{ for all } x \in K \}$. For a finite $S$, $\mathrm{cone}(S)^* = \{ y : y \cdot a_i \ge 0 \text{ for all } i \}$ is polyhedral, and $K^{**} = K$ for every closed convex cone $K$. Farkas' lemma is the statement $\mathrm{cone}(S) = \mathrm{cone}(S)^{**}$.

## Examples and non-examples

- **A wedge in the plane.** For $S = \{(1, 0), (1, 1)\}$, $\mathrm{cone}(S) = \{(x, y) : 0 \le y \le x\}$. The vector $b = (3, 1)$ is in it: solving $\mu_1 (1, 0) + \mu_2 (1, 1) = (3, 1)$ gives $\mu_2 = 1$, $\mu_1 = 2$, both nonnegative. The vector $b' = (1, 2)$ is not: the unique solution is $\mu_2 = 2$, $\mu_1 = -1$. A Farkas certificate for $b'$ is $y = (1, -1)$: $y \cdot (1, 0) = 1 \ge 0$, $y \cdot (1, 1) = 0 \ge 0$, and $y \cdot b' = -1 < 0$.
- **An exact membership test in the plane.** If $a_1, a_2$ are linearly independent and $a_1 \times a_2 > 0$ (cross product), then $b \in \mathrm{cone}(a_1, a_2)$ exactly when $a_1 \times b \ge 0$ and $b \times a_2 \ge 0$. With integer coordinates this is exact. For the wedge above, $a_1 \times b' = 2 \ge 0$ but $b' \times a_2 = 1 \cdot 1 - 2 \cdot 1 = -1 < 0$.
- **A half-plane.** $\mathrm{cone}(\{(1, 0), (-1, 0), (0, 1)\}) = \{(x, y) : y \ge 0\}$. A cone need not be pointed: this one contains the whole line $y = 0$.
- **The nonnegative orthant.** The cone of the unit vectors $e_1, \ldots, e_d$ is $\{x : x \ge 0\}$, the feasible region of the constraint $x \ge 0$ in a [linear program](/linear-programming-problem/).
- **A non-closed conic hull.** Let $S$ be the closed disk $x^2 + (y - 1)^2 \le 1$, which touches the origin. Then $\mathrm{cone}(S) = \{(x, y) : y > 0\} \cup \{(0, 0)\}$. The points $(1, 0)$ and $(-1, 0)$ are limits of points of the cone but are not in it, because the only point of $S$ with $y = 0$ is the origin. So fact 2 needs $S$ to be finite (it also holds for compact $S$ with $0 \notin \mathrm{conv}(S)$).
- **Not a convex cone: two rays.** The union of the rays through $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ is closed under nonnegative scaling but not under addition: $(1, 0) + (0, 1) = (1, 1)$ is on neither ray. Its conic hull is the quadrant $x, y \ge 0$.
- **Not a cone: a half-plane off the origin.** $\{(x, y) : y \ge 1\}$ is convex but does not contain 0 and is not closed under scaling by $\frac{1}{2}$.

## Related definitions

- The [convex hull](/convex-hull/) and the [affine hull](/affine-hull/) use convex and affine combinations; the linear span uses all combinations. Homogenization links the convex hull of $S$ to the conic hull of the lifted set.
- [Farkas' lemma](/farkas-lemma/) gives the certificate of non-membership in a finitely generated cone, and [LP duality](/lp-duality/) is built on it.
- A [basic feasible solution](/basic-feasible-solution/) of $Ax = b$, $x \ge 0$ is a representation of $b$ by linearly independent columns, which exists by Carathéodory's theorem for cones.
- The [Minkowski-Weyl theorem](/minkowski-weyl-theorem/) says that finitely generated cones are exactly the polyhedral cones.
- A [polyhedron](/polyhedron/) $\{x : Ax \le b\}$ is the sum of a polytope and a finitely generated cone, its recession cone.
