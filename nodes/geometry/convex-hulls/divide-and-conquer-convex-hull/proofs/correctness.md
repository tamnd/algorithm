---
title: The divide-and-conquer hull returns the hull vertices in counterclockwise order
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

**Claim.** Let $S$ be the sorted list of distinct points after line L1. DC-HULL returns the extreme points of $S$, each once, in counterclockwise order from the lexicographically smallest point. If $S$ is collinear with $|S| \ge 2$, it returns the two end points.

**Notation.** $D(p, q, r) = (q_x - p_x)(r_y - p_y) - (q_y - p_y)(r_x - p_x)$ and $\mathrm{orient}(p, q, r) = \operatorname{sign} D(p, q, r)$. For a point set $T$ sorted lexicographically with first point $t_0$ and last point $t_1$, the **lower hull** of $T$ is the list of extreme points of $T$ on the counterclockwise boundary path of $\operatorname{conv} T$ from $t_0$ to $t_1$, both ends included. (For collinear $T$ it is $t_0, t_1$.)

**Step 0: a shear removes equal $x$.** The map $\sigma(x, y) = (x + \varepsilon y, y)$ is linear with determinant 1, so $D(\sigma p, \sigma q, \sigma r) = D(p, q, r)$ for all points. It maps convex hulls to convex hulls and extreme points to extreme points. For a finite set and a small enough $\varepsilon > 0$, the points $\sigma p$ have pairwise distinct $x$-coordinates, and the order of these $x$-coordinates is the lexicographic order of the original points. The algorithm uses only the lexicographic order and orientation tests, so it runs identically on $\sigma(S)$. We therefore assume from now on that all $x$-coordinates are distinct and that lexicographic order is the order by $x$. Then "above the line through $p$ and $q$" for $p_x < q_x$ means $D(p, q, r) > 0$, and the lower hull of $T$ is the lower convex chain: its consecutive triples make strict left turns, and every point of $T$ lies on or above each of its edge lines.

**Fact 1 (edges of a lower chain).** Let $C = c_0, \ldots, c_m$ be a list with increasing $x$ in which every consecutive triple makes a strict left turn. Then the slopes of the edges $c_k c_{k+1}$ strictly increase with $k$. So, for each $k$, all points $c_l$ with $l \ne k, k+1$ lie strictly above the line of the edge $c_k c_{k+1}$. And if a line $\ell$ through $c_i$ has a larger slope than the edge $c_{i-1} c_i$, then every $c_l$ with $l < i$ lies strictly above $\ell$; if $\ell$ through $c_j$ has a smaller slope than the edge $c_j c_{j+1}$, every $c_l$ with $l > j$ lies strictly above $\ell$.

*Proof.* A left turn at $c_{k+1}$ with increasing $x$ means the slope of $c_{k+1} c_{k+2}$ exceeds that of $c_k c_{k+1}$. Going left from $c_i$, the chain falls with slopes at most that of $c_{i-1} c_i$, which is less than the slope of $\ell$; so the chain stays strictly above $\ell$ at each $c_l$, $l < i$. The right side is symmetric, and the first statement follows from the second applied to the line of an edge. $\square$

**Lemma (merge).** Let $A$ and $B$ be nonempty sorted lists of points with every point of $A$ to the left of every point of $B$. Let $\underline A = a_0, \ldots, a_s$ and $\underline B = b_0, \ldots, b_t$ be their lower hulls. Then lines L7 to L12 return the lower hull of $A \cup B$, and the loop of line L8 runs at most $s + t + 1$ times.

*Proof.* **Termination.** Line L9 only decreases $i$ and line L10 only increases $j$, within $0 \le i \le s$ and $0 \le j \le t$. Each run of the loop of line L8 except the last moves $i$ or $j$, so there are at most $s + t + 1$ runs.

**Invariant.** Let $\ell(i, j)$ be the line through $a_i$ and $b_j$. Invariant I: every point $a_k$ with $k > i$ and every point $b_l$ with $l < j$ lies on or above $\ell(i, j)$. It holds at the start, with $i = s$ and $j = 0$, because there are no such points. Suppose line L9 moves $i$ to $i - 1$, so $a_{i-1}$ is on or below $\ell(i, j)$. The new line $\ell(i-1, j)$ passes through $b_j$ and through a point on or below $\ell(i, j)$ to the left of $b_j$, so at every $x < (b_j)_x$ it is on or below $\ell(i, j)$. The points named by the invariant for the new pair are $a_i$, which is on $\ell(i, j)$, the old ones $a_k$, $k > i$, and the $b_l$, $l < j$; all are to the left of $b_j$ and on or above $\ell(i, j)$, so they are on or above $\ell(i - 1, j)$. The move of line L10 is symmetric, with the pivot $a_i$ and the points to its right.

**At the end.** The loop stops when neither inner loop moves, so $i = 0$ or $a_{i-1}$ is strictly above $\ell = \ell(i, j)$, and $j = t$ or $b_{j+1}$ is strictly above $\ell$. If $i > 0$, the edge $a_{i-1} a_i$ has a smaller slope than $\ell$, so by Fact 1 all $a_k$, $k < i$, are strictly above $\ell$. In the same way all $b_l$, $l > j$, are strictly above $\ell$. With invariant I, every point of $\underline A \cup \underline B$ is on or above $\ell$, and those on $\ell$ other than $a_i, b_j$ have $x$ strictly between $(a_i)_x$ and $(b_j)_x$. Every point of $A$ lies on or above the edge lines of $\underline A$ and so in the region above the chain $\underline A$; the same holds for $B$, and that region is on or above $\ell$ over each chain's $x$-range. So every point of $A \cup B$ is on or above $\ell$, and the points of $A \cup B$ on $\ell$ lie on the closed segment $a_i b_j$. Hence $a_i b_j$ is an edge of the lower hull of $A \cup B$, with $a_i$ and $b_j$ as its two vertices.

The other edges: for $k < i$, the edge line of $a_k a_{k+1}$ has a slope at most that of $a_{i-1} a_i$, which is less than the slope of $\ell$, and at $x = (a_i)_x$ it is on or below $a_i$ (Fact 1). So to the right of $a_i$ it lies strictly below $\ell$, and every point of $B$, being on or above $\ell$, is strictly above it. All points of $A$ are on or above it, and only $a_k, a_{k+1}$ of $\underline A$ are on it. So $a_k a_{k+1}$ is an edge of the lower hull of $A \cup B$. The same argument gives the edges $b_l b_{l+1}$ for $l \ge j$. The chain $a_0, \ldots, a_i, b_j, \ldots, b_t$ therefore consists of lower hull edges of $A \cup B$, it is connected, and it runs from the leftmost point $a_0$ to the rightmost point $b_t$ of $A \cup B$. A lower hull has exactly one edge path from its leftmost to its rightmost point, so this chain is the lower hull. Line L12 returns it. $\square$

**LOWER is correct.** By induction on $|T|$: for $|T| \le 2$ line L5 returns $T$, which is its own lower hull. For larger $T$, the two halves of line L6 are nonempty, sorted, and the first is to the left of the second; by induction the two calls return their lower hulls, and the lemma shows that lines L7 to L12 return the lower hull of $T$.

**DC-HULL is correct.** If $|S| \le 1$, line L2 returns $S$. Otherwise line L3 computes $L$, the lower hull of $S$: the vertices on the counterclockwise path from the first point $a$ of $S$ to the last point $b$. The map $\rho(p) = -p$ is a rotation by $\pi$. It preserves $D$, reverses the lexicographic order, and maps the counterclockwise path from $b$ to $a$ onto the counterclockwise path from $\rho(b)$ to $\rho(a)$. So the second list of line L3 is the list of vertices on the counterclockwise path from $b$ to $a$. Line L4 joins the two paths, dropping the repeated ends, and gives every vertex once in counterclockwise order from $a$. For collinear $S$, $L = a, b$ and the second list is $b, a$, so the result is $a, b$.

**Termination.** Each call of LOWER on more than two points makes two calls on strictly smaller lists, and each merge loop ends by the lemma. $\blacksquare$
