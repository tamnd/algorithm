---
title: KASTELEYN returns the number of perfect matchings
proves: [correctness]

Notation: $G = (V, E)$ is a connected graph with $n$ vertices, drawn in the plane without crossings. A dart is an edge with a direction of travel. Each face $f$ has a boundary walk, a cyclic list of darts with $f$ on the left of each dart; each dart lies in exactly one walk. An orientation gives each edge a direction $u \to v$. For a face $f$, $\operatorname{cw}(f)$ is the number of darts $(a, b)$ of the walk of $f$ whose edge is oriented $b \to a$. For a cycle $C$ traversed counterclockwise, $\operatorname{cw}(C)$ is the number of edges of $C$ oriented against the traversal. An orientation is Pfaffian if $\operatorname{cw}(f)$ is odd for every bounded face $f$.

We use these standard facts about connected plane graphs: Euler's formula $|V| - |E| + F = 2$, where $F$ is the number of faces; a cycle is a closed Jordan curve, and each face lies inside it or outside it; an edge has the same face on both sides if and only if it is a bridge; and a plane drawing of a tree does not separate the plane.

**Lemma 1 (the Pfaffian).** Let $K$ be a skew-symmetric matrix indexed by $V$, with $|V|$ even. For a perfect matching $M$ of the complete graph on $V$, list its pairs as $(i_1, j_1), \ldots, (i_m, j_m)$ and let $\varepsilon(M) = \operatorname{sgn}(i_1 j_1 i_2 j_2 \cdots i_m j_m) \prod_{k} K_{i_k j_k}$, where $\operatorname{sgn}$ of a sequence of all elements of $V$ is the sign of the permutation it defines relative to a fixed order of $V$. Then $\varepsilon(M)$ does not depend on the listing, $\operatorname{Pf}(K) = \sum_M \varepsilon(M)$, and $\operatorname{Pf}(K)^2 = \det K$.

*Proof.* Exchanging two pairs in the listing permutes the sequence by two transpositions, which keeps the sign. Exchanging $i_k$ and $j_k$ is one transposition, which changes the sign, and replaces $K_{i_k j_k}$ by $K_{j_k i_k} = -K_{i_k j_k}$. So $\varepsilon(M)$ is well defined. The sum $\sum_M \varepsilon(M)$ is the definition of the Pfaffian, and $\operatorname{Pf}(K)^2 = \det K$ is Cayley's theorem (Lovász and Plummer, Chapter 8). $\square$

Let $K$ be the matrix of line L8 for an orientation of $G$. If $M$ contains a pair that is not an edge of $G$, then $\varepsilon(M) = 0$. If $M$ is a perfect matching of $G$, list each edge as (tail, head); then every factor $K_{i_k j_k}$ is 1, and $\varepsilon(M) = \operatorname{sgn}(t_1 h_1 \cdots t_m h_m) = \pm 1$.

**Lemma 2 (two matchings).** Let $M$ and $M'$ be perfect matchings of $G$. The edges in exactly one of them form disjoint cycles $C_1, \ldots, C_r$ of even length. Then $\varepsilon(M) \varepsilon(M') = \prod_{s=1}^{r} (-1)^{1 + \operatorname{cw}(C_s)}$.

*Proof.* Each vertex is in one edge of $M$ and one edge of $M'$, so the symmetric difference has all degrees 0 or 2, and its components are cycles that alternate between $M$ and $M'$, hence have even length. Build two listings. Put each shared edge, in the same order, at the same place in both. For each cycle $C$, with vertices $c_1, \ldots, c_{2l}$ in counterclockwise order and $c_1 c_2 \in M$, list $(c_1, c_2), (c_3, c_4), \ldots, (c_{2l-1}, c_{2l})$ for $M$ and $(c_2, c_3), \ldots, (c_{2l-2}, c_{2l-1}), (c_{2l}, c_1)$ for $M'$, in the same block of positions. The sequence for $M'$ is the sequence for $M$ with each block of a cycle rotated by one place. A rotation of $2l$ places is a cycle of length $2l$, which is an odd permutation. So the two sequences have signs whose product is $(-1)^r$. The products of the entries of $K$ multiply to 1 over the shared edges, since each appears in both with the same pair, and to $\prod_{t=1}^{2l} K_{c_t c_{t+1}}$ over a cycle $C$ (indices modulo $2l$). The factor $K_{c_t c_{t+1}}$ is $+1$ if the edge is oriented along the counterclockwise traversal and $-1$ if it is oriented against it, so the product is $(-1)^{\operatorname{cw}(C)}$. Multiplying gives the claim. $\square$

**Lemma 3 (cycles).** Let the orientation be Pfaffian, and let $C$ be a cycle of $G$ with $v_{\mathrm{in}}$ vertices strictly inside it. Then $\operatorname{cw}(C) \equiv 1 + v_{\mathrm{in}} \pmod 2$.

*Proof.* Let $H$ be the subgraph of $G$ formed by $C$ and all vertices and edges inside $C$. It is connected: a path in $G$ from a vertex inside $C$ to a vertex of $C$ cannot leave the inside without meeting $C$, and edges do not cross, so it meets $C$ at a vertex, and its part up to that vertex lies in $H$. Let the inside of $C$ contain $k$ faces of $G$ and $e_{\mathrm{in}}$ edges not on $C$. These $k$ faces are bounded faces of $G$ and are also faces of $H$, and $H$ has one more face, outside $C$. Euler's formula for $H$ gives $(|C| + v_{\mathrm{in}}) - (|C| + e_{\mathrm{in}}) + (k + 1) = 2$, that is, $e_{\mathrm{in}} = v_{\mathrm{in}} + k - 1$.

Now add $\operatorname{cw}(f)$ over the $k$ faces inside $C$. Each edge of $C$ has exactly one of its darts in these walks, the dart of the counterclockwise traversal, since the inside is on its left. Each inside edge has both darts in these walks, and exactly one of the two darts is opposite to its orientation. So the sum is $\operatorname{cw}(C) + e_{\mathrm{in}}$. Each term is odd, so the sum is congruent to $k$. Hence $\operatorname{cw}(C) \equiv k - e_{\mathrm{in}} = 1 - v_{\mathrm{in}} \equiv 1 + v_{\mathrm{in}} \pmod 2$. $\square$

**Lemma 4 (equal signs).** If the orientation is Pfaffian, then $\varepsilon(M) = \varepsilon(M')$ for all perfect matchings $M, M'$ of $G$.

*Proof.* Take a cycle $C$ of the symmetric difference, as in Lemma 2. Each vertex of $C$ is matched by $M$ to a vertex of $C$, along an edge of $C$. A vertex strictly inside $C$ is matched by $M$ along an edge that cannot cross $C$ and cannot end on $C$, so it is matched to another vertex strictly inside $C$. So $M$ pairs up the vertices strictly inside $C$, and $v_{\mathrm{in}}$ is even. By Lemma 3, $\operatorname{cw}(C)$ is odd, so each factor in Lemma 2 is $+1$, and $\varepsilon(M) \varepsilon(M') = 1$. Both are $\pm 1$, so they are equal. $\square$

**Lemma 5 (the dual tree).** Let $T$ be a spanning tree of $G$. The graph $D$ whose vertices are the faces of $G$, with one edge for each edge $e \notin T$ joining the faces on the two sides of $e$, is a tree, and no edge $e \notin T$ has the same face on both sides.

*Proof.* An edge not in $T$ lies on a cycle (with the path of $T$ between its ends), so it is not a bridge and has two different faces on its sides. By Euler's formula, $D$ has $F = |E| - |V| + 2$ vertices and $|E| - (|V| - 1) = F - 1$ edges. The drawing of $T$ does not separate the plane, so any two faces are joined by a curve that avoids the drawing of $T$ and the vertices, and crosses edges not in $T$ finitely often at inner points. Each crossing moves from one face to the face on the other side of the crossed edge, along an edge of $D$. So $D$ is connected, and a connected graph with $F$ vertices and $F - 1$ edges is a tree. $\square$

**Lemma 6 (the orientation).** After the loop of lines L5 to L7, every edge is oriented and the orientation is Pfaffian.

*Proof.* Line L2 orients the edges of $T$. By Lemma 5, each edge not in $T$ joins a face to its parent in the rooted tree $D$ for exactly one non-root face, so line L6 picks each of them exactly once, and every edge is oriented at the end.

Fix a bounded face $f$, with parent edge $e_f$. The edges of the walk of $f$ are tree edges and edges not in $T$; an edge not in $T$ on the walk of $f$ is an edge of $D$ at $f$, so it is $e_f$ or the parent edge of a child of $f$. Children are handled before $f$, so when line L7 handles $f$, every edge of the walk except $e_f$ is oriented. By Lemma 5, $e_f$ has different faces on its two sides, so exactly one dart of $e_f$ is in the walk of $f$. Choosing its direction against that dart adds 1 to $\operatorname{cw}(f)$, and choosing it along adds 0, so line L7 can make $\operatorname{cw}(f)$ odd. Later passes orient only parent edges $e_g$ of faces $g$ handled after $f$. If such an $e_g$ were on the walk of $f$, then $f$ would be $g$ or the parent of $g$; $f \ne g$, and the parent of $g$ is handled after $g$, not before. So the edges of $f$ do not change, and $\operatorname{cw}(f)$ stays odd. $\square$

**Theorem.** For a connected plane graph $G$, KASTELEYN$(G)$ returns the number of perfect matchings of $G$.

*Proof.* If $n$ is odd, $G$ has no perfect matching, and line L1 returns 0. Let $n$ be even. By Lemma 6, the orientation is Pfaffian. By Lemma 1 and the remark after it, $\operatorname{Pf}(K) = \sum_M \varepsilon(M)$ over the perfect matchings $M$ of $G$, with each $\varepsilon(M) = \pm 1$. By Lemma 4, all these terms are equal, so $|\operatorname{Pf}(K)| = N$, the number of perfect matchings. By Lemma 1, $\det K = \operatorname{Pf}(K)^2 = N^2$, so line L9 returns $\sqrt{\det K} = N$. For a graph that is not connected, a perfect matching is a choice of one perfect matching in each component, so the count is the product of the counts of the components. $\blacksquare$
