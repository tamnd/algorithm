An **equational theory** is a set of equations between [terms](/term/) that is closed under the rules of equational reasoning: every term equals itself, equality is symmetric and transitive, equal arguments give equal results, and an equation stays true when its variables are replaced by terms. The equational theory generated by a set $E$ of axioms is the set of all equations that follow from $E$ by these rules. It is exactly the conversion relation of $E$ read as rewrite rules in both directions, and by the [Birkhoff completeness theorem](/birkhoff-completeness-theorem/) it is also the set of equations that hold in every algebra that satisfies $E$. Deciding membership in it is the [word problem](/word-problem/) for $E$.

## Definition

We use the notation of the term rewriting pages: a signature $\Sigma$, variables $V$, terms $T(\Sigma, V)$, positions $p$ with subterm $t|_p$ and replacement $t[s]_p$, and substitutions $\sigma$ applied as $t\sigma$. An **equation** (or identity) is a pair of terms written $s \approx t$. It is read with all its variables universally quantified.

**Equational logic.** Let $E$ be a set of equations. The relation $\approx_E$ is the least set of equations that contains $E$ and is closed under these five rules, for all terms $s, t, u, s_i, t_i$, each $f \in \Sigma$ of arity $n$ and each substitution $\sigma$:

| rule | from | infer |
|---|---|---|
| reflexivity | | $t \approx t$ |
| symmetry | $s \approx t$ | $t \approx s$ |
| transitivity | $s \approx t$ and $t \approx u$ | $s \approx u$ |
| congruence | $s_1 \approx t_1, \ldots, s_n \approx t_n$ | $f(s_1, \ldots, s_n) \approx f(t_1, \ldots, t_n)$ |
| substitution | $s \approx t$ | $s\sigma \approx t\sigma$ |

A set $T$ of equations is an **equational theory** if it is closed under the five rules. $\mathrm{Th}(E) = \{\, s \approx t : s \approx_E t \,\}$ is the equational theory **generated** (or presented) by $E$, and $E$ is an **axiomatization** of it. The first three rules make $\approx_E$ an [equivalence relation](/equivalence-relation/), the fourth makes it a congruence, and the fifth makes it fully invariant (stable under substitution).

**The rewriting view.** Let $\to_E$ be the rewrite relation of $E$ read left to right, $u[l\sigma]_p \to_E u[r\sigma]_p$ for $l \approx r \in E$, and let $\leftrightarrow_E = \to_E \cup \leftarrow_E$. Then

$$s \approx_E t \iff s \leftrightarrow_E^* t.$$

*Proof.* ($\Leftarrow$) One step $u[l\sigma]_p \leftrightarrow_E u[r\sigma]_p$ is derivable: substitution gives $l\sigma \approx r\sigma$ (or its symmetric), and congruence with reflexivity for the other arguments, once for each symbol on the path from the root to $p$, puts it in the context $u$. Reflexivity and transitivity then cover any finite chain. ($\Rightarrow$) $\leftrightarrow_E^*$ contains $E$ (a step at the root with the identity substitution), and it is closed under the five rules: it is an equivalence by definition, and one step $s \leftrightarrow_E t$ stays one step after a substitution $\sigma$ (because $(l\sigma')\sigma = l(\sigma'\sigma)$) and after placing it inside $f(\ldots, \square, \ldots)$. Congruence for $n$ arguments follows by changing one argument at a time. Since $\approx_E$ is the least such set, $\approx_E \subseteq \leftrightarrow_E^*$. $\square$

So a proof of $s \approx_E t$ can always be written as a chain of replacements of equals by equals. This is the form that [term rewriting systems](/term-rewriting-system/) and [completion](/knuth-bendix-completion/) work with.

**The semantic view.** A $\Sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{A}$ is a set with a function $f^{\mathcal{A}}$ for each $f \in \Sigma$. It **satisfies** $s \approx t$ if $s$ and $t$ take the same value under every assignment of elements of $\mathcal{A}$ to the variables. $\mathcal{A}$ is a **model** of $E$ if it satisfies each equation of $E$. The [Birkhoff completeness theorem](/birkhoff-completeness-theorem/) says that $s \approx_E t$ holds iff every model of $E$ satisfies $s \approx t$.

## Examples and non-examples

**A short derivation.** Let $E = \{ f(f(x)) \approx x \}$. Substitution with $\sigma = \{x \mapsto f(f(y))\}$ gives $f(f(f(f(y)))) \approx f(f(y))$. Substitution with $\{x \mapsto y\}$ gives $f(f(y)) \approx y$. Transitivity gives $f^4(y) \approx_E y$. As a conversion this is $f^4(y) \to_E f^2(y) \to_E y$. By induction, $f^{2k}(y) \approx_E y$ and $f^{2k+1}(y) \approx_E f(y)$ for each $k \ge 0$. The rule $f(f(x)) \to x$ terminates and has a joinable critical pair, so it decides this theory: $s \approx_E t$ iff $s$ and $t$ have the same normal form.

**Groups.** The theory of groups is $\mathrm{Th}(E)$ for $E = \{ e \cdot x \approx x,\ i(x) \cdot x \approx e,\ (x \cdot y) \cdot z \approx x \cdot (y \cdot z) \}$. It contains $x \cdot e \approx x$ and $i(i(x)) \approx x$, which are not in $E$ and need several steps to derive. [Knuth-Bendix completion](/knuth-bendix-completion/) finds a convergent system of ten rules for this theory, which decides it.

**Commutativity.** $E = \{ x + y \approx y + x \}$ generates a theory in which $s \approx_E t$ iff $t$ is obtained from $s$ by swapping the arguments of some occurrences of $+$. The single equation cannot be oriented into a terminating rule, since $x + y \to y + x$ undoes itself. Such theories are built into matching and unification instead, as in [rewriting modulo equations](/rewriting-modulo-equations/) and [AC matching](/ac-matching/).

**A ground theory.** $E = \{ f(a) \approx b \}$ gives $f(f(a)) \approx_E f(b)$ by congruence. It does not give $f(x) \approx b$, because substitution goes from general to specific, never back. Ground theories are decided by [congruence closure](/congruence-closure/) in $O(n \log n)$ time.

**The trivial theory.** If $E$ contains $x \approx y$ with distinct variables, substitution gives $s \approx t$ for every pair of terms, so $\mathrm{Th}(E)$ is everything. Its only models have one element.

**The empty set.** $\mathrm{Th}(\emptyset)$ is syntactic equality: $s \approx_\emptyset t$ iff $s = t$.

**Non-example: a congruence that is not a theory.** On $T(\Sigma, V)$ with a constant $a$, let $\sim$ be the least congruence that contains $f(x) \approx b$, that is, the least set closed under the first four rules only. It contains $f(x) \approx b$ and $g(f(x)) \approx g(b)$, but not $f(a) \approx b$, because the variable $x$ is treated as one more constant. So $\sim$ is a congruence but not an equational theory: it is not closed under substitution. The theory $\mathrm{Th}(\{f(x) \approx b\})$ contains $f(a) \approx b$ and $f(t) \approx b$ for every term $t$.

**Non-example: implications.** "If $x \cdot y \approx x \cdot z$ then $y \approx z$" (cancellation) is not an equation, and the class of cancellative semigroups is not defined by equations: its homomorphic images need not be cancellative. Equational logic handles only universally quantified equations; conditional rewriting handles such implications.

## Related definitions

- The [Birkhoff completeness theorem](/birkhoff-completeness-theorem/) shows that derivability in equational logic equals truth in all models.
- The [word problem](/word-problem/) asks whether $s \approx_E t$. It is undecidable for some finite $E$ ([undecidability of the word problem](/undecidability-of-word-problem/)), and decidable when $E$ has a finite convergent [term rewriting system](/term-rewriting-system/), which [Knuth-Bendix completion](/knuth-bendix-completion/) tries to find.
- An equation $s \approx t$ is an **inductive theorem** of $E$ if all its ground instances are in $\mathrm{Th}(E)$; proving these is [inductive theorem proving](/inductive-theorem-proving/).
- [Equational unification](/equational-unification/) solves $s\sigma \approx_E t\sigma$ for $\sigma$; [narrowing](/narrowing/) does this for theories given by convergent systems.
- [Congruence closure](/congruence-closure/) decides the theory of a finite set of ground equations.
