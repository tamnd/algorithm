The Dyck language $D_k$ is the set of strings over $k$ kinds of brackets in which every bracket is closed by a bracket of its own kind, in properly nested order. It is the basic example of a [context-free language](/context-free-language/) that is not regular, its words of length $2m$ over one bracket pair are counted by the [Catalan numbers](/catalan-numbers/), and by the [Chomsky-Schützenberger theorem](/chomsky-schutzenberger-theorem/) every context-free language is built from a Dyck language, a regular language and a homomorphism. A [stack](/pushdown-automaton/) recognizes it in one left-to-right pass.

## Definition

Fix $k \ge 1$ and the alphabet $\Sigma_k = \{(_1, )_1, (_2, )_2, \ldots, (_k, )_k\}$ of $k$ opening brackets $(_i$ and their closing brackets $)_i$. For $k = 1$ we write $($ and $)$, and for $k = 2$ also $($, $)$, $[$, $]$.

The **Dyck language** $D_k \subseteq \Sigma_k^*$ is the language of the [context-free grammar](/context-free-grammar/)

$$S \to S\,S \mid (_1\, S\, )_1 \mid \cdots \mid (_k\, S\, )_k \mid \varepsilon,$$

in the grammar notation of this site ($A \to \alpha \mid \beta$, $\varepsilon$ the empty string, $S$ the start symbol, $n$ the length of a string). A word of $D_k$ is a **Dyck word**; for $k = 1$ see also [Dyck word](/dyck-word/).

**Equivalent definitions.** For $u \in \Sigma_k^*$ the following are equivalent.

1. **Grammar.** $u \in D_k$.
2. **Unambiguous grammar.** $u$ is generated by $S \to (_1\, S\, )_1\, S \mid \cdots \mid (_k\, S\, )_k\, S \mid \varepsilon$, and this grammar gives each word exactly one parse tree.
3. **Stack test.** Read $u$ from left to right with an empty stack. Push each opening bracket. On a closing bracket $)_i$, pop the top if it is $(_i$, and reject if the stack is empty or its top is another bracket. Accept if the stack is empty at the end.
4. **Cancellation.** Deleting adjacent pairs $(_i )_i$, in any order, until none is left ends with $\varepsilon$.

For $k = 1$ add a fifth: every prefix of $u$ has at least as many $($ as $)$, and $u$ has as many of each.

*Proof of 1, 2, 3.* Call $u$ *good* when the stack test accepts it. **Lemma:** $u$ is good if and only if, started on any stack $\sigma$, the test never pops a symbol of $\sigma$ and ends with stack $\sigma$. The test reads only the top of the stack, so a run from the empty stack that never rejects is the same run on top of $\sigma$. From this, $u$ and $v$ good imply $uv$ good, and $u$ good implies $(_i\, u\, )_i$ good: after $(_i$ the run of $u$ returns to the stack with $(_i$ on top, and $)_i$ pops it. So each word derived in grammar 1 is good, by induction on the length of the derivation. Every word of grammar 2 is in $D_k$, because $(_i S )_i S$ is derived by $S \Rightarrow SS \Rightarrow (_i S )_i S$. It remains to show that each good word has exactly one parse tree in grammar 2. Let $u \neq \varepsilon$ be good. Its first symbol is an opening bracket $(_i$, since a closing bracket on the empty stack is rejected. Let $p$ be the first position after which the stack is empty again; $p$ exists because the final stack is empty. The bracket read at $p$ pops the $(_i$ pushed at position 1, so it is $)_i$, and $u = (_i\, x\, )_i\, y$ where $x$ runs above that $(_i$ without popping it and $y$ runs from the empty stack. By the lemma $x$ and $y$ are good. This **first-return decomposition** is unique, because $p$ is determined by $u$, and a parse tree of grammar 2 must split $u$ exactly this way: the production $S \to (_i S )_i S$ makes the inner $S$ derive a good word, after which the stack is empty for the first time. Induction on $|u|$ gives existence and uniqueness of the tree. $\square$

*Proof of 3 and 4.* Deleting a pair $(_i )_i$ does not change the result of the stack test, since the run pushes $(_i$ and pops it at once. Repeated deletion ends at a word with no adjacent pair $(_i )_i$, and such a word is accepted by the stack test only when it is empty: if it is nonempty, its first closing bracket follows an opening bracket of another kind or comes first, and either case rejects. So 4 holds if and only if 3 holds. For $k = 1$ the stack holds only $($ and acts as a counter, whose value after a prefix is the number of $($ minus the number of $)$; the test rejects when it would go below 0 and accepts when it ends at 0, which is the fifth condition. $\blacksquare$

## Examples and non-examples

**In $D_1$:** $\varepsilon$, $()$, $(())()$, $((()))$. The words of $D_1$ of length 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 number 1, 1, 2, 5 and 14, the Catalan numbers $C_m = \frac{1}{m+1}\binom{2m}{m}$ for $m = n/2$ pairs.

**In $D_2$ (with $($ $)$ and $[$ $]$):** $([])$, $[]()$, $[()[]]$. The words of $D_2$ of length $2m$ number $C_m\, 2^m$: 1, 2, 8, 40 and 224 for $m = 0, \ldots, 4$. In general $D_k$ has $C_m k^m$ words of length $2m$. The proof: erasing the kind of each bracket maps $D_k$ onto $D_1$, the matched pairs of a $D_1$ word are fixed by the word, and each of the $m$ pairs can take any of the $k$ kinds.

**Not in $D_1$:** $)($, where the first prefix has more $)$ than $($; $(()$, where the counts differ.

**Not in $D_2$:** $(]$, a bracket closed by another kind; $([)]$, crossing pairs. Erasing kinds maps $([)]$ to $(())$, which is in $D_1$, so the second kind of bracket adds real information.

**Not regular.** $D_1 \cap (^*\, )^* = \{(^m\, )^m : m \ge 0\}$, which is not a [regular language](/regular-language/), and regular languages are closed under intersection. So no finite automaton recognizes $D_1$, and the same argument with one kind of bracket applies to every $D_k$.

**Deterministic.** The stack test is a deterministic pushdown automaton, so $D_k$ is a [deterministic context-free language](/deterministic-context-free-language/). It runs in $O(n)$ time, and for $k = 1$ it needs only a counter of $O(\log n)$ size.

## Related definitions

- [Dyck word](/dyck-word/) is the combinatorial view of $D_1$, as lattice paths that never go below the axis, and [Catalan numbers](/catalan-numbers/) count them.
- [Parenthesis matching](/parenthesis-matching/) finds the partner of each bracket, and [balanced parentheses](/balanced-parentheses/) as a data structure encodes trees succinctly with a $D_1$ word.
- The [Chomsky-Schützenberger theorem](/chomsky-schutzenberger-theorem/) says that every context-free language is $h(D_k \cap R)$ for a regular $R$ and a homomorphism $h$.
- A [pushdown automaton](/pushdown-automaton/) generalizes the stack test; its stack contents over a run form a Dyck-like pattern of pushes and pops.
- [CFL reachability](/cfl-reachability/) with a Dyck language matches calls with returns along graph paths.
