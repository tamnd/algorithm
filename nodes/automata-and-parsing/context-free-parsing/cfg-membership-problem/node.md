The CFG membership problem asks, for a [context-free grammar](/context-free-grammar/) $G$ and a string $w$, whether $w \in L(G)$. It is decidable, and in fact in polynomial time: the [CYK algorithm](/cyk-algorithm/) and the [Earley parser](/earley-parser/) decide it in $O(n^3 |G|)$ time, and [Valiant's algorithm](/valiant-algorithm/) in the time of [Boolean matrix multiplication](/boolean-matrix-multiplication/). Whether a much faster general algorithm exists is open, and conditional lower bounds tie the question to matrix multiplication and to finding cliques. The problem asks only for yes or no; the [parsing problem](/cfg-parsing-problem/) also asks for a parse tree.

## In plain words

A grammar is a set of rules for building strings, and you are given one string. Can the rules build it?

You cannot simply try all ways of applying the rules, because there are infinitely many, and some rules can loop without producing anything. The useful observation is that every piece of the finished string was built from a smaller piece of the same string. So it is enough to find out, for each piece of the string, which rule symbols can build that piece, starting with the single letters and moving to longer pieces. The string is in the language when the start symbol can build the whole string. A string with a few hundred letters already has tens of thousands of pieces, which is why this takes cubic time.

## Definition

**Notation.** A grammar is $G = (V, \Sigma, R, S)$ with productions $A \to \alpha \mid \beta$, $\varepsilon$ is the empty string, $n$ is the length of the input $w = w_1 \cdots w_n$, and $|G|$, written $g$ in the front matter, is the sum of $1 + |\alpha|$ over the productions $A \to \alpha$.

**Input.** A context-free grammar $G$ and a string $w \in \Sigma^*$.

**Output.** "Yes" if $S \Rightarrow^* w$, and "no" otherwise.

The *uniform* problem takes both $G$ and $w$ as input and measures the cost in $n$ and $|G|$. The *fixed-grammar* problem fixes $G$ and measures the cost in $n$ alone; it is the recognition problem of the language $L(G)$.

**Why it is decidable.** A grammar in [Chomsky normal form](/chomsky-normal-form/) derives a string of length $n \ge 1$ in exactly $2n - 1$ steps: $n - 1$ steps by binary productions and $n$ steps by terminal productions. So after a conversion to that form, a search over all derivations of $2n - 1$ steps decides membership. The search is exponential; dynamic programming over the substrings of $w$ makes it polynomial.

## Variants

- **Fixed grammar or uniform.** For a fixed grammar every algorithm below runs in $O(n^3)$ time or less. With the grammar in the input, the dependence on $|G|$ matters, and conversions such as Chomsky normal form can square it.
- **Parsing.** Output a parse tree or a [shared packed parse forest](/shared-packed-parse-forest/) of all trees. This is the [parsing problem](/cfg-parsing-problem/).
- **Deterministic languages.** For a grammar in a deterministic class, such as [LL(1)](/ll1-parsing/) or [LR(1)](/canonical-lr-parsing/), membership takes $O(n)$ time. The languages with such grammars are the [deterministic context-free languages](/deterministic-context-free-language/).
- **Pushdown automata.** Membership for a [pushdown automaton](/pushdown-automaton/) is the same problem through the conversion to a grammar.
- **All substrings, prefixes, or a graph.** Decide membership for each substring at once (CYK gives this), for each prefix (Earley gives this), or for the label of each path of a graph, which is [CFL reachability](/cfl-reachability/).
- **Weighted versions.** For a [probabilistic grammar](/probabilistic-context-free-grammar/), compute the total probability of $w$ (the [inside algorithm](/inside-outside-algorithm/)) or the [most probable parse](/most-probable-parse/).

## Examples

For the grammar $S \to a S b \mid S S \mid \varepsilon$, whose language is the balanced strings with $a$ for an opening and $b$ for a closing bracket:

| $w$ | answer | reason |
|---|---|---|
| $\varepsilon$ | yes | $S \to \varepsilon$ |
| $ab$ | yes | $S \Rightarrow aSb \Rightarrow ab$ |
| $aabbab$ | yes | $S \Rightarrow SS$, then $aabb$ and $ab$ |
| $abba$ | no | after $ab$ the $b$ closes nothing |
| $aab$ | no | more $a$ than $b$ |

Each yes-instance here has infinitely many parse trees, because $S \Rightarrow SS \Rightarrow S$ can repeat with an empty $S$. So an algorithm that lists derivations must bound them, and a recognizer must not follow such cycles forever. For the grammar $S \to a S b \mid \varepsilon$ the answer for $aabb$ is yes and for $abab$ is no, although both strings have two $a$ and two $b$.

## Known algorithms

- **[CYK](/cyk-algorithm/)**, $O(n^3 |G|)$ for a grammar in [Chomsky normal form](/chomsky-normal-form/), after a [conversion](/chomsky-normal-form-conversion/) that takes $O(|G|^2)$ time and gives a grammar of size $O(|G|^2)$. With a binarization that keeps $\varepsilon$-productions and unit productions and handles them in the table, the total is $O(n^3 |G|)$.
- **[Earley](/earley-parser/)**, $O(n^3 |G|)$ for every grammar, $O(n^2 |G|)$ for an unambiguous grammar, and linear for most LR(k) grammars.
- **[Valiant](/valiant-algorithm/)**, $O(|G| \, n^\omega)$ where $\omega < 2.372$ is the [matrix multiplication exponent](/matrix-multiplication-exponent/), by a reduction to Boolean matrix multiplication. It is of theoretical interest; the constants are large.
- **Deterministic parsers.** [LR(1)](/canonical-lr-parsing/), [LALR(1)](/lalr-parsing/) and [LL(1)](/ll1-parsing/) parsers, $O(n)$ for grammars in their classes.
- **[GLR](/glr-parsing/)**, linear on deterministic parts and polynomial in general with a graph-structured stack.
- **The proof of decidability.** Search all derivations of $2n - 1$ steps in Chomsky normal form, exponential in $n$.

## Bounds

**Upper bounds.** $O(n^3 |G|)$ in the uniform setting; $O(n^\omega)$ for a fixed grammar. Lewis, Stearns and Hartmanis showed that each context-free language is recognized in space $O(\log^2 n)$, and Ruzzo placed context-free recognition in the parallel class NC$^2$.

**The hardest language.** Greibach constructed one fixed context-free language $L_0$ such that every context-free language reduces to it by a homomorphism of the input. So if $L_0$ is recognized in time $T(n)$, every context-free language is recognized in time $O(T(cn))$ for a constant $c$. A lower bound for one fixed grammar is therefore a lower bound for all.

**Conditional lower bounds.** No lower bound better than $\Omega(n)$ is known. Lee showed that a parser of the right kind that runs in $O(|G| n^{3 - \epsilon})$ time gives Boolean matrix multiplication in subcubic time. Abboud, Backurs and Vassilevska Williams gave a fixed grammar whose recognition in $O(n^{\omega - \epsilon})$ time, or in $O(n^{3 - \epsilon})$ time by a combinatorial algorithm, would give a $k$-clique algorithm faster than any known.

**The uniform problem is P-complete.** With the grammar in the input, membership is complete for P under logspace reductions (Jones and Laaser), so it is unlikely to have an algorithm in polylogarithmic space or parallel time in $|G|$.

## Reductions

- **To emptiness.** $w \in L(G)$ exactly when the grammar for $L(G) \cap \{w\}$, with nonterminals $[A, i, j]$ for $0 \le i \le j \le n$, is not [empty](/cfg-emptiness-problem/). This grammar has size $O(n^3 |G|)$ after binarization, and the linear emptiness test on it is the CYK algorithm.
- **To Boolean matrix multiplication**, by Valiant: the table of CYK is a transitive closure that can be computed with matrix products.
- **From Boolean matrix multiplication**, by Lee: the product of two $m \times m$ Boolean matrices is read off the parse information for a grammar of size $O(m^2)$ and a string of length $O(m^{1/3})$.
- **From $k$-clique**, by Abboud, Backurs and Vassilevska Williams, for one fixed grammar.

## Applications

- **Compilers and interpreters**, where a syntax check is membership in the language of the programming language, usually with an [LR](/lr-parsing/) or [LL](/ll1-parsing/) parser.
- **Natural language processing**, where chart parsers decide whether and how a grammar covers a sentence.
- **Bioinformatics**, where grammars model the [secondary structure of RNA](/rna-secondary-structure/), and membership and its weighted forms score a sequence.
- **Program analysis**, where membership on all paths of a graph is [CFL reachability](/cfl-reachability/).
- **Input validation**, where data formats with nesting, such as balanced brackets, are checked against a grammar.
