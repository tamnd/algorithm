The Frank-Tardos algorithm replaces a rational vector $w \in \mathbb{Q}^n$ by an integer vector $\bar{w}$ whose entries have size polynomial in $n$ and $\log N$, such that $w^\top b$ and $\bar{w}^\top b$ have the same sign for every integer vector $b$ with $\|b\|_1 \le N - 1$. It calls [simultaneous Diophantine approximation](/simultaneous-diophantine-approximation/) by the [LLL algorithm](/lll-algorithm/) at most $n$ times. Applied to the objective of an integer program over a bounded set, it keeps the same optimal solutions, so the size of the objective no longer matters. A. Frank and É. Tardos published it in 1987.

## In plain words

Suppose a list of weights has huge numbers, but we only ever use the weights to compare two solutions with small whole-number entries. Then many smaller weights give the same comparisons. This algorithm finds such small whole-number weights.

It first finds a whole-number vector that points almost in the same direction as the weights. That vector decides most comparisons. The comparisons it leaves as ties are decided by what is left over, which gets the same treatment. After a few rounds nothing is left. The final weights put the rounds together, with the first round counting much more than the second, the second more than the third, and so on.

## The problem

**Input.** A vector $w \in \mathbb{Q}^n$ and an integer $N \ge 2$.

**Output.** A vector $\bar{w} \in \mathbb{Z}^n$ such that $\operatorname{sign}(w^\top b) = \operatorname{sign}(\bar{w}^\top b)$ for every $b \in \mathbb{Z}^n$ with $\|b\|_1 \le N - 1$, and such that $\log \|\bar{w}\|_\infty$ is bounded by a polynomial in $n$ and $\log N$.

The main use is with $b = x - y$ for two feasible points $x, y$. If all feasible points lie in $\{0, \dots, K\}^n$, take $N = nK + 1$. Then $w^\top x \ge w^\top y$ exactly when $\bar{w}^\top x \ge \bar{w}^\top y$, so $\max w^\top x$ and $\max \bar{w}^\top x$ have the same optimal solutions. Applied to a constraint $(a, \beta)$ as a vector in $\mathbb{Q}^{n+1}$ with $b = (x, -1)$, the same step replaces $a^\top x \le \beta$ by an inequality with small integers and the same integer solutions in the box.

## The algorithm

The approximation step needs one fact. For $v \in \mathbb{Q}^n$ and $0 < \varepsilon < 1$, let $k = \lceil n(n+1)/4 \rceil$ and $\delta = 2^{-k} \varepsilon^{n+1}$. The first vector of an LLL-reduced basis of the lattice generated by the rows $(e_j, 0)$ for $j = 1, \dots, n$ and $(-v, \delta)$ has the form $(p - q v, q \delta)$ with $p \in \mathbb{Z}^n$ and $q \in \mathbb{Z}$, and after a change of sign it satisfies $1 \le q \le 2^k \varepsilon^{-n}$ and $\|q v - p\|_\infty \le \varepsilon$.

```pseudo id=main
FRANK-TARDOS(w, N)
L1  d ← w;  t ← 0
L2  while d ≠ 0:
L3      v ← d / ‖d‖∞
L4      (q, p) ← SIMULTANEOUS-APPROXIMATION(v, 1/N)      ▷ 1 ≤ q, ‖q v − p‖∞ ≤ 1/N
L5      t ← t + 1;  pₜ ← p
L6      d ← q v − p
L7  M ← (N − 1) · max over i of ‖pᵢ‖∞ + 1
L8  return Σ from i = 1 to t of M^(t−i) pᵢ
```

The vectors $p_1, \dots, p_t$ give a lexicographic test: the sign of $w^\top b$ is the sign of the first nonzero number among $p_1^\top b, \dots, p_t^\top b$, or $0$ if all are zero. Line L8 packs this test into one vector, with base $M$ digits.

## Example

Let $w = (1001, 2001, 3000)$ and $N = 3$, so the test vectors $b$ have $\|b\|_1 \le 2$. Here $n = 3$, $k = 3$ and $\varepsilon = 1/3$.

| round | $v$ (line L3) | $q$ | $p$ | new $d$ (line L6) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $(1001/3000, 667/1000, 1)$ | 3 | $(1, 2, 3)$ | $(1/1000, 1/1000, 0)$ |
| 2 | $(1, 1, 0)$ | 1 | $(1, 1, 0)$ | $(0, 0, 0)$ |

Line L7 gives $M = 2 \cdot 3 + 1 = 7$, and line L8 returns $\bar{w} = 7 \cdot (1, 2, 3) + (1, 1, 0) = (8, 15, 21)$.

Take $b = (1, 1, -1)$. Then $w^\top b = 2$. The first vector gives $p_1^\top b = 0$, a tie, and the second gives $p_2^\top b = 2 > 0$, so the sign is positive, and indeed $\bar{w}^\top b = 8 + 15 - 21 = 2$. Each round adds at least one zero entry to $d$: after round 1 the third entry is 0, and after round 2 all are 0.

## Correctness

**Theorem.** The loop runs at most $n$ times, and the output satisfies $\operatorname{sign}(w^\top b) = \operatorname{sign}(\bar{w}^\top b)$ for every $b \in \mathbb{Z}^n$ with $\|b\|_1 \le N - 1$.

The proof has three parts.

- **Termination.** If $v_j = \pm 1$, then $q v_j$ is an integer at distance at most $1/N < 1$ from $p_j$, so $p_j = q v_j$ and the new $d_j$ is $0$. If $v_j = 0$, then $|p_j| \le 1/N < 1$ gives $p_j = 0$, and the new $d_j$ is $0$. So the set of zero entries of $d$ grows by at least one in each round.
- **The lexicographic test.** In round $i$, $q_i v_i = p_i + d_{i+1}$ with $q_i \ge 1$ and $\|d_{i+1}\|_\infty \le 1/N$. For $\|b\|_1 \le N - 1$, $|d_{i+1}^\top b| \le (N-1)/N < 1$. If $p_i^\top b \ne 0$, it is an integer of absolute value at least 1, so $v_i^\top b$ has its sign. Otherwise $v_i^\top b$ is a positive multiple of $d_{i+1}^\top b$, which is a positive multiple of $v_{i+1}^\top b$, and the test continues with round $i + 1$.
- **Packing.** Each $|p_i^\top b| \le (N-1)\|p_i\|_\infty \le M - 1$. The sum $\sum_{i > j} M^{t-i} |p_i^\top b|$ is at most $M^{t-j} - 1$, so the first nonzero term decides the sign of $\bar{w}^\top b$.

The full proof is in the proof file.

## Complexity

The loop runs at most $n$ times, and each round runs LLL on a lattice of dimension $n + 1$. The entries of $p_i$ satisfy $|p_{ij}| \le q_i \le 2^k N^n$, so $M \le 2^k N^{n+1}$ and
$$\|\bar{w}\|_\infty < M^t \le 2^{kn} N^{n(n+1)} = 2^{O(n^3)} N^{n(n+1)}.$$
The number of digits of $\bar{w}$ is $O(n^3 + n^2 \log N)$, which does not depend on the size of $w$. Frank and Tardos state the bound $\|\bar{w}\|_\infty \le 2^{4n^3} N^{n(n+2)}$ for their version.

The page code runs LLL on $v$ with all its digits, so its time depends polynomially on the size $s$ of $w$. Frank and Tardos first round $v$ to a precision that depends only on $n$ and $N$, which makes the number of arithmetic operations independent of $s$; this is the strongly polynomial form used in their applications.

## Properties

- **Exact.** The output preserves every comparison it promises, with no error term.
- **Size independent of the input numbers.** The output size depends only on $n$ and $N$.
- **Few rounds.** At most $n$ calls of simultaneous Diophantine approximation.
- **Not unique.** Many vectors $\bar{w}$ work. The algorithm finds one with a provable size bound, not the smallest one.

## Variants and improvements

- **Constraints.** The same step applied to each row $(a, \beta)$ of a system $A x \le b$ gives an equivalent system with small integers on the integer points of a box. This is how the numbers of an integer program can be made small before [Lenstra's algorithm](/lenstra-algorithm/) or [Kannan's algorithm](/kannan-algorithm/) runs.
- **Strongly polynomial combinatorial algorithms.** Frank and Tardos use the step to turn polynomial algorithms whose running time depends on the size of the weights into strongly polynomial ones. Their applications include maximum weight cliques in [perfect graphs](/perfect-graph/) and minimum cost submodular flows.
- **Tardos 1986.** An earlier rounding method of Tardos for linear programs whose constraint matrix has small entries uses the same idea of replacing large numbers by rounded ones with the same combinatorial answer.

## When to use it

Use it in theory, when an algorithm is polynomial in the size of the weights and a bound that depends only on the dimension and the range of the solutions is needed. In practice the weights it produces have about $n^3$ binary digits, which is often more than the input has, so solvers do not use it as a preprocessing step.

## Pitfalls

- **N must cover all differences.** For optimization over $\{0, \dots, K\}^n$, the differences of two points have 1-norm up to $nK$, so $N$ must be at least $nK + 1$. With a smaller $N$, two solutions can change order.
- **Ties.** The output keeps ties: if $w^\top b = 0$ then $\bar{w}^\top b = 0$. This is part of the promise, and it is why the packing with base $M$ is needed.
- **Exact arithmetic.** The approximation step must be exact. Floating point LLL can return a $q$ and $p$ that do not satisfy the bound, and then signs can change.

## Implementations

The Python code is exact and uses only the standard library. The function `simultaneous_approximation` builds the lattice of the section above with rational entries and runs LLL with $\delta = 3/4$; it checks that the result is integer. The function `frank_tardos` follows lines L1 to L8 and can record a trace of the rounds. The tests check the example, the bounds on $q$ and on $\|q v - p\|_\infty$, the sign of $w^\top b$ against brute force over all $b$ with $\|b\|_1 \le N - 1$ in dimensions 1 to 3, the size bound of the Complexity section, and that $w$ and $\bar{w}$ have the same optimal solutions over $\{0, 1\}^n$.

## History

Frank and Tardos presented the method at FOCS 1985 and published it in Combinatorica in 1987. It built on the LLL algorithm of 1982, which gave a polynomial-time method for simultaneous Diophantine approximation, and on the earlier strongly polynomial algorithm of Tardos (1986) for linear programs with small constraint matrices. The paper showed that many combinatorial algorithms that are polynomial in the size of the weights can be made strongly polynomial, and that the fixed-dimension integer programming algorithms of Lenstra and Kannan can be made to work with small numbers.
