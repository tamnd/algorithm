---
title: Megiddo's algorithm in two variables is correct and runs in linear time
proves: [correctness, complexity]
status: full
---

**Setting.** After line L1 the program is: minimize $F(x)$ subject to $x \in I = [lo, hi]$ and $F(x) \le G(x)$, where $F$ is the maximum of the lower lines, $G$ the minimum of the upper lines ($G = +\infty$ if there is none), and an infinite end of $I$ is allowed. Write $\Phi = \{x \in I : F(x) \le G(x)\}$, a closed interval, since $F - G$ is convex. The program is infeasible if $\Phi = \emptyset$, unbounded if $F$ has no lower bound on $\Phi$, and otherwise optimal with an optimal $x^* \in \Phi$. The change of variables of line L1 is linear and invertible and maps the objective to $y$, so it keeps each of these three cases and maps optimal points to optimal points. Line L2 is correct: with no lower line, $y$ can go to $-\infty$ at any $x \in I$, and $I \ne \emptyset$ exactly when $lo \le hi$.

**Claim.** Each round of lines L4 to L7 either ends with a correct answer, or replaces $(I, F, G)$ by $(I', F', G')$ with $I' \subseteq I$, such that the new program has the same status, and, if optimal, the same optimal value with an optimal point in $I'$. A round with $N > 6$ lines removes at least $\lceil P/2 \rceil$ of them, where $P \ge (N - 2)/2$ is the number of pairs. The total time is $O(m)$.

**Step 1 (pruning keeps $F$ and $G$ on $I$).** Let two lower lines $\ell_1, \ell_2$ have slopes $s_1 > s_2$ and cross at $x_c$. Then $\ell_1(x) - \ell_2(x) = (s_1 - s_2)(x - x_c)$, so $\ell_1 \ge \ell_2$ on $[x_c, \infty)$ and $\ell_2 \ge \ell_1$ on $(-\infty, x_c]$. If $x_c \le lo$, then $\ell_2 \le \ell_1$ on $I$, and dropping $\ell_2$ does not change the maximum $F$ on $I$. If $x_c \ge hi$, the same holds with $\ell_1$ dropped. Parallel lower lines: the one with the smaller intercept is below everywhere. For upper lines, the same argument with "above" and "minimum" holds. So $F$ and $G$, and therefore $\Phi$ and the program, do not change on $I$.

**Step 2 (the oracle).** Let $x_m \in I$, and let $f_-, f_+$ be the least and largest slopes of the lower lines that attain $F(x_m)$. For a maximum of finitely many lines, every slope $g$ in $[f_-, f_+]$ is a subgradient: $F(x) \ge F(x_m) + g(x - x_m)$ for all $x$. In the same way, $-G$ is convex with subgradients $[-g_+, -g_-]$ at $x_m$, and $F - G$ has the subgradients $[f_- - g_+, f_+ - g_-]$.

- $x_m$ feasible and $0 \in [f_-, f_+]$: then $F(x) \ge F(x_m)$ for all $x$, so $x_m$ is optimal.
- $x_m$ feasible and $f_+ < 0$: for $x < x_m$, $F(x) \ge F(x_m) + f_+(x - x_m) > F(x_m)$. So every feasible $x < x_m$ is worse than the feasible point $x_m$. Hence the program on $I' = I \cap [x_m, \infty)$ has the same status, and the same optimal value with an optimal point in $I'$ when there is one. The case $f_- > 0$ is symmetric.
- $x_m$ infeasible and $f_- - g_+ > 0$: for $x > x_m$, $(F - G)(x) \ge (F - G)(x_m) + (f_- - g_+)(x - x_m) > 0$. So $\Phi \subseteq (-\infty, x_m]$, and $I' = I \cap (-\infty, x_m]$ has the same $\Phi$. The case $f_+ - g_- < 0$ is symmetric.
- $x_m$ infeasible and $0 \in [f_- - g_+, f_+ - g_-]$: then $(F - G)(x) \ge (F - G)(x_m) > 0$ for all $x$, so $\Phi = \emptyset$ and INFEASIBLE is correct.

Line L6 sets $I'$ as in these cases, and line L7 prunes on $I'$, which is correct by Step 1.

**Step 3 (the count).** Let $K$ be the number of pairs that reach line L5. The other $P - K$ pairs each lost a line at line L4. The median $x_m$ is the element of rank $\lceil K/2 \rceil$ (from below) among the $K$ crossing points. If the oracle answers LEFT, then all crossing points $\ge x_m$, at least $\lceil K/2 \rceil$ of them, are not in the open interval $(lo, x_m)$. If it answers RIGHT, all crossing points $\le x_m$, at least $\lceil K/2 \rceil$, are not in $(x_m, hi)$. Each of these pairs loses a line at line L7. In total at least $P - K + \lceil K/2 \rceil \ge \lceil P/2 \rceil$ lines go. With $N$ lines split into two sets, $P \ge (N - 2)/2$.

**Step 4 (the base case and the time).** At line L8 at most 6 lines remain, and by the claim the program on them over $I$ has the status of the input. If an end of $I$ is infinite, say $hi = \infty$, then far to the right, $F$ is the lower line with the largest (slope, intercept) and $G$ the upper line with the least (slope, intercept), so $\Phi$ is unbounded to the right exactly when the first pair is lexicographically at most the second, and $F \to -\infty$ there exactly when that slope is negative. This decides UNBOUNDED to the right, and the left side is symmetric. Otherwise $F$ is bounded below on $\Phi$, and since $\Phi$ is a closed interval and $F$ is piecewise linear, either $\Phi = \emptyset$ or $\min_\Phi F$ is attained at an end of $\Phi$ or at a break point of $F$. Each such point is a finite end of $I$ or a crossing point of two of the lines. Line L8 tests all of them (and the point $0$ if there is none, which covers the case of parallel lines on all of $\mathbb{R}$), so it returns the correct answer.

For the time: a round with $N$ lines costs $O(N)$, using linear-time selection for the median, and leaves at most $N - \lceil P/2 \rceil \le N - (N - 2)/4 = \frac34 N + \frac12$ lines. For $N > 6$ this is at most $\frac{5}{6} N$. So the sizes of the rounds form a series bounded by $m \sum_k (5/6)^k = 6m$, and the total time is $O(m)$. $\blacksquare$
