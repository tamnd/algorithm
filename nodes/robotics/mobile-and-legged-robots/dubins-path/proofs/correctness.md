Line numbers refer to the pseudocode on the [Dubins path](/dubins-path/) page. We work in the frame of lines L1 and L2: the start is at the origin with heading $\alpha$, the goal is at $(d, 0)$ with heading $\beta$, and all lengths are divided by $\rho$, so every arc has radius 1. A rigid motion of the plane and a scaling map paths of curvature at most $1/\rho$ to paths of curvature at most 1 and multiply lengths by $1/\rho$, so a shortest path in this frame is a shortest path in the original one. We write $u(\psi) = (\cos\psi, \sin\psi)$ and $n(\psi) = (-\sin\psi, \cos\psi)$, the unit vector to the left of the heading $\psi$.

**Lemma 1 (motion on one segment).** A car at position $z$ with heading $\psi$ that drives a left arc of angle $a \ge 0$ ends at $z + n(\psi) - n(\psi + a)$ with heading $\psi + a$. A right arc of angle $a$ ends at $z - n(\psi) + n(\psi - a)$ with heading $\psi - a$. A straight segment of length $s$ ends at $z + s\,u(\psi)$ with heading $\psi$.

*Proof.* A left arc of radius 1 has its center at $c = z + n(\psi)$, and the car stays at $c - n(\cdot)$ of its current heading. After turning by $a$ it is at $c - n(\psi + a)$. The right arc is the mirror image with $c = z - n(\psi)$. The straight segment is clear. $\square$

**Lemma 2 (CSC words end at the goal).** If line L5 accepts LSL, RSR, LSR or RSL, the path with segments $(t, p, q)$ from $(0, 0, \alpha)$ ends at $(d, 0, \beta)$.

*Proof.* LSL. The start circle has center $c_0 = n(\alpha) = (-s_\alpha, c_\alpha)$ and the goal circle, the left circle through the goal, has center $c_1 = (d, 0) + n(\beta) = (d - s_\beta, c_\beta)$. Their difference is $c_1 - c_0 = (d + s_\alpha - s_\beta, c_\beta - c_\alpha)$, whose squared norm is $d^2 + 2d(s_\alpha - s_\beta) + (s_\alpha - s_\beta)^2 + (c_\beta - c_\alpha)^2 = 2 + d^2 - 2\cos(\alpha - \beta) + 2d(s_\alpha - s_\beta) = p^2$. So $c_1 - c_0 = p\,u(\gamma)$ with the $\gamma$ of the table. By Lemma 1, after the left arc of angle $t \equiv \gamma - \alpha$ the car is at $c_0 - n(\gamma)$ with heading $\gamma$. After the straight segment of length $p$ it is at $c_0 - n(\gamma) + p\,u(\gamma) = c_1 - n(\gamma)$, still with heading $\gamma$. That point lies on the goal circle, and the car turns about $c_1$. After the left arc of angle $q \equiv \beta - \gamma$ it is at $c_1 - n(\beta) = (d, 0)$ with heading $\beta$. Angles are used only through $u$ and $n$, so reducing $t$ and $q$ modulo $2\pi$ does not change the end point.

RSR is the mirror image of LSL in the $x$ axis: the substitution $\alpha \to -\alpha$, $\beta \to -\beta$ maps the LSL formulas to the RSR formulas, and the mirror maps left arcs to right arcs.

LSR. Now $c_0 = n(\alpha)$ and $c_1 = (d, 0) - n(\beta) = (d + s_\beta, -c_\beta)$. Their difference is $D = (d + s_\alpha + s_\beta, -c_\alpha - c_\beta)$ with $|D|^2 = d^2 + 2d(s_\alpha + s_\beta) + 2 + 2\cos(\alpha - \beta) = p^2 + 4$. After the left arc of angle $t \equiv \gamma - \alpha$, the car is at $c_0 - n(\gamma)$. After the straight segment, it is at $c_0 - n(\gamma) + p\,u(\gamma)$. For this point to be $c_1 + n(\gamma)$, the point of the right circle where the heading is $\gamma$, we need $D = p\,u(\gamma) - 2n(\gamma)$. The vector $p\,u(\gamma) - 2n(\gamma)$ has norm $\sqrt{p^2 + 4} = |D|$ and direction $\gamma + \mathrm{atan2}(-2, p)$. So the choice $\gamma = \mathrm{atan2}(D_y, D_x) - \mathrm{atan2}(-2, p)$ of the table makes them equal. The right arc of angle $q \equiv \gamma - \beta$ then ends at $c_1 + n(\beta) = (d, 0)$ with heading $\beta$. RSL is the mirror image of LSR. $\square$

**Lemma 3 (CCC words end at the goal).** If line L5 accepts RLR or LRL, the path from $(0, 0, \alpha)$ ends at $(d, 0, \beta)$.

*Proof.* RLR. A car on a right circle with center $c$ and heading $\psi$ is at $c + n(\psi)$. The right circles at the start and at the goal have centers $c_0 = -n(\alpha) = (s_\alpha, -c_\alpha)$ and $c_1 = (d, 0) - n(\beta) = (d + s_\beta, -c_\beta)$, so $D = c_1 - c_0 = (d - s_\alpha + s_\beta, c_\alpha - c_\beta)$ and $|D|^2 = 2 + d^2 - 2\cos(\alpha - \beta) + 2d(s_\beta - s_\alpha)$.

Follow the path. The first right arc ends at heading $\psi_1 = \alpha - t$ at the point $c_0 + n(\psi_1)$. The middle left circle has center $m = c_0 + 2n(\psi_1)$. After the left arc of angle $p$, the car has heading $\psi_1 + p$ and is at $m - n(\psi_1 + p)$, and the right circle it turns on next has center $m - 2n(\psi_1 + p)$. So the path ends on the goal circle exactly when $c_1 = c_0 + 2n(\psi_1) - 2n(\psi_1 + p)$. The identity $n(a) - n(a + p) = 2\sin(p/2)\,u(a + p/2)$ turns this into $D = 4\sin(p/2)\,u(\psi_1 + p/2)$.

Taking norms, $|D|^2 = 16\sin^2(p/2) = 8 - 8\cos p$, so $\cos p = (8 - |D|^2)/8 = (6 - d^2 + 2\cos(\alpha - \beta) + 2d(s_\alpha - s_\beta))/8 = c$. A solution exists exactly when $|c| \le 1$, the test of line L5, and then $p = \arccos c$ or $p = 2\pi - \arccos c$; the table takes the second. For $p \in (0, 2\pi)$, $\sin(p/2) > 0$, so the direction of $D$ is $\psi_1 + p/2$, and $\psi_1 = \mathrm{atan2}(c_\alpha - c_\beta, d - s_\alpha + s_\beta) - p/2$. This gives $t = \alpha - \psi_1$ as in the table. The heading changes by $-t$, then $+p$, then $-q$, so the end heading is $\alpha - t + p - q$, and $q = \alpha - \beta - t + p$ makes it $\beta$. The car is then on the right circle with center $c_1$ with heading $\beta$, at $c_1 + n(\beta) = (d, 0)$. LRL is the mirror image of RLR in the $x$ axis. $\square$

**Lemma 4 (uniqueness per word).** For each CSC word, there is at most one path with arcs in $[0, 2\pi)$ and a straight part of length at least 0. For each CCC word, there is at most one path with arcs in $[0, 2\pi)$ and a middle arc in $(\pi, 2\pi)$.

*Proof.* The end circles are fixed by the poses and the turn directions. For LSL, the proof of Lemma 2 shows that a path of the word with straight heading $\gamma$ must satisfy $c_1 - c_0 = p\,u(\gamma)$ with $p \ge 0$. If $c_1 \ne c_0$, this fixes $p$ and $\gamma$, and then $t$ and $q$ modulo $2\pi$. If $c_1 = c_0$, then $p = 0$ and the path is one arc of angle $t + q$; any split with $t + q \ge 2\pi$ contains a full loop, and the implementation takes $\gamma = \alpha$, so $t = 0$ and the arc is the shortest one. For LSR, a path must satisfy $D = p\,u(\gamma) - 2n(\gamma)$, which fixes $p = \sqrt{|D|^2 - 4}$ and then $\gamma$. RSR and RSL are the mirror images. For a CCC word, the proof of Lemma 3 shows that a path of the word must have $\cos p = c$, so $p = \arccos c \le \pi$ or $p = 2\pi - \arccos c \ge \pi$, and that $p$ fixes $\psi_1$, the middle center and the last arc. Only the second value of $p$ is in $(\pi, 2\pi)$. $\square$

**Theorem (Dubins 1957).** Between any two poses there is a forward path of curvature at most $1/\rho$ of least length. Every such shortest path is of the form CSC or CCC, where C is an arc of radius $\rho$ of angle in $[0, 2\pi)$ and S a segment, and some pieces can have length 0. In a CCC shortest path, the middle arc has angle greater than $\pi$.

This theorem is quoted from Dubins (1957); Boissonnat, Cérézo and Leblond (1994) give a proof with the minimum principle. We do not reprove it.

**Theorem (correctness).** DUBINS returns a word and lengths whose path ends at $q_1$ and has the least length among all forward paths of curvature at most $1/\rho$.

*Proof.* By Lemmas 2 and 3, every word accepted in line L5 gives a path from $q_0$ to $q_1$ of length $\ell$, so the returned path is feasible. By Dubins' theorem a shortest path $P^*$ exists and has one of the six words, with arcs in $[0, 2\pi)$, and with a middle arc above $\pi$ if it is CCC. By Lemma 4 it is the only path of its word with these properties. The closed form of that word exists (its $p^2 \ge 0$ or $|c| \le 1$, since $P^*$ is a path with this geometry), and by Lemmas 2 and 3 it describes such a path, so it is $P^*$. So line L5 meets $P^*$, and line L6 keeps the least length seen, which is at most the length of $P^*$. As every candidate is feasible, it is at least that length too, so it is equal. Line L7 returns it. A non-empty word set is guaranteed, because $P^*$ exists. $\square$

**What the tests add.** The tests compare the result with an independent construction that finds the common tangents by bisection and both middle circles by intersecting circles, on 150 random pairs, within $10^{-7}$. They check that no random forward path of up to five segments is shorter, on 3000 cases, and that each of the six words is the answer for some pair.
