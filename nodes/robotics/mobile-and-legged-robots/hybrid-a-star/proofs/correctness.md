title: Hybrid A* returns drivable paths, terminates, and is optimal relative to its primitives when the bins separate the states
proves: [correctness]
status: full
---

Line numbers refer to the pseudocode on the [hybrid A\*](/hybrid-a-star/) page. Hybrid A\* is not complete and not optimal for the continuous problem; Dolgov et al. (2010) say so, and the page gives measured cases. What can be proved is narrower: every path it returns is a path of the car, it always stops, and, when its bins are fine enough, it finds the best sequence of its own motion primitives. The page calls this optimality relative to the discretization.

**Setting.** A pose is $q = (x, y, \theta)$. A primitive is a pair $(\kappa, d)$ with $\kappa \in \{-1/\rho, 0, 1/\rho\}$ and $d \in \{+1, -1\}$ (only $+1$ when reverse motion is off); it drives the arc of curvature $\kappa$ and signed length $d\,\ell$ from $q$. Its cost is $\ell$ for $d = +1$ and $c_r\ell$ for $d = -1$, with $c_r \ge 1$, plus $c_s \ge 0$ when $d$ differs from the direction of the previous primitive. The key of line L3 maps a pose to a bin $(\lfloor x/r\rfloor, \lfloor y/r\rfloor, \lfloor \theta/\Delta\theta\rfloor)$. The goal region is $G = \{q : |q_{xy} - g_{xy}| \le \epsilon_p,\ |\theta - g_\theta| \le \epsilon_\theta\}$.

**Lemma 1 (soundness).** If the search returns a path, the path starts at the start pose, consists of arcs of curvature at most $1/\rho$ in absolute value, passes the collision test of line L9 on every piece, and ends in $G$, or exactly at the goal pose when it ends with a shot. The returned cost equals the cost of the path.

*Proof.* Line L12 stores in each node the parent bin and the primitive that led to it, and the pose of the node is the exact end pose of that primitive from the pose of the parent (the function `move` integrates the arc in closed form). By induction on the order in which nodes are stored, following the parents from any stored node gives a chain of primitives from the start pose to the pose of the node, and the stored $g$ is the sum of their costs, by line L11. A node is stored only after line L9 tests its primitive, so every primitive of the chain passed the test. A node replaced in its bin by line L12 is no longer reachable from the bin, but the chain of the node that replaced it has the same property. Line L6 returns the chain of a node whose pose is in $G$. Line L7 returns the chain followed by a Dubins path, which consists of arcs of curvature $\pm 1/\rho$ and lines, ends exactly at the goal pose (Lemma 2 of the [Dubins path proof](/dubins-path/)), and passes the same test; its cost adds its length and one switch penalty if the chain ended in reverse. $\square$

The collision test samples the arc at spacing at most $r/4$. It is exact for the samples, not for the whole arc: an arc can clip the corner of a blocked cell between two samples. This is a limit of the test, and real implementations dilate the obstacles by a margin for this reason.

**Lemma 2 (termination).** On a map of $n_x \times n_y$ cells, the loop of lines L4 to L12 runs at most $B = n_x n_y n_\theta$ expansions (times $(r_{map}/r)^2$ if the bins are finer than the cells), and then returns.

*Proof.* A pose that line L9 accepts is in a free cell, so its bin is one of the $B$ bins of the map. Line L5 closes a bin when it expands its node, and a closed bin is never expanded again: line L10 skips closed bins, and line L4 skips heap entries of closed bins. So the number of expansions is at most $B$, each pushes at most six entries, and the heap empties after finitely many pops. Line L13 then returns that no path was found. $\square$

**Lemma 3 (the heuristic is consistent).** Let $h(q) = \max(0, |q_{xy} - g_{xy}| - \epsilon_p)$. Then $h \le$ the cost to reach $G$ from $q$, and $h(q) \le c(q, q') + h(q')$ for every primitive from $q$ to $q'$.

*Proof.* A primitive of arc length $\ell$ moves the position by at most $\ell$, and its cost is at least $\ell$. The function $q \mapsto |q_{xy} - g_{xy}|$ is 1-Lipschitz in the position, and so is $h$, so $h(q) - h(q') \le |q_{xy} - q'_{xy}| \le \ell \le c(q, q')$. At a pose of $G$, $h = 0$. Summing along any path to $G$ gives admissibility. $\square$

The grid heuristic of the option `use_grid_heuristic` is the distance on the 8-connected grid of free cells, minus a margin of two cell diagonals. It is not admissible in general, because the 8-connected distance can exceed the Euclidean shortest path by up to about 8%, and Dolgov et al. accept this.

**Theorem (optimality relative to the primitives).** Run the search without shots, with the Euclidean heuristic and $c_s = 0$. Let $Q$ be the set of poses reachable from the start by sequences of primitives of cost at most $C^*$, where $C^*$ is the least cost of a sequence that ends in $G$ and passes the collision test. If the key of line L3 is injective on $Q$, the search returns a path of cost $C^*$.

*Proof.* With $c_s = 0$, the cost of a primitive depends only on its own pose and direction, not on the previous one, so the cost-to-go from a pose does not depend on how the pose was reached. When the key is injective on $Q$, two different poses of $Q$ never share a bin, so line L12 never replaces a pose of $Q$ by a different pose; it only lowers the $g$ of the same pose. Then lines L4 to L12 are A\* on the graph whose vertices are the poses of $Q$ and whose edges are the free primitives, with the consistent heuristic of Lemma 3. Poses outside $Q$ have $g > C^*$, so $f > C^*$, and they are never expanded before a goal pose of cost $C^*$. If a pose outside $Q$ shares a bin with a pose $p$ of $Q$, its $g$ exceeds $C^* \ge g^*(p)$, so line L12 replaces it by $p$ when $p$ is reached with its least cost, and the bin is not closed before that, because its $f$ is above $C^*$ until then. A\* with a consistent heuristic closes each vertex with its least cost and returns the first goal vertex it expands, which has the least cost $C^*$; see [A\* search](/a-star-search/) and [consistent heuristic](/consistent-heuristic/). $\square$

The theorem needs injectivity, and that is the point of the method: with coarse bins, line L12 keeps only one pose per bin, and the dropped pose may be the only one that reaches the goal cheaply, or at all. Then the result costs more than $C^*$, or the search fails while a path exists. The test `test_cost_is_at_least_the_best_primitive_sequence` checks both sides on 12 random goals: with bins of 1 mm and $10^5$ headings, the result equals the least cost found by a uniform-cost search over exact poses, to $10^{-9}$; with 1 m bins and 36 headings, the result is never cheaper. With a positive switch penalty, the bin would also have to keep the direction of motion for the theorem to hold.

**What is not proved.** Completeness and optimality for the continuous problem are false in general. A shot found earlier can be worse than a shot found later, which the page shows by measurement. The grid heuristic is only approximately admissible, so even the relative optimality needs the Euclidean heuristic.
