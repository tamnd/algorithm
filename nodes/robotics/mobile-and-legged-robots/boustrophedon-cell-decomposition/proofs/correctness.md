Line numbers refer to the pseudocode on the [boustrophedon cell decomposition](/boustrophedon-cell-decomposition/) page. The region is the rectangle $R = [0, W] \times [0, H]$, and the obstacles $O_1, \dots, O_k$ are disjoint closed convex polygons in the interior of $R$. The free space is $F = R \setminus \bigcup_j O_j$. General position: each obstacle has one leftmost vertex and one rightmost vertex, and the $2k$ event abscissas are distinct. For an obstacle $O$ and an $x$ in its $x$-range, write $O(x) = [\ell_O(x), u_O(x)]$ for its slice on the vertical line at $x$. A cell $C$ with abscissas $x_0 < x_1$ and walls $f$ and $g$ is the open set $\{(x, y) : x_0 < x < x_1,\ f(x) < y < g(x)\}$, where $f$ is $0$ or $u_O$ for an obstacle $O$, and $g$ is $H$ or $\ell_O$.

**Lemma 1 (the order of the obstacles is fixed between events).** If the $x$-ranges of two obstacles $A$ and $B$ share an open interval $I$, then either $u_A(x) < \ell_B(x)$ for all $x$ in $I$, or $u_B(x) < \ell_A(x)$ for all $x$ in $I$.

*Proof.* $A$ and $B$ are disjoint compact convex sets, so a line $L$ strictly separates them. If $L$ were vertical, at $x = c$, then one $x$-range would be in $x < c$ and the other in $x > c$, and the ranges would not share an open interval. So $L$ is the graph of an affine function $h$, with, say, $A$ below and $B$ above. Then $u_A(x) < h(x) < \ell_B(x)$ for every $x$ in $I$. $\square$

So, on each open interval between two consecutive events, the active obstacles have a fixed order from bottom to top, and their slices are disjoint closed intervals in that order. Write the boundaries in order as $b_0 = 0, O_{(1)}, \dots, O_{(a)}, b_{a+1} = H$. The free slice at such an $x$ is the union of the $a + 1$ open gaps between consecutive boundaries (the lower end of a gap is $0$ or $u$ of the obstacle below, the upper end is $H$ or $\ell$ of the obstacle above), together with the boundary points of the slices, which are a finite set on each line.

**Lemma 2 (the sweep invariant).** After line L2 and after each event of lines L4 and L5, let $e$ be the last event abscissa ($e = 0$ after L2) and let $e'$ be the next one ($e' = W$ after the last event). Then `active` lists the obstacles whose $x$-range contains $(e, e')$, in order from bottom to top, and `gaps` lists $a + 1$ open cells, with $x_0 \le e$, such that the $i$-th cell has the walls of the $i$-th gap. Each of these cells has $x_0$ set and $x_1$ not yet set, and every other cell has $x_1 \le e$.

*Proof.* By induction on the events. After line L2, no obstacle is active, since all obstacles lie in the interior of $R$, and the one cell has walls $0$ and $H$.

IN event of $O$ at $x = e$, with leftmost vertex $v = (e, y_v)$. The point $v$ is on the boundary of $O$, so it is not in another obstacle, and it is not on the boundary of $R$. By Lemma 1 and the induction hypothesis, the active obstacles at $e$ have disjoint slices in their order, and $y_v$ lies in none of them. So $y_v$ lies in exactly one gap: the one with index $p$ equal to the number of active obstacles with $u(e) < y_v$, which line L4 computes. For $x$ slightly larger than $e$, the slice of $O$ is a short interval near $y_v$, inside gap $p$. So gap $p$ splits into the gap from its old lower wall to $\ell_O$ and the gap from $u_O$ to its old upper wall. Line L4 sets $x_1 = e$ on the old cell, opens these two cells, inserts $O$ at index $p$ in `active`, and replaces the old cell by the two new cells in `gaps`. The invariant holds again.

OUT event of $O$ at $x = e$. $O$ is active at index $p$. Gap $p$ lies just below $O$ and gap $p + 1$ just above it. For $x$ slightly larger than $e$, $O$ is gone, and these two gaps become one gap from the lower wall of gap $p$ to the upper wall of gap $p + 1$. Line L5 closes both cells at $e$, opens the merged cell, and replaces the two cells by it. The invariant holds again. $\square$

**Theorem 1 (partition).** The cells are open, pairwise disjoint, and contained in $F$. Every point of $F$ outside a finite union of vertical segments and obstacle edges is in exactly one cell. So the closures of the cells cover $F$, and the areas of the cells add up to the area of $F$.

*Proof.* Take $(x, y)$ with $x$ not an event abscissa and $y$ not on a slice boundary. Let $e < x < e'$ for consecutive events $e$ and $e'$. By Lemma 2, the cells with $x_0 < x < x_1$ are exactly the cells in `gaps` during $(e, e')$: the cells opened later have $x_0 \ge e'$, and the cells closed earlier have $x_1 \le e$. Line L6 closes the last cells at $W$. The open intervals of these cells at $x$ are the gaps, which are disjoint, and their union is the free slice minus the boundary points. So $(x, y)$ is in exactly one cell if it is free, and in no cell if it is in an obstacle. The excluded points are on the $2k$ event lines and on the obstacle edges, which have area zero. $\square$

**Theorem 2 (the count and the graph).** There are exactly $1 + 3k$ cells and $4k$ adjacency edges. The adjacency graph is connected, and two linked cells share a boundary segment of positive length on a vertical line.

*Proof.* Line L2 opens one cell. Each IN event opens two cells and adds two edges, and each OUT event opens one cell and adds two edges. There are $k$ events of each kind, so there are $1 + 2k + k$ cells and $4k$ edges. The two edges of an event join a new cell to a cell opened earlier, and no edge is added twice, since each new cell is linked only at its own event. By induction on the order in which the cells are opened, each cell is joined to cell 0. So the depth-first search of line L7 reaches every cell.

At an IN event at $x = e$, the old cell's interval at $e$ is $(f(e), g(e))$ with $f(e) < y_v < g(e)$. The slice of $O$ at $e$ is the point $y_v$, so the new cells have the intervals $(f(e), y_v)$ and $(y_v, g(e))$ at $e$, and each shares a segment of positive length with the old cell. At an OUT event, the same holds with the rightmost vertex of $O$ and the merged cell. $\square$

**Theorem 3 (the lanes).** For a cell with width $s = x_1 - x_0$ and a tool width $w > 0$, line L8 makes $m = \max(1, \lceil s/w \rceil)$ lanes at $x_i = x_0 + (2i + 1)s/(2m)$ for $i = 0, \dots, m - 1$. Each lane is a nonempty vertical segment inside the closure of the cell, and every $x$ in $[x_0, x_1]$ is within $w/2$ of some $x_i$.

*Proof.* Since $m \ge s/w$, the gap between consecutive lanes is $s/m \le w$, and the first and last lanes are $s/(2m) \le w/2$ from $x_0$ and $x_1$. Each $x_i$ lies strictly between $x_0$ and $x_1$, where the interval of the cell is open and nonempty by Lemma 2, so the lane from $f(x_i)$ to $g(x_i)$ has positive length. The coverage path runs lane $i$ upward when $i$ is even and downward when $i$ is odd, so consecutive lanes of a cell join at the same wall. $\square$

**Remarks.** Theorem 3 bounds the horizontal distance from a free point to the line of a lane. It does not say that the tool, a segment of width $w$ swept along the lane, covers every point: next to a slanted wall, the lane at $x_i$ may end before the wall at $x$ is reached, which leaves a thin wedge. General position is used in Lemma 1 (no shared event abscissa) and in Lemma 2 (one leftmost and one rightmost vertex). The code does not use a balanced tree. Each event scans the active list and slices each active obstacle, so the decomposition takes $O(k \log k + kn)$ steps. The tests check Theorem 1 on 150 random points in each of 12 random scenes, with exact fractions and with points kept off the event lines, and they check the areas against the shoelace formula. They also check the counts of Theorem 2 and the shared segments. Theorem 3 is checked on 8 scenes with three tool widths.
