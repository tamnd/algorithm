A kinematic pair is two rigid bodies that touch each other in a way that leaves only some relative motions possible. A door hinge leaves one rotation, a drawer slide leaves one translation, and a ball joint leaves three rotations about one point. The number of relative motions that remain is the freedom $f$ of the pair. In robotics the pair is called a joint, and the bodies are called links. Every count of the mobility of a mechanism, such as [Grübler's formula](/grubler-formula/), starts from the list of pairs and their freedoms.

## Definition

Fix a frame on each of the two bodies, $\{a\}$ and $\{b\}$. A relative position of the bodies is the pose $T_{ab}$ of $\{b\}$ in $\{a\}$, an element of the [special Euclidean group](/special-euclidean-group/) $SE(3)$ in space, or of $SE(2)$ in the plane. Let $m = 6$ in space and $m = 3$ in the plane, the dimension of that group.

**Kinematic pair.** A kinematic pair is a pair of rigid bodies together with a contact between them that holds for all time. The contact allows a set $K \subset SE(3)$ of relative poses, and $K$ is a smooth submanifold of dimension $f$. The number $f$ is the **freedom** of the pair, and $c = m - f$ is the number of **constraints** it puts on the relative motion.

The constraint is on the relative pose, not on the relative velocity. Constraints on the velocity only, such as rolling without slip, are a different class (see the non-examples).

**Lower and higher pairs.** Reuleaux (1875) called a pair *lower* if the two bodies touch along a surface, and *higher* if they touch along a line or at points. Six surfaces can slide on a copy of themselves and give the six lower pairs:

| pair | symbol | contact surface | relative motion | $f$ | $c$ in space | $c$ in the plane |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| revolute | R | a surface of revolution | rotation about an axis | 1 | 5 | 2 |
| prismatic | P | a prism | translation along a direction | 1 | 5 | 2 |
| helical (screw) | H | a helicoid | rotation with a proportional translation | 1 | 5 | not planar |
| cylindrical | C | a circular cylinder | rotation about and translation along one axis | 2 | 4 | not planar |
| spherical | S | a sphere | all rotations about one point | 3 | 3 | not planar |
| planar | E | a plane | planar motion | 3 | 3 | 0 |

For each lower pair, the set $K$ is a subgroup of $SE(3)$ when $\{a\}$ and $\{b\}$ are chosen to coincide at one allowed pose. For example, the rotations about a fixed axis form a group, and so do the rigid motions of a plane in itself. For R, P and H, $K$ is a one-parameter group $\{e^{[\mathcal{S}]\theta}\}$ generated by a screw axis $\mathcal{S} = (\omega, v)$, with $\omega$ a unit vector and $v = h\omega$ for a helical pair of pitch $h$ through the origin, $h = 0$ for a revolute pair, and $\omega = 0$ with a unit $v$ for a prismatic pair. This is the [screw motion](/screw-motion/) of the joint, and it is the form that the [product of exponentials formula](/product-of-exponentials-formula/) uses.

**Common pairs that are not lower pairs.** The universal joint U (Hooke or Cardan joint) has $f = 2$. It is two revolute pairs whose axes meet at a right angle, joined by a small cross. A cam on a follower and two gear teeth are higher pairs with line contact.

## Examples and non-examples

**A screw.** An ISO M10 coarse thread has a lead of 1.5 mm per turn. As a helical pair it has $f = 1$ and pitch $h = 1.5 / (2\pi) = 0.23873$ mm per radian. A quarter turn, $\theta = \pi/2$, moves the nut by $h\theta = 0.375$ mm along the axis. The angle and the travel are not independent, so the pair has one freedom, not two.

**A hinge in the plane and in space.** A revolute pair leaves 1 of the 3 planar motions of one body against the other, so it removes $c = 3 - 1 = 2$. In space it leaves 1 of 6 and removes $c = 5$. The same pin is counted with $c = 2$ in a planar linkage and with $c = 5$ in a spatial one. Using the wrong $m$ is a common error in mobility counts.

**A universal joint, two ways.** Count a universal joint as one pair with $f = 2$, or as two revolute pairs with the cross as an extra link. The second way adds one link and one joint, so $N - 1 - J$ does not change, and the sum of the freedoms is $1 + 1 = 2$ both ways. So [Grübler's formula](/grubler-formula/) $m(N - 1 - J) + \sum f_i$ gives the same mobility both ways.

**Non-example: a wheel that rolls without slip.** A disc that rolls on the ground has its contact point at rest. This is a condition on the velocity, $v_{contact} = 0$, and it does not cut down the set of reachable relative poses. A coin can roll along a closed path and come back with any heading. Such a contact is a nonholonomic constraint, not a kinematic pair in the sense above, and counting it as a pair with $f = 1$ gives wrong mobility counts.

**Non-example: a block that rests on a table.** Gravity keeps the block down, but nothing stops it from being lifted. The allowed set is given by an inequality, so it has full dimension 6 and is not a pair with a fixed freedom. A pair needs the contact to be kept, either by the shape (a pin in a hole, a ball in a socket) or by a force that is assumed to hold all the time.

## Related definitions

- [Kinematic chain](/kinematic-chain/): links joined by pairs. The pairs are the edges of the chain.
- [Grübler's formula](/grubler-formula/): the mobility of a chain from the number of links, the number of pairs and their freedoms.
- [Screw motion](/screw-motion/) and [rigid body twist](/rigid-body-twist/): the motion that a one-freedom pair allows, and its velocity.
- [Instant center of velocity](/instant-center-of-velocity/): in the plane, a revolute pair is the instant center of its two links, and a prismatic pair puts the center at infinity.
- [Configuration space](/configuration-space/): the configuration space of a robot is built from the sets $K$ of its joints.

### History

Reuleaux (1875) made the pair of elements the basic unit of the theory of machines. He split pairs into lower pairs, with surface contact, and higher pairs, with line or point contact, and he described a machine as a closed chain of pairs. Kennedy translated the book into English in 1876. The six lower pairs listed above are the standard list in later texts on mechanisms. In robotics, the revolute and prismatic pairs are by far the most common joints.
