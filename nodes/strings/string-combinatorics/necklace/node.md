A necklace is a word read around a circle, with no marked starting point: formally, a class of words under [conjugacy](/word-conjugacy/), the relation that identifies a word with each of its rotations. Each necklace is named by its least rotation, so the necklace of `baab` is written `aabb`. Over $k$ letters there are $\frac{1}{n} \sum_{d \mid n} \varphi(d)\, k^{n/d}$ necklaces of length $n$, and the necklaces whose words are [primitive](/primitive-word/) correspond one to one to the [Lyndon words](/lyndon-word/).

## Definition

Let $\Sigma$ be a finite [alphabet](/alphabet/) with $k = |\Sigma|$ letters. The class itself needs only equality of letters; the representative below uses a total order on $\Sigma$ and the [lexicographic order](/lexicographic-order/) on words. Indices are 0-based.

**Necklace.** For $n \ge 1$, the rotation $\mathrm{rot}_i(w) = w[i..n-1]\,w[0..i-1]$ maps $\Sigma^n$ to itself. A *necklace of length $n$* is a conjugacy class
$$ [w] = \{ \mathrm{rot}_i(w) : 0 \le i \le n - 1 \} \subseteq \Sigma^n. $$
In the word-based convention of Ruskey, Savage and Wang and of Knuth, the word $w$ itself is called a necklace when it is the least element of its class, that is, when $w \le \mathrm{rot}_i(w)$ for every $i$. The two conventions name the same objects: each class has exactly one least element.

**Size of a class.** The class $[w]$ has exactly $d$ elements, where $d$ is the length of the primitive root of $w$ (shown on the [conjugacy](/word-conjugacy/) page). A necklace is *aperiodic* (or primitive) when $d = n$, and *periodic* otherwise.

**Aperiodic necklaces and Lyndon words.** A word is a Lyndon word if it is strictly smaller than all its other rotations. The least element of an aperiodic class is a Lyndon word, and the least element of a periodic class $[\rho^{n/d}]$ is $\lambda^{n/d}$, where $\lambda$ is the Lyndon word in the class of $\rho$. So the necklaces of length $n$ correspond to the pairs $(d, \lambda)$ with $d \mid n$ and $\lambda$ a Lyndon word of length $d$.

**Prenecklace.** A word is a *prenecklace* if it is a prefix of some necklace (in the word convention). Prenecklaces are the objects that the [FKM algorithm](/fkm-algorithm/) visits.

**Bracelet.** If reversal of the word is also allowed, the classes are [bracelets](/bracelet/), or turnover necklaces.

**The number of necklaces.** Let $N_k(n)$ be the number of necklaces of length $n$ over $k$ letters. Then
$$ N_k(n) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{d \mid n} \varphi(d)\, k^{n/d}, $$
where $\varphi$ is [Euler's totient](/euler-totient/).

*Proof.* Count the set $P$ of pairs $(w, i)$ with $w \in \Sigma^n$, $0 \le i \le n-1$ and $\mathrm{rot}_i(w) = w$ in two ways. By class: a class of size $d$ has $d$ words, and each of them is fixed by exactly $n/d$ shifts $i$ (the multiples of $d$, by the conjugacy page), so each class contributes $d \cdot n/d = n$ pairs and $|P| = n N_k(n)$. By shift: $\mathrm{rot}_i(w) = w$ means $w[t] = w[(t + i) \bmod n]$ for all $t$, so $w$ is constant on each coset of the subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}_n$ generated by $i$. That subgroup has $n / \gcd(i, n)$ elements and so $\gcd(i, n)$ cosets, and the letters on the cosets are free, so $k^{\gcd(i, n)}$ words are fixed by $i$. The number of $i \in \{0, \ldots, n-1\}$ with $\gcd(i, n) = n/d$ is $\varphi(d)$, for each divisor $d$ of $n$. So $|P| = \sum_{d \mid n} \varphi(d)\, k^{n/d}$, and dividing by $n$ gives the formula. $\square$

This is the [Burnside lemma](/burnside-lemma/) for the cyclic group acting on $\Sigma^n$, written out for this case.

## Examples and non-examples

The binary necklaces of length 4, as least representatives, are `aaaa`, `aaab`, `aabb`, `abab`, `abbb`, `bbbb`. The shift counts for $n = 4$, $k = 2$ are $16, 2, 4, 2$ fixed words for $i = 0, 1, 2, 3$, so $N_2(4) = (16 + 2 + 4 + 2)/4 = 6$.

| $n$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| necklaces $N_2(n)$ | 2 | 3 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 14 | 20 | 36 |
| Lyndon words, $k = 2$ | 2 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 6 | 9 | 18 | 30 |
| bracelets, $k = 2$ | 2 | 3 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 13 | 18 | 30 |
| necklaces $N_3(n)$ | 3 | 6 | 11 | 24 | 51 | 130 | 315 | 834 |
| Lyndon words, $k = 3$ | 3 | 3 | 8 | 18 | 48 | 116 | 312 | 810 |

- **Length 6, binary.** The 14 necklaces are `aaaaaa`, `aaaaab`, `aaaabb`, `aaabab`, `aaabbb`, `aabaab`, `aababb`, `aabbab`, `aabbbb`, `ababab`, `ababbb`, `abbabb`, `abbbbb`, `bbbbbb`. Nine have 6 rotations (the Lyndon words), two have 3 (`aabaab`, `abbabb`), one has 2 (`ababab`), and two have 1. The class sizes sum to $9 \cdot 6 + 2 \cdot 3 + 2 + 2 = 64 = 2^6$.
- **Ternary, length 3.** The 11 necklaces are `aaa`, `aab`, `aac`, `abb`, `abc`, `acb`, `acc`, `bbb`, `bbc`, `bcc`, `ccc`. The words `abc` and `acb` are in different necklaces: reading `acb` around the circle gives `cba`, the reversal of `abc`, so they form one bracelet.
- **Non-example: same letters, different necklace.** `aabb` and `abab` have the same letters, but `abab` is not a rotation of `aabb`.
- **Non-example of the word convention.** `abaab` is not a necklace in the word sense, because its rotation `aabab` is smaller. It is not a prenecklace either: a word that starts with `abaab` has a rotation that starts with `aab`, and that rotation is smaller.
- **A prenecklace that is not a necklace.** `aaba` is a prefix of the necklace `aabab`, but its own rotation `aaab` is smaller, so `aaba` is a prenecklace and not a necklace.

## Related definitions

- **Conjugacy.** The relation whose classes are necklaces; see [conjugacy of words](/word-conjugacy/) and [conjugacy testing](/word-conjugacy-testing/).
- **Least rotation.** Computing the representative of a necklace is the [minimal rotation](/minimal-rotation/) problem, solved in linear time by [Booth's algorithm](/booth-algorithm/) and [Shiloach's algorithm](/shiloach-least-rotation/).
- **Lyndon word.** An aperiodic necklace in the word convention; see [Lyndon word](/lyndon-word/) and the count in the [Witt formula](/witt-formula/).
- **Listing and counting.** [Necklace enumeration](/necklace-enumeration/) lists all necklaces, for instance by the [FKM algorithm](/fkm-algorithm/); [necklace counting](/necklace-counting/) and the [Pólya enumeration theorem](/polya-enumeration-theorem/) count them, also with given letter contents.
- **De Bruijn sequence.** Concatenating the Lyndon words of length dividing $n$ in lexicographic order gives a [de Bruijn sequence](/de-bruijn-sequence/), a cyclic word containing every word of length $n$ once.
- **Bracelet.** A class under rotation and reversal; see [bracelet](/bracelet/).
