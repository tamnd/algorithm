title: Patience diff returns a matching of equal lines, and a longest one when no line repeats

We use the pseudocode of the page, with half-open ranges: a call MATCH works on the old lines $A[alo \ldots ahi - 1]$ and the new lines $B[blo \ldots bhi - 1]$. A *matching* of the range is a set of pairs $(i, j)$ with $A[i] = B[j]$ inside the range, such that for two pairs $(i, j)$ and $(i', j')$ we have $i < i'$ if and only if $j < j'$. Its *length* is its number of pairs.

**Lemma 1 (each call is local and ordered).** A call MATCH on a range appends to $M$ only pairs inside the range, and the pairs that it appends form a matching of the range, appended in increasing order.

*Proof.* By induction on the size $(ahi - alo) + (bhi - blo)$ of the range. Line L2 appends pairs $(alo, blo), (alo + 1, blo + 1), \ldots$ of equal lines from the start of the range, and moves the start past them. Line L3 takes pairs of equal lines from the end of the range and moves the end before them; line L7 appends them after everything else, in increasing order. Between these two parts lies the inner range, and all later pairs of the call are inside it. Line L4 takes pairs of equal lines inside the inner range, and line L5 keeps a subset $S$ that increases in both coordinates. If $S$ is empty, the fallback appends nothing, or a longest matching of the inner range, which is in order. Otherwise line L6 visits $S$ in order: before the anchor $(i, j)$ it calls MATCH on the gap between the previous anchor and $(i, j)$, then appends $(i, j)$, and at the end it calls MATCH on the gap after the last anchor. Each gap is inside the inner range and is strictly smaller than the range of the call, since it leaves out at least the anchor. By induction, each gap call appends a matching of its gap, in order. The gaps come one after the other in both files, separated by the anchors, so all the pairs appended are in increasing order in both coordinates. $\square$

**Theorem 1 (a valid diff).** PATIENCE terminates and returns a matching of $A$ and $B$, and the script of line L8 turns $A$ into $B$.

*Proof.* Each recursive call is on a strictly smaller range, so the recursion is finite, and each call does a finite amount of work. By Lemma 1 applied to the whole range, $M$ is a matching of $A$ and $B$. The helper matching_to_script keeps the pairs of $M$, deletes the other old lines and inserts the other new lines in order, so applying the script to $A$ gives $B$. $\square$

**Lemma 2 (equal first lines).** If $A[alo] = B[blo]$, some longest matching of the range contains the pair $(alo, blo)$. The same holds for the last lines.

*Proof.* Take a longest matching $P$. If neither $alo$ nor $blo$ is in a pair of $P$, add $(alo, blo)$: every other pair is after it in both files, so $P$ grows, which is not possible. If $alo$ is in the pair $(alo, j)$ and $blo$ is in no pair, replace $(alo, j)$ with $(alo, blo)$; it has equal lines, and since $blo \le j$ the order is kept. The case of $blo$ alone is the same. If $(alo, j)$ and $(i, blo)$ are both in $P$ and differ, then $alo < i$ and $j > blo$, so the two pairs cross, which a matching does not allow. So the only remaining case is $(alo, blo) \in P$. The last lines are symmetric. $\square$

**Theorem 2 (no repeated lines).** If no line occurs twice in $A$ and no line occurs twice in $B$, then $M$ is a longest common subsequence of $A$ and $B$, and the script is a [shortest edit script](/shortest-edit-script/), with or without the fallback.

*Proof.* We show that each call appends a longest matching of its range, by induction on the size of the range. By Lemma 2 used once for each step of line L2 and line L3, a longest matching of the range consists of the pairs of these lines plus a longest matching of the inner range. In the inner range, a pair $(i, j)$ with $A[i] = B[j]$ has a line that occurs only once in each file, so also only once in each side of the inner range: line L4 finds every such pair, and only such pairs. A matching of the inner range is then a subset of these pairs that increases in both coordinates, and line L5 takes a longest one, $S$. If $S$ is empty, there is no pair in the inner range, and nothing more is possible. Otherwise, a gap of line L6 contains no pair with equal lines: such a pair would lie in both files strictly between two consecutive anchors of $S$, or before the first or after the last, so adding it to $S$ would give a longer increasing set of pairs. So the gap calls append nothing, and the call appends a longest matching. For the whole range this gives a longest common subsequence, and the script of a longest matching has the least number $|A| + |B| - 2|M|$ of operations. $\square$

**The result is not always minimal.** With repeated lines the anchors can block a longer matching. For $A = [u, a, a, a, v]$ and $B = [a, a, a, u, v]$, line L3 matches $v$, and line L4 finds only $u$, the single line that occurs once on each side of the inner range. The anchor $(1, 4)$, in the numbering from 1, leaves a gap before it that is empty in $A$ and a gap after it that is empty in $B$, so nothing more is matched, and the script has 6 operations. The matching $a, a, a, v$ gives 2.

**Cost.** One call on a range of $s$ lines in total counts the lines with a hash table, in $O(s)$ expected time, sorts the unique pairs, and finds $S$ with patience piles and binary search, in $O(s \log s)$ time. Every call other than a call without anchors appends at least one anchor, and each line is an anchor at most once, so there are at most $n + 1$ calls with anchors and at most $2n + 2$ calls in total, each on at most $n$ lines. This gives the bound $O(n^2 \log n)$ expected. When no line repeats, the proof of Theorem 2 shows that the gap calls find no pairs, so the work is one top call of $O(n \log n)$ plus calls on gaps that are disjoint, which is $O(n \log n)$ in total.
