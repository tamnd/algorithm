We use the pseudocode of the page. $T_1$ and $T_2$ are ordered trees whose nodes have a label $l$ and a value $v$, and $M$ is a matching of some nodes of $T_1$ with nodes of $T_2$ of the same label. We write $p(x)$ for the parent of $x$ and *partner* for the other node of a pair. Line L1 makes the two roots a pair, so from now on we take $T_1$ and $T_2$ to be the trees with the new roots, and we count the new roots as a pair of $M$. The algorithm applies each operation to $T_1$ when it writes it, and $t$ denotes this changing tree. The operations are those of Section 3.2 of the paper: INS adds a leaf as the $k$-th child of a node, DEL removes a leaf, UPD changes a value, and MOV$(a, w, k)$ makes $a$, with its subtree, the $k$-th child of $w$. INS, DEL and MOV of a node do not change the relative order of the other children of any node.

A node $x$ of $T_2$ is *processed* when the loop of line L2 has done lines L3 to L7 for it. Since the loop goes in breadth-first order, a node is processed after its parent, and the children of a node are processed one after the other, from left to right.

**Lemma 1 (what changes a placed node).** A node of $t$ changes its parent only through a MOV of that node. When $x$ is processed, the only nodes that move are the partner of $x$ (line L6) and children of the partner of $x$ whose partners are children of $x$ (line L13). So once a node $x$ is processed, the parent and the value of its partner do not change again during the loop.

*Proof.* DEL and INS act on leaves and move no other node. Line L6 moves the partner $w$ of $x$, and line L13 moves children $a$ of $w$ whose partners are children of $x$, which come later in breadth-first order than $x$ and are not processed yet. A node $x$ processed earlier is neither of these, and the only update to its partner is at line L5 while $x$ is processed. $\square$

**Invariant.** For each node $y$ of $T_2$ whose children have been aligned at line L7, the partners of the children of $y$ that are marked "in order" are children of the partner of $y$, and they stand there in the same order as their partners stand in $T_2$.

**Lemma 2 (FIND-POS).** Let $y$ be a node of $T_2$ that meets the invariant, $w$ its partner and $x$ a child of $y$ that is not marked. If the algorithm puts a node $a$, the new partner or the partner of $x$, as the $k$-th child of $w$ with $k = $ FIND-POS$(x)$ and then marks $x$, the invariant still holds for $y$.

*Proof.* If no sibling of $x$ to its left is marked, $k = 1$ and $a$ becomes the first child of $w$; every marked sibling of $x$ is to its right in $T_2$, and its partner is to the right of $a$. Otherwise let $v$ be the rightmost marked sibling to the left of $x$ and $u$ its partner, which is a child of $w$ by the invariant. Line L15 counts the children of $w$ without $a$ and returns one more than the place of $u$, so after the operation $a$ stands directly after $u$. The marked siblings to the left of $x$ are $v$ and siblings to the left of $v$, whose partners stand before or at $u$. The marked siblings to the right of $x$ are to the right of $v$, so their partners stand after $u$, and so after $a$. The order of the other marked partners does not change, as the operations do not change the relative order of other children. $\square$

**Lemma 3 (ALIGN-CHILDREN).** After ALIGN-CHILDREN$(w, x)$, every child of $x$ whose partner is a child of $w$ is marked, and the invariant holds for $x$.

*Proof.* Line L10 removes the marks of the children of $x$, so no child of $x$ is marked. Line L12 marks the nodes of $S_2$ in the common subsequence; their partners are in $S_1$ in the same order as in $S_2$, and $S_1$ and $S_2$ are in the orders of $t$ and of $T_2$. So the invariant holds. Line L13 moves each other node of $S_1$ and marks its partner, and the invariant still holds by Lemma 2. At the end each node of $S_2$ is marked. $\square$

**Lemma 4 (the loop).** When the loop ends, every node $x$ of $T_2$ has a partner $w$ in $t$ with $l(w) = l(x)$ and $v(w) = v(x)$; if $x$ is not the root, $p(w)$ is the partner of $p(x)$; and the partners of the children of each node $y$ are children of the partner of $y$, in the order of $T_2$. Every operation of the loop can be applied.

*Proof.* Take $x$ when it is processed, with $y = p(x)$ processed before. If $x$ has no partner, line L3 inserts a leaf with the label and the value of $x$ under the partner of $y$, which exists. Else the partner $w$ has the label of $x$ by the definition of a matching, and line L5 gives it the value of $x$. If $p(w)$ is not the partner of $y$, line L6 moves $w$ there. This move is legal: the ancestors of the partner of $y$ are the partners of the ancestors of $y$, by the claim for the nodes processed before, and $x$ is not an ancestor of $y$, so the partner of $y$ is not in the subtree of $w$. The moves of line L13 stay inside one parent and are legal. Each position $k$ is between 1 and the number of children plus 1, since FIND-POS returns 1 or one more than the place of a child that stays. By Lemma 1, the parent and the value fixed now stay until the end of the loop.

The children of $x$ are processed after ALIGN-CHILDREN$(w, x)$ at line L7. By Lemma 3, after it, the children of $x$ whose partners are children of $w$ are marked. Every other child of $x$ is marked when it is processed, by lines L3 or L6, and Lemma 2 keeps the invariant. Line L10 is the only place where marks are removed, and it is done once for each node. So at the end every child of $y$ is marked, and the invariant gives the claim on the order. $\square$

**Lemma 5 (the delete phase).** Line L8 deletes exactly the nodes of $T_1$ that have no partner, each one while it is a leaf.

*Proof.* Let $u$ be a node of $t$ without a partner. Such a node is a node of $T_1$ that $M$ does not match, since all inserted nodes get a partner. A child $c$ of $u$ cannot have a partner: by Lemma 4, the parent of a node with a partner $z$ is the partner of $p(z)$, which is not $u$. So all descendants of $u$ have no partner either. The post-order visits them before $u$ and deletes them, so $u$ is a leaf when it is deleted. Nodes with partners are never deleted. $\square$

**Theorem 1 (a conforming edit script).** The script $E$ of EditScript transforms $T_1$ into a tree isomorphic to $T_2$, and the final matching contains $M$, so $E$ conforms to $M$.

*Proof.* After line L8, by Lemma 5, the nodes of $t$ are the nodes with partners. By Lemma 4 the partner map is one to one and onto $T_2$, keeps labels and values, maps the parent of a node to the parent of its partner, and keeps the order of children. A node of $t$ that is a child of the partner of $y$ has a partner $z$ with $p(z) = y$, so the partner of $y$ has no other children. So the partner map is an isomorphism. The pairs of $M$ are never changed, and the final matching is $M$ plus one pair for each inserted node. $\square$

**Respecting the matching.** We call a script that transforms $T_1$ into a tree isomorphic to $T_2$ *respectful* of $M$ if its final matching, restricted to the nodes of $T_1$, is exactly $M$. So it deletes each node of $T_1$ that $M$ does not match, and it pairs each node of $T_2$ that $M$ does not match with an inserted node. The script of EditScript is respectful. The paper calls a script conforming if its final matching contains $M$; such a script may also pair an unmatched node of $T_1$ with an unmatched node of $T_2$. For $T_1 = r(a)$ and $T_2 = r(b)$ with leaves of the same label and $M = \{(r, r)\}$, the script UPD$(a, b)$ conforms to $M$ and costs compare$(a, b)$, while EditScript writes an insert and a delete, of cost 2. So minimality holds for respectful scripts, or for conforming scripts when $M$ is maximal in the sense of the paper, in which case no such pair is left.

**Lemma 6 (the number of operations).** Let $S_1(u, v)$ be the children of $u$ in $T_1$ whose partners are children of $v$ in $T_2$, in the order of $T_1$, and $S_2(u, v)$ their partners, in the order of $T_2$. The script $E$ has

$$|E| = d_1 + d_2 + c + m + \sum_{(u, v) \in M} \big(|S_1(u, v)| - |\mathrm{LCS}(S_1(u, v), S_2(u, v))|\big),$$

where $d_1$ and $d_2$ are the numbers of nodes of $T_1$ and $T_2$ that $M$ does not match, $c$ is the number of pairs $(a, b)$ of $M$ with $v(a) \ne v(b)$, and $m$ is the number of pairs $(a, b)$ of $M$, other than the roots, with $(p(a), p(b)) \notin M$.

*Proof.* Line L3 inserts one node for each node of $T_2$ without a partner, and line L8 deletes one node for each node of $T_1$ without a partner, by Lemma 5. Line L5 updates a pair of $M$ when the values differ, and never an inserted node, which has the value of its partner. By Lemma 1, when $x$ is processed, its partner $w$ has not moved yet, so $p(w)$ is still its parent in $T_1$, and line L6 moves it exactly when $(p(w), p(x)) \notin M$; if $p(x)$ has an inserted partner, $(p(w), p(x))$ is not in $M$ either. At ALIGN-CHILDREN$(w, x)$ with $(w, x) \in M$, the children of $w$ whose partners are children of $x$ are those of $T_1$, in the order of $T_1$: by Lemma 1, none of them has moved, and the nodes that come into $w$ later are partners of children of $x$ processed after this call. So the sequences of line L11 are $S_1(w, x)$ and $S_2(w, x)$, and line L13 moves $|S_1| - |\mathrm{LCS}|$ nodes. For an inserted $w$, $S_1$ is empty. These are all the operations. $\square$

**Theorem 2 (minimum cost).** With unit costs for INS, DEL and MOV and a cost compare$(v, v')$ for each update, where compare meets the triangle inequality, no script that respects $M$ costs less than $E$.

*Proof.* Take a respectful script $E'$. It contains a delete of each of the $d_1$ unmatched nodes of $T_1$ and an insert for each of the $d_2$ unmatched nodes of $T_2$. For each pair $(a, b)$ of $M$ with $v(a) \ne v(b)$, it updates $a$; the updates of $a$ go from $v(a)$ to $v(b)$, so by the triangle inequality they cost at least compare$(v(a), v(b))$, which is what $E$ pays. For each pair $(a, b)$ with $(p(a), p(b)) \notin M$, the final parent of $a$ is the final partner of $p(b)$, which is not $p(a)$: either $p(b)$ has a partner in $M$ other than $p(a)$, or its final partner is an inserted node. Only a MOV of $a$ changes its parent, so $E'$ moves $a$. For each pair $(u, v)$ of $M$, the nodes of $S_1(u, v)$ are children of $u$ at the start and at the end. Those that $E'$ never moves keep their relative order, since no operation on other nodes changes it, and at the end they stand in the order of their partners in $T_2$. So they form a common subsequence of $S_1$ and $S_2$, and $E'$ moves at least $|S_1| - |\mathrm{LCS}|$ of them. These moved nodes differ from those of the pairs counted in $m$, since their parents are a pair, and they differ between pairs $(u, v)$. Adding up, $E'$ costs at least the cost of $E$ given by Lemma 6. This is Lemma C.1 and Theorem C.2 of the paper. $\square$

**Running time of the code.** The breadth-first and post-order traversals take $O(n)$ for trees with $n$ nodes in total. FIND-POS scans the children of one node, so it costs $O(n)$ for each insert or move. ALIGN-CHILDREN with the dynamic program of the longest common subsequence costs $O(|S_1| |S_2|)$, and the sizes of all the $S_1$ add up to at most $n$. So the total is $O(n^2)$ time and space in the worst case. The paper computes the common subsequence with Myers' algorithm, which takes $O((|S_1| + |S_2|) \cdot (|S_1| - |\mathrm{LCS}|))$, and states $O(n d)$ in total, with $d$ the number of misaligned nodes.

**The matching of FastMatch.** Every pair that fast_match adds passes the test equal at the time it is added. For leaves the test does not change. For internal nodes, all leaf pairs are made before the first internal pair, so the count of common leaves is the final one. So the result is a matching of nodes with the same labels that meets Matching Criteria 1 and 2. The paper shows that, with acyclic labels and Criteria 1 to 3, there is a unique maximal matching of this kind (Theorem 5.2), and states that its matching algorithms give an optimal matching when Criterion 3 holds. This file does not prove that statement.
