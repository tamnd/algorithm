A partition pair of a finite state machine is a pair of partitions $(\pi, \tau)$ of its states such that knowing the block of the present state in $\pi$, and the input, is enough to know the block of the next state in $\tau$. A partition $\pi$ with the substitution property is one for which $(\pi, \pi)$ is a pair: the machine then maps blocks of $\pi$ to blocks of $\pi$, and so contains a smaller machine whose states are the blocks. Hartmanis and Stearns built their structure theory of sequential machines on these two ideas in the 1960s. Partition pairs say which parts of the state a component machine must see to compute its own part of the next state, which makes them the basis of [FSM decomposition](/fsm-decomposition/) and of the early methods of [state assignment](/state-assignment/).

## Definition

**Machine.** $M = (S, I, O, \delta, \lambda)$ is a deterministic, completely specified [Mealy machine](/mealy-machine/) with a finite set of states $S$, inputs $I$, outputs $O$, next-state function $\delta : S \times I \to S$ and output function $\lambda : S \times I \to O$. Partitions of $S$ are ordered by refinement, as in the [partition lattice](/partition-lattice/): $\pi \le \tau$ when each block of $\pi$ lies inside a block of $\tau$. We write $s \equiv_\pi t$ when $s$ and $t$ share a block of $\pi$, $0$ for the partition into single states, and $1$ for the partition with one block.

**Partition pair.** $(\pi, \tau)$ is a partition pair of $M$ if for all states $s, t$ and every input $x$,
$$s \equiv_\pi t \implies \delta(s, x) \equiv_\tau \delta(t, x).$$
Equivalently, there is a function $\delta_{\pi\tau}$ from blocks of $\pi$ and inputs to blocks of $\tau$ with $\delta_{\pi\tau}(B_\pi(s), x) = B_\tau(\delta(s, x))$, where $B_\pi(s)$ is the block of $\pi$ that holds $s$.

**Substitution property.** $\pi$ has the substitution property, or is an SP partition, if $(\pi, \pi)$ is a partition pair. Then the blocks of $\pi$ with the map $\delta_{\pi\pi}$ form a machine $M/\pi$, the quotient of $M$ by $\pi$. In algebraic terms, an SP partition is a congruence of the machine.

**Output consistency.** $\pi$ is output consistent if $s \equiv_\pi t$ implies $\lambda(s, x) = \lambda(t, x)$ for every input $x$.

**The operators m and M.** For a partition $\pi$, let $m(\pi)$ be the finest $\tau$ such that $(\pi, \tau)$ is a pair, and for a partition $\tau$ let $M(\tau)$ be the coarsest $\pi$ such that $(\pi, \tau)$ is a pair. Both exist:

- $M(\tau)$ is the relation "$s \sim t$ when $\delta(s, x) \equiv_\tau \delta(t, x)$ for every $x$". It is an [equivalence relation](/equivalence-relation/), so a partition, and by the definition every $\pi$ with $(\pi, \tau)$ a pair satisfies $\pi \le M(\tau)$.
- $m(\pi)$ is the partition generated by the pairs $\{\delta(s, x), \delta(t, x)\}$ for $s \equiv_\pi t$ and $x \in I$, that is, the finest partition in which each such pair shares a block. A partition $\tau$ makes $(\pi, \tau)$ a pair exactly when it puts each such pair in one block, so exactly when $m(\pi) \le \tau$.

**Properties.** All of the following follow from the definition.

1. *Galois connection.* $(\pi, \tau)$ is a pair if and only if $m(\pi) \le \tau$, if and only if $\pi \le M(\tau)$. Hence $m$ and $M$ are monotone.
2. *Monotonicity.* If $(\pi, \tau)$ is a pair, $\pi' \le \pi$ and $\tau \le \tau'$, then $(\pi', \tau')$ is a pair. The pairs $(0, \tau)$ and $(\pi, 1)$ are always pairs.
3. *Meets and joins.* If $(\pi_1, \tau_1)$ and $(\pi_2, \tau_2)$ are pairs, so are $(\pi_1 \wedge \pi_2, \tau_1 \wedge \tau_2)$ and $(\pi_1 \vee \pi_2, \tau_1 \vee \tau_2)$. For the meet, $s \equiv t$ in both $\pi_i$ gives $\delta(s, x) \equiv \delta(t, x)$ in both $\tau_i$. For the join, $s \equiv_{\pi_1 \vee \pi_2} t$ means a chain $s = s_0, s_1, \ldots, s_k = t$ in which each step stays in a block of $\pi_1$ or of $\pi_2$; each step of the image chain $\delta(s_i, x)$ then stays in a block of $\tau_1$ or $\tau_2$, so the ends share a block of $\tau_1 \vee \tau_2$.
4. *The SP lattice.* By property 3 with $\pi_i = \tau_i$, the SP partitions are closed under meet and join, so they form a sublattice of the partition lattice that holds $0$ and $1$. $\pi$ is SP if and only if $m(\pi) \le \pi$.
5. *Generators.* For two states $s \ne t$, let $\rho_{st}$ be the finest SP partition in which $s$ and $t$ share a block. It is found by starting from the partition that merges only $s$ and $t$ and replacing $\pi$ by $\pi \vee m(\pi)$ until nothing changes, which ends after at most $|S| - 1$ merges. Every SP partition $\pi$ is the join of the $\rho_{st}$ over the pairs $s \equiv_\pi t$: each such $\rho_{st}$ is at most $\pi$, since $\pi$ is SP and merges $s$ and $t$, and their join merges every pair that $\pi$ merges. So the at most $\binom{|S|}{2}$ partitions $\rho_{st}$ generate the whole SP lattice, which is how programs list the SP partitions of a machine.
6. *State equivalence.* The partition of $S$ into classes of equivalent states, the one that [DFA minimization](/dfa-minimization/) and [Moore's algorithm](/moore-minimization/) compute, is the coarsest partition that is both SP and output consistent. It is SP and output consistent because equivalent states give equal outputs now and move to equivalent states. Conversely, if $\pi$ is SP and output consistent and $s \equiv_\pi t$, then by induction on the length of an input word the two runs from $s$ and from $t$ stay in one block of $\pi$ and so give equal outputs at every step; hence $s$ and $t$ are equivalent. This is the machine form of the [Myhill-Nerode theorem](/myhill-nerode-theorem/).

## Examples and non-examples

**A counter with enable.** Let $S = \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$, $I = \{0, 1\}$ and $\delta(s, e) = s + e \bmod 4$.

- The parity partition $\pi_2 = \{0, 2\}\{1, 3\}$ is SP: if $s \equiv t \pmod 2$ then $s + e \equiv t + e \pmod 2$. The quotient $M/\pi_2$ is a counter modulo 2.
- $\tau = \{0, 1\}\{2, 3\}$ is not SP: the states 0 and 1 share a block, but with $e = 1$ they go to 1 and 2, which do not. Here $m(\tau) = 1$, since the pairs $\{1, 2\}$ and $\{3, 0\}$ together with the blocks of $\tau$ join everything, and $M(\tau) = 0$, since every two distinct states are told apart by some input. So the only pairs with $\tau$ in them are the trivial ones $(0, \tau)$ and $(\tau, 1)$.
- More generally, for the counter modulo $n$ with enable, the SP partitions are exactly the partitions into cosets of a subgroup of the [cyclic group](/cyclic-group/) $\mathbb{Z}_n$. If $\pi$ is SP, then $s \equiv_\pi t$ implies $s + 1 \equiv_\pi t + 1$, so $\pi$ is invariant under translation; the block $H$ of 0 is then closed under addition (from $a \equiv 0$, translating by $b$ gives $a + b \equiv b$, and $b \equiv 0$), so it is a subgroup, and $s \equiv_\pi t$ holds exactly when $t - s \in H$. Conversely, a coset partition is invariant under translation. Modulo 4 there are three SP partitions, $0$, $\pi_2$ and $1$; modulo 6 there are four, from the subgroups $\{0\}$, $\{0, 3\}$, $\{0, 2, 4\}$ and $\mathbb{Z}_6$.

**A shift register.** Let the states be the bit pairs $(a, b)$, the input a bit $x$, and $\delta((a, b), x) = (x, a)$. Let $\pi_a$ group the states by $a$ and $\pi_b$ by $b$.

- $(1, \pi_a)$ is a pair: the new first bit is the input, whatever the state.
- $(\pi_a, 0)$ is a pair: the next state $(x, a)$ depends only on $a$ and the input. So $\pi_a$ is SP, and $m(\pi_a) = 0$.
- $\pi_b$ is not SP: $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 0)$ share a block of $\pi_b$ but go to $(x, 0)$ and $(x, 1)$, which do not. Here $m(\pi_b) = \pi_a = m(1)$: knowing $b$ tells nothing more about the next state than the input alone does.

The chain $1 \to \pi_a \to 0$ of pairs says that the register can be built from a first component that needs only the input and a second that needs only the first. This is the serial decomposition of [FSM decomposition](/fsm-decomposition/).

**The order matters.** $(\tau, 1)$ is a pair for every $\tau$, but $(1, \tau)$ need not be. In the counter, $(1, \pi_2)$ is not a pair: with $e = 0$ the states 0 and 1 stay at 0 and 1, which are in different blocks of $\pi_2$.

## Related definitions

- **Partition lattice and equivalence relation.** A partition of $S$ is an equivalence relation on $S$, and refinement is inclusion of relations; meet and join are as in the [partition lattice](/partition-lattice/).
- **FSM decomposition.** An SP partition $\pi$ and a partition $\tau$ with $\pi \wedge \tau = 0$ give a serial decomposition, and two SP partitions with meet $0$ give a parallel decomposition; see [FSM decomposition](/fsm-decomposition/), which proves both.
- **State assignment.** Hartmanis used SP partitions and partition pairs to choose state codes in which each bit depends on few others: a state variable that encodes the blocks of an SP partition has next-state functions that do not depend on the other variables. The later encoders [KISS](/kiss-symbolic-minimization/), [NOVA](/nova-state-encoding/) and [MUSTANG](/mustang-state-assignment/) aim at two-level or multilevel logic instead; see [state assignment](/state-assignment/).
- **Minimization.** The coarsest output-consistent SP partition is state equivalence (property 6), computed by [partition refinement](/partition-refinement/) as in [Hopcroft's algorithm](/hopcroft-minimization/). For machines with unspecified entries, the classes of compatible states may overlap, so covers take the place of partitions; see [incompletely specified FSM minimization](/incompletely-specified-fsm-minimization/).
- **Krohn-Rhodes theory.** The quotient by an SP partition is a homomorphic image of the machine and the front part of a serial decomposition. The [Krohn-Rhodes theorem](/krohn-rhodes-theorem/) states that every finite machine is covered by a cascade of components that are simple groups or two-state reset machines; partitions alone are not enough to reach that result, and its proofs use covers whose blocks may overlap.
