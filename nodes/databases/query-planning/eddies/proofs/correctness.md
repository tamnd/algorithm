---
title: An eddy with timestamped SteMs outputs each row of the filtered join exactly once, for every arrival order and every routing policy
proves: [correctness]
status: full
---

**Setting.** The query is a natural join of relations $R_1, \ldots, R_n$ with filters, each filter a predicate on one relation. The join graph has a node for each relation and an edge between two relations that share an attribute, and it is connected (the code rejects other queries). The answer is the bag $A$ of tuples $\gamma = (r_1, \ldots, r_n)$, one base tuple $r_j$ from each $R_j$, such that every filter holds on its tuple and $r_i$ and $r_j$ agree on their shared attributes for each pair $i, j$. Base tuples are distinct objects, so equal values in two tuples still give two results.

The base tuples arrive one at a time in an arbitrary order, and the $m$-th arrival gets timestamp $m$ and is built into the SteM of its relation at once (line L2). Arrivals and routing steps interleave in an arbitrary way: at each step the eddy either takes the next arrival or routes one waiting tuple (lines L1 and L4), so tuples of earlier arrivals can still be in the eddy when later tuples arrive. A tuple in the eddy is a partial result $t = (P, D, \tau)$: a set $P$ of base tuples from a set $S(P)$ of relations, the set $D$ of filters it has passed, and the timestamp $\tau$ of the base tuple whose arrival started it (line L3). An operator is eligible for $t$ (line L5) if it is a filter on a relation in $S(P)$ that is not in $D$, or the SteM of a relation $R_j \notin S(P)$ that shares an attribute with a relation in $S(P)$. The routing policy may choose any eligible operator (line L7); it may use randomness and the whole history. A filter passes $t$ on with the filter added to $D$, or drops it (line L8). A probe into the SteM of $R_j$ returns the base tuples $s$ of $R_j$ with timestamp less than $\tau$ that agree with $P$ on the attributes $R_j$ shares with $S(P)$, and adds $P \cup \{s\}$ for each such $s$ (line L9). A tuple with no eligible operator is output (line L6).

**Lemma 1 (invariant).** For every tuple $(P, D, \tau)$ in the queue: the base tuples of $P$ agree on all shared attributes; every filter in $D$ holds on its tuple; $P$ contains the base tuple $b$ with timestamp $\tau$; and every other tuple of $P$ has timestamp less than $\tau$.

*Proof.* By induction on the steps. The tuple of line L3 is $(\{b\}, \emptyset, \mathrm{ts}(b))$. A filter adds itself to $D$ only when it holds. A probe adds a tuple $s$ that agrees with $P$ on all attributes it shares with $S(P)$, and whose timestamp is less than $\tau$. $\square$

**Lemma 2 (output is complete).** A tuple with no eligible operator has $S(P)$ equal to all relations and $D$ equal to all filters, so by Lemma 1 it is a row of $A$.

*Proof.* If some relation is not in $S(P)$, then, because the join graph is connected, some relation outside $S(P)$ shares an attribute with one inside, and its SteM is eligible. So $S(P)$ is all relations, and then every filter not in $D$ is eligible. $\square$

**Theorem 1.** For every arrival order and every routing policy, the eddy outputs every row of $A$ exactly once and nothing else.

*Proof.* By Lemma 2, every output is in $A$. Now fix $\gamma = (r_1, \ldots, r_n) \in A$, and let $b$ be its base tuple with the largest timestamp $\tau$.

*At least once.* When $b$ arrives, all other tuples of $\gamma$ have arrived and are in their SteMs, with timestamps less than $\tau$, and they stay there. Follow the tuple $(\{b\}, \emptyset, \tau)$ and, at each step, the one queued tuple whose $P$ is a subset of $\gamma$. When the policy chooses a filter, the filter holds on the part of $\gamma$ it tests, so the tuple goes on. When it chooses the SteM of $R_j$, the tuple $r_j$ of $\gamma$ agrees with $P$ (because $\gamma$ is a join row) and has timestamp less than $\tau$, so the probe returns it and queues $P \cup \{r_j\}$. Each step adds a relation or a filter, so after finitely many steps the tuple has no eligible operator and has $P = \gamma$; it is output.

*At most once.* An output of $\gamma$ comes from a tuple started by some base tuple $b'$ of $\gamma$ (Lemma 1). If $b' \ne b$, then $b$ is in $P$ with timestamp $\tau > \mathrm{ts}(b')$, which contradicts Lemma 1. So every output of $\gamma$ comes from the routing that started at the arrival of $b$. In that routing, each queued tuple is routed to exactly one operator, and each operator queues each successor at most once: a filter queues one tuple, and a probe queues one tuple for each distinct matching SteM entry. So the queued tuples form a tree under the parent relation "was produced from", and the tuples whose $P$ is a subset of $\gamma$ form a single path from the root, because a probe into $R_j$ produces at most one tuple whose new part is $r_j$. So $\gamma$ is output at most once. $\blacksquare$

**Why the timestamps matter.** Take $R(a, b) = \{r_1\}$ and $S(b, c) = \{s_1\}$ with $r_1$ and $s_1$ equal on $b$, and let $r_1$ and then $s_1$ arrive before any routing step. Without the test $\mathrm{ts}(s) < \tau$, the tuple of $r_1$ probes the SteM of $S$ and finds $s_1$, and the tuple of $s_1$ probes the SteM of $R$ and finds $r_1$, so the result is output twice. With the test, only the tuple of $s_1$, the later arrival, finds its partner. The tests of the code show this case and also random cases where results repeat without timestamps. If each arrival is routed to the end before the next one arrives, the test never removes anything, because a SteM then holds only earlier tuples; it is needed because a pipelined eddy keeps old tuples in flight.

**Termination and work.** Each routing step adds a relation or a filter to the tuple, so a tuple is routed at most $n + f$ times, where $f$ is the number of filters. Each probe returns finitely many tuples, so the tuples started by one arrival form a finite tree, and the run ends after all arrivals have come and the eddy is empty. The total number of probe results over the run is the number of partial join results the chosen orders build, and this is the quantity a good policy keeps small.

**The lottery policy.** The lottery policy gives an operator a ticket when it receives a tuple and takes one away for each tuple it returns, and draws among the eligible operators with probability proportional to the tickets. A filter that drops a fraction $1 - \sigma$ of its input gains about $(1 - \sigma)$ tickets per tuple, so the most selective filters gain tickets fastest and are tried first. This is a heuristic: Theorem 1 holds for every policy, but no bound on the work of the lottery policy is proved here. The tests measure it on a two filter query, where it does close to the work of the better fixed order.
