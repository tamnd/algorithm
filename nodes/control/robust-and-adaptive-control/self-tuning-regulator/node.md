A self-tuning regulator (STR) is an adaptive controller that repeats two steps at every sample: it updates an estimate of a plant model from the latest input and output, and it computes the controller from the estimate as if the estimate were exact ([certainty equivalence](/certainty-equivalence/)). The estimator is usually the [RLS algorithm](/rls-algorithm/); the RLS page covers the estimator alone, and this page covers the closed loop that wraps a control design around it, where the data are generated by the controller that the estimate defines. This page treats the direct minimum variance self-tuner of Åström and Wittenmark (1973). Its main result is the self-tuning property: if the estimates converge, the limit controller gives the minimum output variance, even though the least squares estimates of a plant with coloured noise are biased.

## In plain words

The regulator keeps a simple model of the plant and keeps improving it. At each sample it measures the output, checks how wrong the model's last prediction was, and corrects the model a little. Then it computes the input that, by the corrected model, would bring the next output as close to zero as possible, and it applies that input. The model is never assumed to be exact; the regulator just acts as if it were, and fixes the model again at the next sample. For the noise regulation problem on this page, this simple loop finds the best possible controller, even though the model it uses is too simple to describe the noise.

## The problem

The plant is an [ARMAX model](/armax-model/) with a delay of one sample,

$$y_t = -a_1 y_{t-1} - \dots - a_n y_{t-n} + b_1 u_{t-1} + \dots + b_m u_{t-m} + e_t + c_1 e_{t-1} + \dots + c_k e_{t-k},$$

where $e_t$ is white noise with variance $\sigma^2$, the polynomial $C(q) = q^k + c_1 q^{k-1} + \dots + c_k$ has all zeros inside the unit circle, and $B(q) = b_1 q^{m-1} + \dots + b_m$ has all zeros inside the unit circle (the plant is minimum phase). The coefficients are unknown. The aim is to minimize the output variance $E[y_t^2]$. The controller that does this, when the plant is known, is the minimum variance controller: it makes the one step ahead prediction $\hat y_{t+1|t}$ equal to zero, so that $y_{t+1}$ is the prediction error $e_{t+1}$, and the minimum variance is $\sigma^2$.

## The algorithm

The direct self-tuner does not estimate $A$, $B$ and $C$. It estimates the parameters of the controller directly, from the model

$$y_{t+1} - \beta_0 u_t = s_0 y_t + \dots + s_{n_s} y_{t-n_s} + r_1 u_{t-1} + \dots + r_{n_r} u_{t-n_r} + \varepsilon_{t+1} = \varphi_t^T \theta + \varepsilon_{t+1},$$

where $\beta_0$ is a fixed guess of $b_1$ with the correct sign, and $\theta = (s_0, \dots, s_{n_s}, r_1, \dots, r_{n_r})$. The control sets the predicted output of this model to zero.

```pseudo id=main
STR-MV(β₀, θ₀, p₀, λ, N)                         ▷ λ ∈ (0, 1] is the forgetting factor
L1  θ̂ ← θ₀; P ← p₀ I
L2  for t ← 0 to N - 1 do
L3      measure y_t
L4      z ← y_t - β₀ u_{t-1}                       ▷ the regression target
L5      K ← P φ_{t-1} / (λ + φ_{t-1}ᵀ P φ_{t-1})
L6      θ̂ ← θ̂ + K (z - φ_{t-1}ᵀ θ̂)
L7      P ← (P - K φ_{t-1}ᵀ P) / λ
L8      u_t ← -φ_tᵀ θ̂ / β₀                          ▷ certainty equivalence: predicted y_{t+1} = 0
L9  return θ̂
```

Lines L4 to L7 are one step of [recursive least squares](/rls-algorithm/). Line L8 is the control design; for this problem it is one division. In an indirect self-tuner, line L8 is replaced by a full design from an estimated plant model, for example the polynomial equation of [adaptive pole placement](/adaptive-pole-placement/).

## Example

Take the first order plant $y_{t+1} = 0.9\,y_t + 0.5\,u_t + e_{t+1} + 0.5\,e_t$ with $\sigma = 1$, so $a_1 = -0.9$, $b_1 = 0.5$ and $c_1 = 0.5$. The open loop has the pole $0.9$ and the variance $(1 + c_1^2 - 2a_1c_1)/(1 - a_1^2) = 2.15/0.19 = 11.3$. The prediction is $\hat y_{t+1|t} = 0.9\,y_t + 0.5\,u_t + 0.5\,e_t$. Under the minimum variance law $e_t = y_t$, so the law is $u_t = -2.8\,y_t$, and then $y_t = e_t$ exactly.

The self-tuner uses $n_s = 0$, $n_r = 0$, so it estimates one number $s_0$ in $y_{t+1} - \beta_0 u_t = s_0 y_t$, and it applies $u_t = -(s_0/\beta_0) y_t$. The ideal value of $s_0/\beta_0$ is $2.8$. The start is $\theta_0 = 0$, $p_0 = 100$, $\lambda = 1$ and $N = 5000$; the variance is taken over $t \ge 1000$. These are measurements for one noise sequence (seed 0).

| $\beta_0$ | $s_0/\beta_0$ at $N$ | output variance |
|---|---|---|
| 0.25 | 2.803 | 1.014 |
| 0.5 | 2.780 | 1.014 |
| 1 | 2.620 | 1.031 |
| 2 | 2.104 | 1.171 |

With $\beta_0 = 1$ and the seeds 0 to 7, $s_0$ at $N = 5000$ is between 2.62 and 2.785, the output variance is between 0.97 and 1.04, and the lag one autocorrelation of $y$ is at most 0.13 in absolute value. The variance is flat near the optimum: the gain 2.62 gives the closed loop pole $-0.41$ and the variance $(1 + 0.25 - 2 \cdot 0.41 \cdot 0.5)/(1 - 0.41^2) = 1.010$, only 1 percent above the minimum. The open loop variance measured on one sequence of 3500 samples is 12.3.

## Correctness

The proof in [proofs/correctness.md](proofs/correctness.md) shows two facts. First, the parameter $\theta^*$ of the minimum variance controller is a fixed point of the least squares estimator in closed loop: when the regulator uses $\theta^*$, the output is $y_t = e_t$, the residual $y_{t+1} - \beta_0 u_t - \varphi_t^T\theta^*$ is $e_{t+1}$, and this is uncorrelated with $\varphi_t$, so the normal equations hold at $\theta^*$. Second (the self-tuning property, a theorem of Åström and Wittenmark), if $\hat\theta_t$ converges and the closed loop signals are bounded in mean square, the limit makes the sample correlations $r_y(\tau)$ for $\tau = 1, \dots, n_s + 1$ and $r_{yu}(\tau)$ for $\tau = 1, \dots, n_r$ zero; when $n_s$ and $n_r$ are large enough, this forces the minimum variance controller. Convergence itself is a separate and harder theorem: Ljung (1977) proved convergence with probability one under a positive real condition on $C$, with the method of an associated differential equation.

## Complexity

With $d = n_s + 1 + n_r$ parameters, one RLS step costs $O(d^2)$ operations in the real-RAM model and the control law costs $O(d)$. So $N$ samples cost $O(N d^2)$, and the memory is the $d \times d$ matrix $P$, $O(d^2)$. An indirect self-tuner adds the cost of the design, for example solving a polynomial equation of size $O(n)$ by Gaussian elimination in $O(n^3)$ at each sample.

## Properties

- **Self-tuning property.** Theorem (Åström and Wittenmark 1973): if the estimates converge, the limit controller is the minimum variance controller, for a model with enough parameters. The bias of least squares under coloured noise does not matter, because the controller, not the plant, is estimated.
- **Convergence.** Theorem (Ljung 1977): convergence with probability one holds under a positive real condition on the noise polynomial $C$. Without it, the estimates can fail to converge.
- **Choice of $\beta_0$.** Only the sign of $\beta_0$ must be correct for the fixed point, but the speed of convergence depends on the ratio $b_1/\beta_0$. The table shows faster convergence for $\beta_0$ near or below $b_1$ (measurement).
- **Not a dual controller.** The regulator ignores the uncertainty of the estimate when it chooses the input. It does not add probing signals, so the estimates can stop learning when the loop is quiet.
- **Minimum phase plants only.** The minimum variance law cancels the zeros of $B$. An unstable zero gives an unbounded input. Indirect self-tuners with pole placement avoid this.

## Variants and improvements

- **Indirect self-tuners.** Estimate $A$ and $B$ by RLS and solve a design problem at each sample, for example [adaptive pole placement](/adaptive-pole-placement/) or a linear quadratic design. This allows non-minimum phase plants.
- **Generalized minimum variance.** Add a penalty on the input to the cost, which limits the control effort and can handle some non-minimum phase plants (Clarke and Gawthrop).
- **Forgetting.** A forgetting factor $\lambda < 1$ tracks slowly changing parameters. Without excitation, $P$ then grows without bound (estimator windup); constant trace or directional forgetting limits this.
- **Predictive self-tuners.** Generalized predictive control and [model predictive control](/model-predictive-control/) with an online model use a cost over a longer horizon.
- **Model reference adaptive control.** [MRAC](/model-reference-adaptive-control/) is the continuous-time, direct counterpart with a Lyapunov design in place of least squares.

## When to use it

Use a self-tuning regulator for a sampled process with slowly varying dynamics and a noise disturbance, where a model of fixed structure is acceptable, for example a process control loop whose gain drifts with the production rate. Use the minimum variance form only for minimum phase plants with a known delay. For a plant with a known operating schedule, [gain scheduling](/gain-scheduling/) is often simpler, and for a one-time tuning, [relay autotuning](/relay-autotuning/) of a [PID controller](/pid-controller/) is the usual industrial choice.

## Pitfalls

- **Wrong delay.** The law assumes a delay of one sample. With a longer delay, the one step predictor is wrong and the loop can go unstable; use the $d$ step predictor.
- **Wrong sign of $\beta_0$.** The fixed point then has the wrong sign of the gain.
- **Estimator windup.** With forgetting and a quiet loop, $P$ grows, and a later disturbance gives a large jump of the estimate.
- **Bursting.** Without excitation the estimates can drift slowly until the closed loop becomes unstable, then the burst of data corrects them, and the cycle repeats.
- **Non-minimum phase zeros.** Sampling a continuous plant of relative degree at least 3 with a short sampling period $h$ creates zeros outside the unit circle (a theorem of Åström, Hagander and Sternby, 1984); the minimum variance law then gives an unbounded input.

## History

Kalman (1958) built a self-optimizing controller that combined least squares estimation of a plant model with a control design, an early form of the idea. Åström and Wittenmark (1973) proposed the self-tuning regulator with least squares estimation and minimum variance control and proved the self-tuning property. Ljung (1977) proved convergence with an ordinary differential equation that describes the average behaviour of the estimates. Clarke and Gawthrop added a penalty on the control signal, and many industrial self-tuners followed in the late 1970s and the 1980s.
