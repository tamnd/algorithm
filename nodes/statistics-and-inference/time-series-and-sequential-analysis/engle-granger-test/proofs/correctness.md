---
title: Engle-Granger computes the residual Dickey-Fuller t ratio, the ratio is invariant to affine changes of the series and pivotal under a Gaussian null, and the levels slope is superconsistent
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

We prove that lines L1 to L4 compute the least squares residuals and their Dickey-Fuller t ratio (Lemmas 1 and 2), that $\tau$ is unchanged by the affine maps listed on the page (Lemma 3), that under a null of Gaussian random walks the law of $\tau$ depends only on $N$, $n$, $p$ and the case, so a Monte Carlo p-value from independent walks is exact (Theorem 4), and that in the bivariate cointegrated model with an exogenous random walk regressor $n(\hat\beta - \beta)$ is bounded in probability (Theorem 5). Theorem 5 uses one cited fact, the functional central limit theorem for the denominator. We do not prove the limit law of $\tau$ under the null (Phillips and Ouliaris 1990), the general superconsistency result with an endogenous regressor (Stock 1987), the consistency of the test, or the accuracy of the MacKinnon (2010) response surface, which is itself a fit to simulations.

**Lemma 1 (line L1).** Let $Z$ be the $n \times k$ matrix with the deterministic columns and the $m$ regressor columns, of full column rank $k$, and let $Z = QR$ be its thin QR factorization, which modified Gram-Schmidt computes. Then $\hat b = R^{-1}Q^\top y$ minimizes $\lVert y - Zb\rVert$, and the residual is $\hat u = My$ with $M = I - QQ^\top$, the orthogonal projection onto the complement of the column span of $Z$.

*Proof.* For any $b$, $y - Zb = (y - QQ^\top y) + Q(Q^\top y - Rb)$, and the two terms are orthogonal because $Q^\top(I - QQ^\top) = 0$. So $\lVert y - Zb\rVert^2 = \lVert My\rVert^2 + \lVert Q^\top y - Rb\rVert^2$, which is smallest when $Rb = Q^\top y$. $R$ is upper triangular with positive diagonal when $Z$ has full rank, so $\hat b = R^{-1}Q^\top y$ is the unique minimizer, and $y - Z\hat b = My$. When a column is in the span of the earlier ones, its Gram-Schmidt remainder is 0, and the implementation stops with an error. $\square$

**Lemma 2 (lines L2 to L4).** Let $W$ be the matrix with columns $\hat u_{t-1}$ and $\nabla\hat u_{t-i}$, $i = 1..p$, for $t = p + 2..n$, and let $r = \nabla\hat u$ on the same rows. If $W$ has full rank $p + 1$, line L4 returns $\tau = \hat\rho/(s\sqrt{[(W^\top W)^{-1}]_{11}})$ with $s^2 = \lVert r - W\hat c\rVert^2/(T - p - 1)$ and $T = n - p - 1$. This is the ordinary least squares t ratio of the first coefficient.

*Proof.* By Lemma 1 applied to $W$, $\hat c = R_W^{-1}Q_W^\top r$ is the least squares fit and $\hat\rho = \hat c_1$. Since $W^\top W = R_W^\top R_W$, $(W^\top W)^{-1} = R_W^{-1}R_W^{-\top}$, whose $(1, 1)$ entry is the squared norm of the first row of $R_W^{-1}$. The implementation computes $R_W^{-1}$ by back substitution and takes $\operatorname{se}(\hat\rho) = s\lVert(R_W^{-1})_{1,\cdot}\rVert$. $\square$

**Lemma 3 (invariance).** Let $c \ne 0$, let $a$ be in the span of the deterministic columns, and let $d \in \mathbb{R}^m$. Replacing $y$ by $a + cy + Xd$, where $X$ holds the regressor columns, or replacing $X$ by $XA + \mathbf 1 g^\top$ for an invertible $m \times m$ matrix $A$ and any $g$, leaves $\tau$ unchanged.

*Proof.* The first map changes the residual to $M(a + cy + Xd) = cMy$, since $a$ and $Xd$ lie in the span of $Z$, which $M$ annihilates. The second map does not change the span of $Z$, so it does not change $M$ or $\hat u$. In lines L2 and L3 every column of $W$ and the response $r$ are linear in $\hat u$, so multiplying $\hat u$ by $c$ multiplies $\hat c$ by 1, the residual of line L3 by $c$, $s$ by $|c|$ and $(W^\top W)^{-1}$ by $c^{-2}$. Hence $\hat\rho$ is unchanged and $\operatorname{se}(\hat\rho)$ is unchanged. $\square$

**Theorem 4 (an exact pivot under a Gaussian null).** Let $(y_t, x_t^\top)^\top = \sum_{s \le t}\eta_s$, with $\eta_s$ iid $N(0, \Sigma)$ in $\mathbb{R}^{N}$ and $\Sigma$ positive definite. Then for fixed $n$, $p$ and case, the law of $\tau$ does not depend on $\Sigma$. So $\tau$ has the same law as for $N$ independent standard Gaussian walks, and the rank of the observed $\tau$ among $R$ simulated values from independent walks gives an exact Monte Carlo p-value.

*Proof.* Write $\Sigma$ in blocks, with $\Sigma_{xx}$ the $m \times m$ block of the regressors. Let $d = \Sigma_{xx}^{-1}\Sigma_{xy}$ and $\omega^2 = \Sigma_{yy} - \Sigma_{yx}\Sigma_{xx}^{-1}\Sigma_{xy} > 0$. For a Gaussian vector, $\eta^y_s - d^\top\eta^x_s$ is independent of $\eta^x_s$ with variance $\omega^2$. So $y_t = d^\top x_t + \omega w_t$, where $w_t$ is a standard Gaussian walk independent of $x$. Also $x_t = \Sigma_{xx}^{1/2}\xi_t$ with $\xi$ an $m$-vector of independent standard walks, independent of $w$. By Lemma 3, $\tau(y, x) = \tau(\omega w + xd, x) = \tau(w, x) = \tau(w, \xi^\top)$, using the second map with $A = \Sigma_{xx}^{1/2}$. The right side is a fixed function of $N$ independent standard walks. If the observed $\tau$ and $R$ simulated values are exchangeable, the rank of the observed value is uniform, which makes $(1 + \#\{\tau_i \le \tau\})/(R + 1)$ an exact p-value. $\square$

The function simulate_null draws exactly the independent standard walks of Theorem 4. With non-Gaussian or serially correlated increments the law of $\tau$ changes in finite samples, and only the asymptotic statement of Phillips and Ouliaris applies, with $p \to \infty$ when the increments are serially correlated.

**Theorem 5 (superconsistency, bivariate exogenous case).** Let $y_t = \alpha + \beta x_t + u_t$, $t = 1..n$, where $x_t = \sum_{s \le t}v_s$ with $v_s$ iid, mean 0, variance $\sigma_v^2 > 0$, and $u_t$ iid, mean 0, variance $\sigma_u^2$, independent of $(v_s)$. Let $\hat\beta$ be the slope of line L1 in case "c". Then $E\,S_{xx} = \sigma_v^2(n^2 - 1)/6$ exactly, with $S_{xx} = \sum_t(x_t - \bar x)^2$, and $n(\hat\beta - \beta) = O_p(1)$.

*Proof.* The slope is $\hat\beta = \beta + \sum_t(x_t - \bar x)u_t/S_{xx}$. First the mean of $S_{xx} = \sum_tx_t^2 - n\bar x^2$. Since $\operatorname{Cov}(x_s, x_t) = \sigma_v^2\min(s, t)$,
$$E\sum_tx_t^2 = \sigma_v^2\sum_{t=1}^{n}t = \sigma_v^2\frac{n(n+1)}{2}, \qquad E\,n\bar x^2 = \frac{\sigma_v^2}{n}\sum_{s,t}\min(s, t) = \frac{\sigma_v^2}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^2 = \sigma_v^2\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6},$$
where $\sum_{s,t}\min(s, t) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}(\text{number of pairs with both } s, t \ge k) = \sum_k(n - k + 1)^2$. The difference is $\sigma_v^2(n+1)(3n - 2n - 1)/6 = \sigma_v^2(n^2 - 1)/6$.

Next, conditional on $x$, the numerator $\sum_t(x_t - \bar x)u_t$ has mean 0 and variance $\sigma_u^2S_{xx}$, since the $u_t$ are uncorrelated and independent of $x$. So, conditional on $x$, $\hat\beta - \beta$ has mean 0 and variance $\sigma_u^2/S_{xx}$. For $M > 0$ and $\epsilon > 0$, conditional Chebyshev gives
$$P(n|\hat\beta - \beta| > M) \le P(S_{xx} < \epsilon n^2) + E\left[\frac{n^2\sigma_u^2}{M^2S_{xx}}\mathbf 1\{S_{xx} \ge \epsilon n^2\}\right] \le P(n^{-2}S_{xx} < \epsilon) + \frac{\sigma_u^2}{M^2\epsilon}.$$
The cited fact is the functional central limit theorem (Donsker), with the continuous mapping theorem: $n^{-2}S_{xx} \Rightarrow \sigma_v^2\int_0^1\tilde W(r)^2dr$, where $\tilde W = W - \int_0^1W$ is a demeaned standard Brownian motion. The limit is positive with probability 1 and has a continuous distribution, so $\limsup_nP(n^{-2}S_{xx} < \epsilon) = P(\sigma_v^2\int\tilde W^2 < \epsilon)$, which tends to 0 as $\epsilon \to 0$. Given $\delta > 0$, choose $\epsilon$ so that this term is below $\delta/2$ for all large $n$, then $M$ so that $\sigma_u^2/(M^2\epsilon) < \delta/2$. Hence $n(\hat\beta - \beta) = O_p(1)$. $\square$

The exact mean says that the denominator grows like $n^2$, which is the source of the rate: with a stationary regressor $S_{xx}$ grows like $n$, and the same argument gives only $\sqrt n(\hat\beta - \beta) = O_p(1)$. The mean alone does not bound $S_{xx}$ away from 0 in probability; that is the role of the cited limit. Stock (1987) proves $n(\hat\beta - \beta) = O_p(1)$ when $u_t$ is stationary and correlated with $v_t$, where the conditional argument above does not apply and $n(\hat\beta - \beta)$ has a nonzero mean in the limit. Under the same assumptions the residuals satisfy $\hat u_t - u_t = -(\hat\alpha - \alpha) - (\hat\beta - \beta)x_t$, and since $\max_t|x_t| = O_p(\sqrt n)$ the error is $O_p(n^{-1/2})$ uniformly in $t$. That is why the step-two regression on $\hat u$ behaves like a regression on the stationary $u$ in large samples.

**Termination.** Line L1 is a QR factorization of $k$ columns of length $n$ and one back substitution. Line L3 is one more factorization with $p + 1$ columns, or one for each $p \le p_{\max}$ when the lag is chosen by AIC or BIC. All loops have fixed bounds, so the procedure stops after $O(n(k^2 + p_{\max}^3))$ operations.
