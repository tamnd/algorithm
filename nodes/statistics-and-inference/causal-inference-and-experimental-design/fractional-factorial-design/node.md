A fractional factorial design runs only a fraction of the $2^k$ runs of a two-level [factorial design](/factorial-design/): a half, a quarter, or in general $2^{k-p}$ runs. It picks the fraction by setting $p$ added factors equal to products of the others, for example $D = ABC$. The price is aliasing: in the fraction some effects have the same contrast column, so the experiment estimates only their sum. The generators fix which effects are aliased, and the defining relation lists all of them. The resolution, the length of the shortest word in the defining relation, says how bad the aliasing is. In a design of resolution III, main effects are aliased with two-factor interactions; in resolution IV they are not; in resolution V two-factor interactions are not aliased with each other. Finney (1945, in a volume of the Annals of Eugenics that the publisher dates 1943) introduced fractional replication, and Box and Hunter (1961) named the resolution.

## In plain words

A full factorial experiment with seven switches needs 128 trials, and most of what those trials measure is complicated combinations of switches that rarely matter. A fractional design runs a carefully chosen eighth or sixteenth of the trials. The choice is made so that the simple questions, such as "what does this switch do?", stay answerable. The cost is that each answer is mixed with the answers to a few complicated questions, and the design tells you exactly which ones. If the complicated effects are small, as they usually are, the answers are clean. If a mixed answer looks strange, you run the other half of the trials and separate the mix.

## When it applies

- There are many two-level factors, runs are expensive, and the aim is to find the few factors that matter (screening).
- Effect sparsity: only a few effects are large. Effect hierarchy: main effects are larger than two-factor interactions, which are larger than higher interactions. These are working assumptions about the process, not results, and the analysis is only as good as they are.
- The experiment can be sequential, so that an ambiguous result can be followed by a fold-over or by more runs.
- The run count is a power of two. For other multiples of 4 use a [Plackett-Burman design](/plackett-burman-design/).

## The template

Write $N = 2^{k-p}$ for the number of runs. Words are sets of factor letters, and the product of two words is their symmetric difference, because $x_i^2 = 1$ for a $\pm 1$ column. $I$ is the empty word, and $x_W$ is the contrast column of the word $W$, the product of its factor columns.

1. **Base design.** Run the first $k - p$ factors as a full $2^{k-p}$ factorial in standard order.
2. **Generators.** Set each of the $p$ added factors to a product of base factors, for example $D = ABC$. The generator word is the product with the added letter, $ABCD$, so $x_{ABCD} = x_D^2 = 1$ on every run, written $I = ABCD$. Using $D = -ABC$ instead selects the other half.
3. **Defining relation.** The defining contrast subgroup is the group generated by the $p$ generator words. Each generator word holds its own added letter and no other, so the words are independent and the group has $2^p$ elements, $I$ and $2^p - 1$ defining words.
4. **Aliases.** The alias set of an effect $E$ is $\{E W : W \text{ in the group}\}$, a coset of $2^p$ effects. The $2^k$ effects split into $2^{k-p}$ alias sets.
5. **Resolution and wordlength pattern.** The resolution $R$ is the length of the shortest defining word. The wordlength pattern $(A_3, A_4, \dots)$ counts the defining words of each length.
6. **Analysis.** Apply Yates's algorithm to the responses in base standard order. Each of the $N$ contrasts estimates the signed sum of the effects in one alias set.
7. **Randomize** which fraction is run (the signs of the generators) and the run order, as in any [randomized experiment](/randomized-experiment/).

**Theorem 1 (the alias structure).** On the runs of the fraction, $x_E = \pm x_F$ when $EF$ is in the defining group, and $x_E^\top x_F = 0$ otherwise. So the $N$ alias sets have orthogonal contrast columns, and under the full model the contrast of an alias set estimates the signed sum of its effects.

*Proof.* Each generator word $W_j$ has $x_{W_j} = \pm 1$, a constant, on every run by step 2. Since $x_U x_V = x_{UV}$, every $W$ in the group also has a constant column $x_W = \pm 1$. Now take any word $U$. For each added letter in $U$, multiply $U$ by the generator word of that letter. This removes the added letter and adds only base letters, so the result $U'$ has base letters only, and $x_U = \pm x_{U'}$ on the fraction. $U'$ is empty exactly when $U$ is a product of generator words, that is, when $U$ is in the group. If $U'$ is not empty, $x_{U'}$ is a contrast column of the full base factorial, and its entries sum to zero by Theorem 1 of the [factorial design](/factorial-design/) page. Apply this to $U = EF$, using $x_E x_F = x_{EF}$: if $EF$ is in the group, $x_E x_F$ is a constant $\pm 1$; if not, $x_E^\top x_F = \sum x_{EF} = 0$. The expected contrast $\sum_x x_E(x) \mathbb E[y_x]$ under the full model $\mathbb E[y_x] = \sum_S b_S x_S(x)$ keeps only the terms with $x_S = \pm x_E$, which are the members of the alias set. $\square$

**Theorem 2 (what resolution guarantees).** In a design of resolution $R$, an effect of order $a$ is not aliased with an effect of order $b$ when $a + b < R$. So in resolution III main effects are not aliased with each other, in resolution IV they are not aliased with two-factor interactions, and in resolution V two-factor interactions are not aliased with each other.

*Proof.* If $E$ and $F$ are aliased, $EF$ is a defining word, so $R \le |EF| \le |E| + |F| = a + b$. $\square$

**Theorem 3 (the fold-over).** Let a $2^{k-p}$ design have at least one defining word of odd length, and add to it its mirror image, the same runs with every sign reversed. The $2^{k-p+1}$ runs form a design whose defining words are exactly the old defining words of even length. So the fold-over of a resolution III design has resolution at least IV.

*Proof.* In the mirror runs $x_W$ becomes $(-1)^{|W|} x_W$. A defining word of even length keeps its constant on both halves and stays a defining word. A defining word of odd length has the constant $+c$ on one half and $-c$ on the other, so its column now sums to zero. A word outside the old group has a column that sums to zero on each half (Theorem 1). So the new defining words are the old words of even length. They form a subgroup of index 2 in the old group, because a word of odd length exists, which matches the doubled run count. $\square$

**Codes.** Write each defining word as a 0/1 vector of length $k$. The group is then a [linear code](/linear-code/) of dimension $p$, and the resolution is its minimum distance. With level $+1$ written as bit 0 and level $-1$ as bit 1, the runs are the codewords of the [dual code](/dual-code/), and the [orthogonal array](/orthogonal-array/) page shows why this makes the design an array of strength $R - 1$.

**What is cited, not proved.** Two designs with the same resolution can differ. Fries and Hunter (1980) defined **minimum aberration**: among designs with the same $k$ and $p$, prefer the lexicographically smallest wordlength pattern, which keeps the fewest short words. The test file finds the minimum by an exhaustive search over all generator pairs for the $2^{7-2}$ case below; the general tables are cited.

**Cost.** Building the defining group takes $2^p$ word products, and the analysis is Yates's algorithm on $N$ responses: $N \log_2 N$ additions.

## Classic examples

**A half fraction that reverses a sign.** Take the $2^3$ pilot plant data of the [factorial design](/factorial-design/) page and run only the half with $I = ABC$ ($C = AB$): runs $a, b, c, abc$ with yields $72, 54, 52, 80$. The design has resolution III, with aliases $A = BC$, $B = AC$, $C = AB$. The estimates are

| contrast | estimates | value | full-factorial values |
|---|---|---|---|
| $A$ | $A + BC$ | 23 | $23 + 0$ |
| $B$ | $B + AC$ | 5 | $-5 + 10$ |
| $C$ | $C + AB$ | 3 | $1.5 + 1.5$ |

The fraction reports $B = +5$, but the true main effect is $-5$: the large $AC$ interaction reversed the sign. The other half, $I = -ABC$ (runs $(1), ab, ac, bc$, yields $60, 68, 83, 45$), gives $A - BC = 23$, $B - AC = -15$ and $C - AB = 0$. Half the sum and half the difference of the two halves give back $B = -5$, $AC = 10$, $C = 1.5$ and $AB = 1.5$. The test file checks every number.

**A $2^{4-1}$ design of resolution IV.** $D = ABC$, so $I = ABCD$. Main effects are aliased with three-factor interactions ($A = BCD$), and the two-factor interactions come in pairs: $AB = CD$, $AC = BD$, $AD = BC$. Eight runs study four factors.

**A $2^{5-1}$ design of resolution V.** $E = ABCD$, so $I = ABCDE$. All 5 main effects and all 10 two-factor interactions are in different alias sets, so 16 runs estimate all of them.

**The saturated $2^{7-4}$ design.** $D = AB$, $E = AC$, $F = BC$, $G = ABC$ give 7 factors in 8 runs. The group has 15 defining words with wordlength pattern $(A_3, \dots, A_7) = (7, 7, 0, 0, 1)$, and each main effect is aliased with three two-factor interactions, for example $A = BD = CE = FG$. As 0/1 vectors the 16 words of the group are the [7, 4] [Hamming code](/hamming-code/), whose weights are $0, 3, 4, 7$, and the 8 runs form its dual, the [7, 3] simplex code. Its fold-over has 16 runs and resolution IV (Theorem 3), which the test file checks column by column.

**Minimum aberration for $2^{7-2}$.** $F = ABCD$, $G = ABDE$ give the words $CEFG$, $ABCDF$, $ABDEG$ and the pattern $(0, 1, 2, 0, 0)$. $F = ABC$, $G = ADE$ give $ABCF$, $ADEG$, $BCDEFG$ and the pattern $(0, 2, 0, 1, 0)$. Both have resolution IV, but each word of length 4 aliases three pairs of two-factor interactions, so the first design aliases 3 pairs and the second 6. The exhaustive search in the test file confirms that $(0, 1, 2, 0, 0)$ is the smallest pattern.

## Variations

- **Single-factor fold-over.** Reverse the signs of one factor only in the second half. In a design of resolution III, this frees that factor's main effect and all its two-factor interactions from aliasing with other main effects and two-factor interactions (Exercise 4).
- **Blocking.** A fraction can itself be split into blocks by confounding a further word with blocks; the block word must not be aliased with an effect of interest.
- **More than two levels.** For $s$ a prime power, regular $s^{k-p}$ fractions use generators over the field with $s$ elements. A [Latin square design](/latin-square-design/) of order 3 is a $3^{3-1}$ fraction with rows, columns and treatments as the factors.
- **Non-regular fractions.** [Plackett-Burman designs](/plackett-burman-design/) have run counts that are multiples of 4 but not powers of 2. Their effects are partly aliased, with correlations other than $0$ and $\pm 1$.
- **Unreplicated analysis.** Without replicates, judge the effects on a normal or half-normal probability plot (Daniel, 1959), or estimate the noise from the smaller effects.
- **Response surface work.** A resolution V fraction plus center and axial runs is a central composite design for [response surface methodology](/response-surface-methodology/).

## Pitfalls

- A contrast estimates a sum of aliased effects. Interactions can hide a main effect or reverse its sign, as in the pilot plant half fraction.
- Read the aliases from the whole defining group, not from the generators alone. For $E = ABC$, $F = BCD$, the product $ABCE \cdot BCDF = ADEF$ is also a defining word, so $AD = EF$.
- Resolution alone does not rank designs. Use the wordlength pattern, and check that the interactions you care about are in alias sets of their own.
- Effect sparsity and hierarchy are assumptions. When a result looks strange, run the fold-over before you act on it.
- An unreplicated fraction has no error degrees of freedom; the probability plot is a judgement, not a test of fixed size.
- Losing a run breaks the orthogonality, as for a full factorial.

## Exercises

1. Write the 8 runs of the $2^{4-1}$ design with $I = -ABCD$ and give the signs in its alias sets.
2. For the $2^{6-2}$ design with $E = ABC$ and $F = BCD$, list the alias sets of all 15 two-factor interactions. Which of them are not aliased with any other two-factor interaction?
3. Show that a regular $2^{k-p}$ design of resolution V must have $N \ge 1 + k + k(k-1)/2$ runs.
4. In a design of resolution III, prove that a single-factor fold-over on factor $A$ makes $A$ and every two-factor interaction that contains $A$ orthogonal to all other main effects and two-factor interactions.
5. Show that the 16 words of the defining group of the saturated $2^{7-4}$ design have the weight distribution $1, 0, 0, 7, 7, 0, 0, 1$, the weight distribution of the [7, 4] Hamming code.
