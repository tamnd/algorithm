The Data Encryption Standard (DES) is a [block cipher](/block-cipher/) on 64-bit blocks with a 56-bit key, built as a 16-round [Feistel network](/feistel-network/) with eight [S-boxes](/s-box/) from 6 to 4 bits. The US National Bureau of Standards published it in 1977 as FIPS 46, and it was the main civilian cipher for two decades. Its key is too short: the EFF DES cracker found a key by [exhaustive search](/exhaustive-key-search/) in 56 hours in July 1998, and NIST withdrew FIPS 46-3 in 2005. DES survives inside [Triple DES](/triple-des/), which is itself now disallowed for new encryption, and as the standard example of Feistel design, [differential](/differential-cryptanalysis/) and [linear cryptanalysis](/linear-cryptanalysis/).

## In plain words

DES cuts a block of 64 bits into two halves of 32 bits. In each of 16 rounds, it takes the right half, stretches it to 48 bits by copying some bits twice, mixes in 48 bits of key, and sends the result through eight small lookup tables. Each table turns 6 bits into 4, so 32 bits come out. These bits are shuffled and added to the left half. Then the halves trade places.

Before the first round and after the last, the bits are reordered by a fixed table. This does not add security.

Each round uses a different set of 48 key bits, picked from the 56-bit key by rotating and selecting. To decrypt, DES runs the same steps with the round keys in reverse order.

## The problem

DES computes a permutation $E_K$ of $\{0,1\}^{64}$ for each key $K \in \{0,1\}^{64}$, and its inverse $D_K$. Bits 8, 16, ..., 64 of the key are parity bits and do not affect the result, so there are $2^{56}$ effective keys. Bits are numbered 1 to 64 from the left, as in FIPS 46-3. Encryption of longer messages needs a mode of operation, and DES used alone as a function on one block is the [ECB mode](/ecb-mode/) case, which is not IND-CPA secure.

## The algorithm

The three procedures follow FIPS 46-3. IP is the initial permutation and FP $= \mathrm{IP}^{-1}$ the final one; E expands 32 bits to 48; $S_1, \ldots, S_8$ map 6 bits to 4; P permutes 32 bits; PC-1 and PC-2 are the permuted choices of the key schedule, and $s_i$ is the left shift of round $i$.

```pseudo id=main
DES-ENCRYPT(K, x)
L1  k1, ..., k16 ← KEY-SCHEDULE(K)
L2  (L0, R0) ← IP(x), as two halves of 32 bits
L3  for i ← 1 to 16 do
L4      Li ← R(i−1)
L5      Ri ← L(i−1) ⊕ f(R(i−1), ki)
L6  return FP(R16 ‖ L16)                    ▷ the halves are swapped once more

f(R, k)                                     ▷ R has 32 bits, k has 48 bits
L7  B ← E(R) ⊕ k
L8  for j ← 1 to 8 do                       ▷ B = B1 ‖ ... ‖ B8, each of 6 bits
L9      Cj ← Sj(Bj)                         ▷ row = first and last bit, column = middle 4 bits
L10 return P(C1 ‖ ... ‖ C8)

KEY-SCHEDULE(K)
L11 (C0, D0) ← PC-1(K), as two halves of 28 bits ▷ the 8 parity bits are dropped
L12 for i ← 1 to 16 do
L13     Ci ← rotl(C(i−1), si), Di ← rotl(D(i−1), si)
L14     ki ← PC-2(Ci ‖ Di)                  ▷ 48 of the 56 bits
L15 return k1, ..., k16
```

DES-DECRYPT is DES-ENCRYPT with the round keys used in the order $k_{16}, \ldots, k_1$. The shifts are $s = (1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 1, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 1)$. The tables IP, E, P, PC-1, PC-2 and $S_1$ to $S_8$ are in FIPS 46-3 and in the code for this page.

The Python code for this page is for study only. It is not constant time, and DES is broken: do not use it to protect data. Use AES from a FIPS 140 validated module, or libsodium, instead.

## Correctness

DES-DECRYPT inverts DES-ENCRYPT for every key. FP is the inverse of IP, and the 16 rounds with the swap of line L6 form the Feistel network $\sigma \circ \Phi_{k_{16}} \circ \cdots \circ \Phi_{k_1}$, whose inverse is the same network with the keys reversed, for any round function f. ## Complexity

One block costs one IP, 16 rounds and one FP. A round evaluates E, a 48-bit XOR, 8 S-box lookups, P and a 32-bit XOR, so a block costs 128 S-box lookups. The key schedule costs PC-1, 16 pairs of rotations and 16 applications of PC-2, once for each key. On $b$ blocks the time is $\Theta(b)$, with constant extra space.

The cost of attacks, in DES encryptions:

| attack | data | time | source |
|---|---|---|---|
| exhaustive search | 1 or 2 known pairs | $2^{56}$, or $2^{55}$ with the complementation property | generic |
| differential cryptanalysis | $2^{47}$ chosen plaintexts | about $2^{37}$ | Biham and Shamir 1992 |
| linear cryptanalysis | $2^{43}$ known plaintexts | about $2^{43}$ | Matsui 1994 |

The analytic attacks need far more data than an attacker can usually collect, so exhaustive search is the practical attack. The EFF machine of 1998 cost less than 250,000 US dollars, tested about 88 billion keys per second on average and found the key of the RSA DES Challenge II-2 in 56 hours, after searching 24.8% of the key space.

## Properties

- **Complementation.** $E_{\bar K}(\bar x) = \overline{E_K(x)}$ for all $K$ and $x$, where the bar is the bitwise complement. So a chosen-plaintext search can test $K$ and $\bar K$ with one encryption, which halves the search.
- **Weak keys.** For the four keys 0101010101010101, FEFEFEFEFEFEFEFE, E0E0E0E0F1F1F1F1 and 1F1F1F1F0E0E0E0E, all 16 round keys are equal, so $E_K$ is an involution: $E_K(E_K(x)) = x$ (NIST SP 800-67 Rev. 2, Section 3.3.2).
- **Semi-weak keys.** There are 6 pairs, such as 01FE01FE01FE01FE and FE01FE01FE01FE01, whose round keys are those of the other key in reverse order. So $E_{K_1} = D_{K_2}$.
- **Parity bits.** Changing bit 8, 16, ..., or 64 of the key does not change the result.
- **Avalanche.** Over random keys and inputs, a one-bit change of the plaintext changes on average 2.89 output bits after 1 round, 22.41 after 3, 31.76 after 5 and 31.96 after all 16. The ideal value is 32.
- **Not a group.** Campbell and Wiener showed in 1992 that the DES permutations do not form a group, so double and triple encryption are not equal to single encryption.

## Variants and improvements

- **[Triple DES](/triple-des/).** Three DES calls in the order encrypt, decrypt, encrypt with two or three keys. It resists exhaustive search, but keeps the 64-bit block.
- **DESX.** Whitening keys XORed before and after DES, $K_2 \oplus \mathrm{DES}_K(x \oplus K_1)$ (Rivest 1984). In the ideal cipher model, Kilian and Rogaway showed that a generic attack with $m$ known pairs needs about $2^{k+n-1}/m = 2^{119}/m$ evaluations of DES. See [key whitening](/key-whitening/).
- **Bitslice implementations.** Software that computes 64 or more DES blocks in parallel with Boolean operations on words, one bit per block. It avoids table lookups and is constant time.
- **Lucifer.** IBM's earlier Feistel cipher with a 128-bit key, from which DES was derived.

## When to use it

Do not use DES to protect data. Its key is found by exhaustive search on modest hardware, and its 64-bit block causes birthday collisions after about $2^{32}$ blocks under one key. Use it only to decrypt legacy data, to interoperate with old systems that cannot change, or for study. For new systems use [AES](/advanced-encryption-standard/).

## Pitfalls

- **Bit numbering.** FIPS 46-3 numbers bits from 1 at the left. Reading the tables with 0-based or right-to-left numbering gives a cipher that round-trips but fails the test vectors.
- **S-box row and column.** The row is the first and last of the 6 input bits, and the column is the middle 4 bits.
- **The final swap.** The output is FP($R_{16} \| L_{16}$), not FP($L_{16} \| R_{16}$). Without the swap, decryption with reversed keys fails.
- **Parity bits.** A test that changes only a parity bit of the key and expects a new ciphertext is wrong.
- **Weak keys.** Key generation for legacy uses should reject the 4 weak and 12 semi-weak keys.
- **Table lookups and timing.** An S-box lookup at a secret index leaks through the cache. Bitsliced code avoids this.

## History

IBM designed DES from Horst Feistel's Lucifer cipher in the early 1970s, after a call for proposals by the National Bureau of Standards. NBS published it as FIPS 46 in 1977, with a 56-bit key. The design criteria of the S-boxes stayed secret until Coppersmith published them in 1994, after Biham and Shamir had made differential cryptanalysis public and shown that DES resists it better than random S-boxes would. Matsui found linear cryptanalysis in 1993 and ran the first experimental attack on full DES in 1994. The EFF DES cracker found a DES key in 56 hours in July 1998. FIPS 46-3 reaffirmed DES in 1999, but allowed single DES only for legacy systems, and NIST withdrew it in 2005.
