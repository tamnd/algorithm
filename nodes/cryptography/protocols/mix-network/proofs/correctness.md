We use the notation and the line numbers of the page. $\mathbb{G}$ is the subgroup of prime order $q$ generated by $g$, the trustees hold $x_1, \ldots, x_T$, and $x = x_1 + \cdots + x_T \bmod q$, so $y = g^x$ by line L1. For $s \in \mathbb{Z}_q$ we write $E(s) = (g^s, y^s)$, an encryption of $1$, and we multiply ciphertexts part by part. Then line L5 sets $c'_{\pi(i)} = c_i \cdot E(s_i)$.

**Lemma 1 (decryption).** For $c = (a, b)$, lines L8 and L9 output $b / a^x$. If $c = (g^r, m y^r)$, the output is $m$.

*Proof.* $d_1 \cdots d_T = a^{x_1} \cdots a^{x_T} = a^{x_1 + \cdots + x_T} = a^x$, because $a$ has order dividing $q$. If $a = g^r$ and $b = m y^r$, then $a^x = g^{rx} = y^r$, so $b / a^x = m$. $\square$

**Lemma 2 (re-encryption keeps the plaintext).** For every ciphertext $c = (a, b)$ and every $s$, $c \cdot E(s)$ decrypts to the same value as $c$.

*Proof.* $c \cdot E(s) = (a g^s, b y^s)$. By Lemma 1 it decrypts to $b y^s / (a g^s)^x = b y^s / (a^x g^{sx}) = b y^s / (a^x y^s) = b / a^x$, which is the decryption of $c$. $\square$

**Theorem 3 (correctness).** Let $D(c)$ be the decryption of $c$. After any number $K$ of mixes (line L7), the multiset $\{D(c_i^{(K)})\}$ equals the multiset $\{D(c_i^{(0)})\}$ of the inputs. In particular, if every sender encrypts $m_i$ with line L2, the trustees output exactly the multiset $\{m_1, \ldots, m_N\}$.

*Proof.* By induction on $K$. For $K = 0$ there is nothing to prove. One mix takes $c_1, \ldots, c_N$ to $c'_1, \ldots, c'_N$ with $c'_{\pi(i)} = c_i \cdot E(s_i)$. Since $\pi$ is a permutation (line L3), each output position $j$ is $\pi(i)$ for exactly one $i$, and $D(c'_{\pi(i)}) = D(c_i)$ by Lemma 2. So the map $i \mapsto \pi(i)$ is a bijection between inputs and outputs that keeps $D$, and the two multisets are equal. The cascade applies this $K$ times. The last statement follows from Lemma 1. $\square$

**Theorem 4 (one honest mix hides the permutation).** Assume the decisional Diffie-Hellman (DDH) problem is hard in $\mathbb{G}$, and that the public key $y$ is uniform in $\mathbb{G}$ and its discrete logarithm $x$ is unknown to the adversary. (Key generation must ensure this, for example by having each trustee commit to $h_j$, or prove that it knows $x_j$, before the others reveal theirs.) Fix an honest mix with input $c_1, \ldots, c_N$, which the adversary may choose. Then the input and the output of the mix are computationally indistinguishable from the same input with $N$ independent uniform pairs in $\mathbb{G}^2$ as output. In particular, from the board up to the end of the mixing, no efficient adversary links an output of the cascade to its input with probability more than $1/N$ plus a negligible amount, whatever the adversary does at the other mixes.

*Proof.* The adversary may know the inputs, choose them, and know the permutations of every other mix. The output of the honest mix is the list $c'$ with $c'_{\pi(i)} = c_i \cdot (g^{s_i}, y^{s_i})$ for a uniform $\pi$ and independent uniform $s_i$.

Hybrid $H_0$ is this real output. In hybrid $H_k$, for $k = 1, \ldots, N$, the first $k$ pairs $(g^{s_i}, y^{s_i})$ are replaced by $(g^{s_i}, g^{z_i})$ with $z_i$ uniform and independent. An adversary that tells $H_{k-1}$ from $H_k$ breaks DDH: given a triple $(y, g^s, Z)$ with $y$ uniform and $Z = y^s$ or $Z$ uniform, the reduction uses this $y$ as the public key, uses $(g^s, Z)$ as the $k$-th pair, and makes the other pairs itself, with fresh $s_i$ for $i > k$ and fresh $z_i$ for $i < k$. It needs no secret key for this. The view is $H_{k-1}$ if $Z = y^s$ and $H_k$ if $Z$ is uniform. So adjacent hybrids differ by at most the DDH advantage, and $H_0$ and $H_N$ differ by at most $N$ times it.

In $H_N$ each pair $(g^{s_i}, g^{z_i})$ is uniform in $\mathbb{G}^2$ and independent of everything else, so $c_i \cdot (g^{s_i}, g^{z_i})$ is uniform in $\mathbb{G}^2$ for every fixed $c_i$, since multiplying by a fixed element is a bijection of the group. So the output in $H_N$ is $N$ independent uniform pairs, whatever $\pi$ is, and it carries no information about $\pi$. An adversary in $H_N$ who names an input for a given output is right with probability exactly $1/N$, because $\pi$ is uniform and independent of its view. By the hybrids, in the real run its success is at most $1/N$ plus $N$ times the DDH advantage. The later mixes apply to this output functions whose randomness is independent of $\pi$, so they do not change the bound. Since the composed permutation of the cascade is $\pi_K \circ \cdots \circ \pi_1$, and one uniform factor independent of the others makes the composition uniform, one honest mix is enough to hide the link from the first input to the last output. $\square$

*Remark.* The theorem covers the board up to the last mix. The decryption reveals the multiset of plaintexts, which by Theorem 3 does not depend on $\pi$. A full proof that the decryption shares and their proofs reveal nothing more needs a simulation of the trustees, as in the analyses of verifiable mixnets, and is not given here. The theorem also does not cover an adversary who copies an honest input as its own input, because then two equal plaintexts in the output show where the copy went; senders must prove that they know their plaintext, as the page explains.

**Theorem 5 (the cut-and-choose proof).** Let a mix post $c'$ and run the proof of the code for $k$ rounds, with a fresh shadow shuffle and a uniform challenge bit in each round.

1. *Completeness.* If $c'$ is a permuted re-encryption of $c$ and the mix follows the protocol, the verifier accepts.
2. *Soundness.* If $c'$ is not a permuted re-encryption of $c$, the verifier accepts with probability at most $2^{-k}$.
3. *Honest-verifier zero knowledge.* Each round reveals nothing about $\pi$.

*Proof.* Write $c \Rightarrow d$ when there are a permutation $\rho$ and exponents $u_i$ with $d_{\rho(i)} = c_i \cdot E(u_i)$ for all $i$.

(1) For bit $0$ the mix opens $\sigma$ and $t$, and the shadow is by construction $c \Rightarrow$ shadow. For bit $1$ the mix opens $\phi = \pi \circ \sigma^{-1}$ and $u_{\sigma(i)} = s_i - t_i$. Then $\mathrm{shadow}_{\sigma(i)} \cdot E(s_i - t_i) = c_i \cdot E(t_i) \cdot E(s_i - t_i) = c_i \cdot E(s_i) = c'_{\pi(i)} = c'_{\phi(\sigma(i))}$, using $E(a) E(b) = E(a + b)$. So both checks pass.

(2) The relation $\Rightarrow$ is transitive: if $c_{\rho(i)}' = c_i E(u_i)$ and $d_{\tau(j)} = c'_j E(v_j)$, then $d_{\tau(\rho(i))} = c_i E(u_i + v_{\rho(i)})$. Suppose that in some round the mix could answer both bits for the same shadow $w$. Then $c \Rightarrow w$ and $w \Rightarrow c'$, so $c \Rightarrow c'$, which contradicts the assumption. So for each shadow at most one bit has an accepting answer. The shadow is fixed before the bit, and the bit is uniform and independent, so each round accepts with probability at most $1/2$, and $k$ independent rounds accept with probability at most $2^{-k}$.

(3) For bit $0$ the verifier sees $\sigma$ and $t$, which are uniform and independent of $\pi$. For bit $1$ it sees $\phi = \pi \circ \sigma^{-1}$ and $u_{\sigma(i)} = s_i - t_i$. Since $\sigma$ is uniform and independent of $\pi$, $\phi$ is a uniform permutation, and since each $t_i$ is uniform, each $u$ is uniform, independent of $\pi$ and $s$. In both cases a simulator that knows the bit in advance can make a shadow and an answer with the same distribution: for bit $0$ it shuffles $c$ itself, and for bit $1$ it draws $\phi$ and $u$ and sets $w_j = c'_{\phi(j)} \cdot E(-u_j)$. So the transcript of a round with an honest verifier can be simulated without $\pi$. $\square$

**Proposition 6 (the $n - 1$ attack).** Let a mix flush a batch as soon as it holds $N$ messages, and let an adversary control which messages reach it. If the adversary lets through only the target's message and adds $N - 1$ messages whose plaintexts it knows, then after the cascade and the decryption it learns the target's plaintext with probability $1$.

*Proof.* By Theorem 3 the decrypted output of the cascade is the multiset of the $N$ input plaintexts. The adversary removes its own $N - 1$ known plaintexts from this multiset, and the one value left is the target's plaintext. Theorem 4 does not help, because it bounds only what the output reveals about $\pi$, and here the plaintexts alone identify the target. $\blacksquare$
