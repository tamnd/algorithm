An $n$-th root of unity is an element $\omega$ of a ring with $\omega^n = 1$. Over the complex numbers the $n$-th roots of unity are the $n$ points $e^{2\pi i k/n}$, evenly spaced on the unit circle, and they form a [cyclic group](/cyclic-group/) of order $n$. A *primitive* $n$-th root has [order](/multiplicative-order/) exactly $n$, and a *principal* one also makes every sum $\sum_j \omega^{jk}$ with $n \nmid k$ vanish. These sums are what make the [discrete Fourier transform](/discrete-fourier-transform/) invertible and the [fast Fourier transform](/fast-fourier-transform/) possible, over $\mathbb{C}$ and over finite rings such as $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$.

## In plain words

Picture a clock face with equally spaced marks and a hand at twelve. One step moves the hand to the next mark. After as many steps as there are marks, the hand is back at twelve. Each mark is a root of unity: a number that comes back to one when you multiply it by itself often enough.

Two facts make these numbers useful. First, if you take every second mark of a clock with an even number of marks, you get a clock with half as many marks. Fast Fourier methods use this to split a problem into two halves. Second, if you add up the marks as arrows from the centre, they cancel to zero, because they point evenly in all directions. This is why a Fourier transform can be undone. The same patterns exist in modular arithmetic, where they give exact answers with no rounding.

## The idea

**Definitions.** Let $R$ be a commutative ring with $1$, and let $n \ge 1$.

- $\omega \in R$ is an *$n$-th root of unity* if $\omega^n = 1$. Then $\omega$ is a unit, with $\omega^{-1} = \omega^{n-1}$.
- It is *primitive* if its order is exactly $n$: $\omega^k \ne 1$ for $1 \le k < n$.
- It is *principal* if $\omega^n = 1$ and $\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \omega^{jk} = 0$ for $1 \le k \le n - 1$.

In a field, and in particular in $\mathbb{C}$ and in $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for a prime $p$, primitive and principal mean the same thing. Von zur Gathen and Gerhard use the word *primitive* for the stronger notion, which needs care in a ring with zero divisors; these pages say *principal* for it.

**Notation used on all pages of fast transforms.** The transform length is $n$. Over $\mathbb{C}$, $\omega_n = e^{-2\pi i/n} = \cos(2\pi/n) - i \sin(2\pi/n)$. The minus sign is the convention of signal processing, FFTW and NumPy. CLRS uses $e^{+2\pi i/n}$. Both are principal $n$-th roots, and the two choices are conjugates of each other, so every statement here holds for both. The DFT is $X_k = \sum_j x_j \omega^{jk}$, and the inverse carries the factor $1/n$.

**Complex roots.** The $n$-th roots of unity in $\mathbb{C}$ are $\omega_n^k$ for $0 \le k < n$, the $n$ roots of $z^n - 1$. They form the cyclic group generated by $\omega_n$. The root $\omega_n^k$ is primitive exactly when $\gcd(k, n) = 1$, so there are $\varphi(n)$ primitive roots, where $\varphi$ is [Euler's totient](/euler-totient/).

**Proof that a primitive root is principal in a field.** Let $1 \le k \le n-1$. Then $\omega^k \ne 1$ because the order of $\omega$ is $n > k$. By the [geometric series](/geometric-series/), $(\omega^k - 1)\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \omega^{jk} = \omega^{nk} - 1 = 0$. In a field, $\omega^k - 1 \ne 0$ can be cancelled, so the sum is $0$. In a ring this cancellation needs $\omega^k - 1$ to be a non-zero-divisor, which is where the two notions part.

**Three lemmas.** For a principal $n$-th root $\omega$ (over $\mathbb{C}$, $\omega = \omega_n$):

1. *Cancellation.* For $d \ge 1$, $\omega_{dn}^{dk} = \omega_n^k$. In a ring, if $\omega$ is a principal $dn$-th root and $d \cdot 1$ is not a zero divisor, then $\omega^d$ is a principal $n$-th root. See the [cancellation lemma](/fft-cancellation-lemma/).
2. *Halving.* If $n$ is even, $\omega^{n/2} = -1$ (over $\mathbb{C}$, and in a ring when $\frac n2 \cdot 1$ is not a zero divisor), and the squares of the $n$ roots $\omega^k$ are the $n/2$ roots $(\omega^2)^k$, each one twice. See the [halving lemma](/fft-halving-lemma/).
3. *Summation.* $\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \omega^{jk}$ is $n$ if $n \mid k$ and $0$ otherwise. See the [summation lemma](/fft-summation-lemma/).

Lemma 2 drives the split of the FFT into two halves, and Lemma 3 gives the inverse DFT.

**A test for powers of two.** Let $n = 2^t$ with $t \ge 1$, and let $\omega \in R$ satisfy $\omega^{n/2} = -1$. Then $\omega$ is a principal $n$-th root of unity, in every commutative ring. *Proof.* $\omega^n = (-1)^2 = 1$. Expanding the product gives $\sum_{j=0}^{2^t - 1} z^j = \prod_{i=0}^{t-1} (1 + z^{2^i})$ for each $z$, since each $j < 2^t$ is a unique sum of distinct powers $2^i$. Let $1 \le k < n$ and write $k = 2^a b$ with $b$ odd and $a < t$. For $i = t - 1 - a$, the factor with $z = \omega^k$ is $1 + \omega^{2^{t-1-a} 2^a b} = 1 + (\omega^{n/2})^b = 1 + (-1)^b = 0$. So the sum is $0$. $\square$ This test is how the FFT over $\mathbb{Z}/(2^N + 1)$ in [Schönhage-Strassen multiplication](/schonhage-strassen-multiplication/) gets its roots: there $2^N \equiv -1$, so $2$ is a principal $2N$-th root when $2N$ is a power of two.

**Roots in $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$.** For a prime $p$, the group $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}$ is cyclic of order $p - 1$ (Ireland and Rosen, Chapter 4), generated by a [primitive root](/primitive-root/) $g$. So a primitive $n$-th root exists exactly when $n \mid p - 1$, and then $\omega = g^{(p-1)/n}$ is one. For NTTs of length $2^t$ one uses primes $p = c \cdot 2^s + 1$ with $s \ge t$.

## Examples

**The eighth roots.** $\omega_8 = e^{-2\pi i/8} = (1 - i)/\sqrt 2$, and its powers are

$$1, \ \tfrac{1 - i}{\sqrt 2}, \ -i, \ \tfrac{-1 - i}{\sqrt 2}, \ -1, \ \tfrac{-1 + i}{\sqrt 2}, \ i, \ \tfrac{1 + i}{\sqrt 2}.$$

Their sum is $0$. The primitive ones are $\omega_8^1, \omega_8^3, \omega_8^5, \omega_8^7$, so $\varphi(8) = 4$. Squaring gives $1, -i, -1, i$ twice, the fourth roots, as the halving lemma says. And $\omega_8^4 = -1$.

**Modulo 17.** The orders of $1, 2, \ldots, 16$ modulo $17$ are $1, 8, 16, 4, 16, 16, 16, 8, 8, 16, 16, 16, 4, 16, 8, 2$. So $3$ is a primitive root of $17$, the primitive $8$-th roots are $2, 8, 9, 15$, and the primitive $4$-th roots are $4$ and $13 = 4^{-1}$. With $g = 3$, $\omega = 3^{16/8} = 9$ is a principal $8$-th root, and $2 = 3^{14}$ is another one. Indeed $2^4 = 16 \equiv -1$, so the test for powers of two applies.

**Primes for the NTT.** All of these were checked by computing the order of the generator:

| $p$ | form | largest power-of-two length | primitive root |
|---|---|---|---|
| 17 | $2^4 + 1$ | 16 | 3 |
| 97 | $3 \cdot 2^5 + 1$ | 32 | 5 (but not 3) |
| 65537 | $2^{16} + 1$ | 65536 | 3 |
| 7340033 | $7 \cdot 2^{20} + 1$ | $2^{20}$ | 3 |
| 167772161 | $5 \cdot 2^{25} + 1$ | $2^{25}$ | 3 |
| 469762049 | $7 \cdot 2^{26} + 1$ | $2^{26}$ | 3 |
| 998244353 | $119 \cdot 2^{23} + 1$ | $2^{23}$ | 3 |

**A non-example: primitive but not principal.** In $\mathbb{Z}/15\mathbb{Z}$, $4^2 = 16 \equiv 1$ and $4 \ne 1$, so $4$ is a primitive square root of unity. But $1 + 4 = 5 \ne 0$, so it is not principal. The reason is that $4 - 1 = 3$ is a zero divisor modulo $15$. A DFT of length $2$ with this root is the map $(a, b) \mapsto (a + b, a + 4b)$, which sends $(5, 10)$ to $(0, 0)$ and so cannot be inverted.

**A non-example: no root of that length.** Modulo $97$ there is no primitive $64$-th root of unity, because $64 \nmid 96$. An NTT of length $64$ modulo $97$ is impossible; the longest power-of-two length is $32$.

## Where it is used

- **The DFT and its inverse.** The [DFT](/discrete-fourier-transform/) is evaluation at the powers of a principal root, and the [inverse DFT](/inverse-discrete-fourier-transform/) uses $\omega^{-1}$ and $1/n$.
- **FFT algorithms.** The [FFT](/fast-fourier-transform/) uses $\omega^{n/2} = -1$ in every [butterfly](/butterfly-operation/), and the powers $\omega^k$ that it multiplies in are the [twiddle factors](/twiddle-factor/). The [split-radix](/split-radix-fft/) and [mixed-radix](/mixed-radix-fft/) FFTs use roots of other orders.
- **Exact transforms.** The [number-theoretic transform](/number-theoretic-transform/) uses roots in $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, and [Schönhage-Strassen multiplication](/schonhage-strassen-multiplication/) uses the root $2$ in $\mathbb{Z}/(2^N + 1)$.
- **Prime lengths.** [Rader's algorithm](/rader-fft/) reorders the roots $\omega_p^k$ by the powers of a primitive root modulo $p$.
- **Single frequencies.** The [Goertzel algorithm](/goertzel-algorithm/) uses the real part $\cos(2\pi k/n)$ of one root.
- **Convolution.** The [convolution theorem](/convolution-theorem/) rests on the summation lemma.
