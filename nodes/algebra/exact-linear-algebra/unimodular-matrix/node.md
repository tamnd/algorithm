A *unimodular matrix* is a square integer matrix with determinant $+1$ or $-1$. These are exactly the integer matrices whose inverse is again an integer matrix, so they form the group $GL_n(\mathbb{Z})$. They describe every change of basis of $\mathbb{Z}^n$ and of a [lattice](/lattice/), every sequence of integer row or column operations, and the transforms $U$ and $V$ that the [Hermite](/hermite-normal-form/) and [Smith normal forms](/smith-normal-form/) return.

## Definition

**Notation.** $R$ is a commutative ring with $1$, usually $\mathbb{Z}$, and $n \ge 0$. $R^\times$ is the group of units of $R$; $\mathbb{Z}^\times = \{1, -1\}$.

**Definition.** An $n \times n$ matrix $U$ with entries in $R$ is *unimodular* over $R$ if $\det U \in R^\times$. Over $\mathbb{Z}$: $U$ is an integer matrix with $\det U = \pm 1$. The unimodular matrices over $R$ form the group $GL_n(R)$, and those of determinant $1$ the subgroup $SL_n(R)$.

**Equivalent conditions over $\mathbb{Z}$.** For an $n \times n$ integer matrix $U$, the following are equivalent.

1. $\det U = \pm 1$.
2. $U$ is invertible and $U^{-1}$ has integer entries.
3. The map $x \mapsto U x$ is a bijection of $\mathbb{Z}^n$ onto itself.
4. $U$ is a product of elementary integer matrices: swaps of two rows, negation of a row, and addition of an integer multiple of one row to another.

*Proof.* (1) implies (2): by the [Laplace expansion](/laplace-expansion/), $U \operatorname{adj}(U) = \det(U) I$, and $\operatorname{adj}(U)$ has integer entries, so $U^{-1} = \pm \operatorname{adj}(U)$ is integral. (2) implies (1): $\det U \cdot \det U^{-1} = \det I = 1$ is a product of two integers, so both are $\pm 1$. (2) implies (3): $U^{-1}$ maps $\mathbb{Z}^n$ to $\mathbb{Z}^n$ and inverts $U$. (3) implies (2): the preimages of the unit vectors $e_1, \ldots, e_n$ are integer vectors, and they are the columns of $U^{-1}$. (4) implies (1): each elementary matrix has determinant $\pm 1$. (1) implies (4): by induction on $n$. The first column of $U$ has entries with greatest common divisor $1$, since that divisor divides $\det U$ by expansion along the column. Run the [Euclidean algorithm](/extended-euclidean-algorithm/) on that column with row operations (subtract a multiple of one row from another, swap rows) until it is $(\pm 1, 0, \ldots, 0)^T$; negate the first row if needed. Then clear the rest of row $1$ by subtracting integer multiples of column $1$ from the other columns; column operations are right multiplications by elementary matrices. What is left is $\operatorname{diag}(1, U')$ with $U'$ unimodular of size $n - 1$. So $E U F = \operatorname{diag}(1, U')$ with $E$ and $F$ products of elementary matrices, and $U = E^{-1} \operatorname{diag}(1, U') F^{-1}$, where each inverse of an elementary matrix is elementary. $\square$

**Over other rings.** Over a field every invertible matrix is unimodular. Over $K[x]$ the unimodular matrices are those with a nonzero constant determinant. Over every Euclidean domain condition (4) still holds by the same proof, but over some principal ideal domains $SL_2(R)$ is not generated by elementary matrices (Cohn, 1966, for the ring of integers of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-19})$).

**Two facts used everywhere.**

- **Change of basis.** Two $n \times k$ integer matrices $B$ and $B'$ of rank $k$ have the same column lattice $B \mathbb{Z}^k = B' \mathbb{Z}^k$ if and only if $B' = B U$ for a $k \times k$ unimodular $U$. *Proof.* If the lattices are equal, $B' = B U$ and $B = B' V$ for integer $U$ and $V$, so $B = B U V$, and since the columns of $B$ are independent, $U V = I$, and $U$ is unimodular by (2). The converse follows from (3).
- **Content is preserved.** For $x \in \mathbb{Z}^n$ and unimodular $U$, $\gcd(U x) = \gcd(x)$, since each divides every entry of the other vector by (2). A vector with $\gcd(x) = 1$, called *primitive*, is the first column of some unimodular matrix: the Euclidean row operations of the proof above give $E x = \pm e_1$, so $\pm E^{-1}$ has first column $x$.

## Examples and non-examples

1. $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $1$ and inverse $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. It factors into elementary matrices as $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. Permutation matrices, diagonal matrices with entries $\pm 1$, and triangular integer matrices with $\pm 1$ on the diagonal are unimodular.
3. **Completing a primitive vector.** $(3, 5)$ is primitive, and $\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $6 - 5 = 1$. The second column comes from Bezout: $3 \cdot 2 - 5 \cdot 1 = 1$.
4. **A unimodular matrix with large entries.** $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1000 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is unimodular; the size of the entries is not bounded, which is why normal form algorithms must control the entries of the transforms they return.
5. **Non-example: invertible over $\mathbb{Q}$ only.** $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $5$, so its inverse $\tfrac15 \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ is not integral. Its columns span a sublattice of index $5$ in $\mathbb{Z}^2$. It is unimodular over $\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}$ exactly when $\gcd(5, N) = 1$.
6. **Non-example: determinant $\pm 1$ but not integral.** $\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac12, 2)$ has determinant $1$ and is not unimodular over $\mathbb{Z}$.
7. **Polynomial unimodular matrices.** $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & x \\ x & x^2 + 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $1$, so it is unimodular over $K[x]$, and its inverse $\begin{pmatrix} x^2 + 1 & -x \\ -x & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is a polynomial matrix.

## Related definitions

- **Unimodular equivalence.** $A$ and $B$ are *equivalent* if $B = U A V$ with $U$, $V$ unimodular, and *row equivalent* if $B = U A$. The [Smith normal form](/smith-normal-form/) is the canonical form for equivalence, and the [Hermite normal form](/hermite-normal-form/) for row equivalence.
- **Lattice bases.** Bases of a [lattice](/lattice-basis/) differ by unimodular matrices, so the lattice determinant $|\det B|$ is an invariant, and [size reduction](/size-reduction/) and the [LLL algorithm](/lll-algorithm/) apply unimodular transforms.
- **Totally unimodular matrices.** A different notion: a matrix, not necessarily square, all of whose square submatrices have determinant $0$, $1$ or $-1$. Linear programs with such constraint matrices and integer right-hand sides have integer vertices (Hoffman and Kruskal, 1956); see Schrijver, Chapter 19.
- **Linear Diophantine systems.** A unimodular change of variables $x = V y$ turns $A x = c$ into a diagonal system; see [linear Diophantine system](/linear-diophantine-system/).
