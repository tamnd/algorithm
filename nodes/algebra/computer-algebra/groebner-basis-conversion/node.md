Gröbner basis conversion is the problem of turning a Gröbner basis of an ideal for one monomial order into the Gröbner basis of the same ideal for another order. It matters because the orders that are cheap to compute with, above all grevlex, are not the ones that answer the questions users ask: elimination and solving need lex or block orders, whose bases are usually far more expensive to compute directly. The standard strategy for solving a polynomial system is therefore *compute in grevlex, then convert*. For zero-dimensional ideals the FGLM algorithm (1993) does the conversion by linear algebra in the finite-dimensional quotient ring; for ideals of any dimension the Gröbner walk (1997) and Hilbert-driven methods are used.

## In plain words

A Gröbner basis is a rewritten form of a system of polynomial equations, and the rewriting depends on a rule for ordering the terms. One rule makes the computation fast. Another rule makes the answer useful, for example by isolating an equation in one unknown that can be solved first.

Conversion means doing the fast computation first and then turning its result into the useful form. When the system has finitely many solutions, the turning step is ordinary linear algebra with a matrix whose size is the number of solutions. That step is usually much cheaper than computing the useful form from scratch.

## Definition

Let $K$ be a field and $R = K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$. For a monomial order $>$, $\operatorname{LT}(f)$, $\operatorname{LM}(f)$ and $\operatorname{LC}(f)$ are the leading term, leading monomial and leading coefficient of $f$, so $\operatorname{LT}(f) = \operatorname{LC}(f) \operatorname{LM}(f)$. The orders in use are lex, grlex, grevlex, and block or weight orders built from them; see [monomial ordering](/monomial-ordering/).

**Input:** two monomial orders $>_1$ and $>_2$, and the [reduced Gröbner basis](/reduced-groebner-basis/) $G_1$ of an ideal $I \subseteq R$ with respect to $>_1$.
**Output:** the reduced Gröbner basis $G_2$ of $I$ with respect to $>_2$.

The ideal $I$ is *zero-dimensional* if $V(I)$ is finite over the algebraic closure of $K$, which holds if and only if the quotient $R/I$ has finite dimension $D$ as a $K$-vector space. Then $D$ is the number of standard monomials, those not divisible by any $\operatorname{LM}(g)$ for $g \in G_1$, and it counts the solutions with multiplicity. By Bézout's theorem, $D \le d^n$ if $I$ is generated by $n$ polynomials of degree at most $d$.

Costs are counted in operations in $K$. The parameters are $n$, $D$, the degree bound $d$ of the input, and the number of terms $t$ of the polynomials.

## Variants

- **Zero-dimensional conversion,** the main case, solved by [FGLM](/fglm-algorithm/).
- **Conversion to lex in shape position.** When the lex basis has the form $\{x_1 - g_1(x_n), \ldots, x_{n-1} - g_{n-1}(x_n), h(x_n)\}$ with $\deg h = D$, which holds after a generic linear change of coordinates for radical ideals, only the minimal polynomial of $x_n$ and $n - 1$ further vectors are needed. This is the setting of the fast and sparse variants and of the [rational univariate representation](/rational-univariate-representation/).
- **Positive-dimensional conversion,** solved by the [Gröbner walk](/groebner-walk/), which moves through a sequence of intermediate orders, or by Hilbert-driven Buchberger (Traverso 1997), which uses the [Hilbert series](/hilbert-series/) known from $G_1$ to discard useless S-pairs.
- **Conversion to an elimination order** only, to obtain $I \cap K[x_{k+1}, \ldots, x_n]$; see [elimination ideal](/elimination-ideal/).

## Examples

Let $I = \langle x^2 + y^2 - 4,\ xy - 1 \rangle \subseteq \mathbb{Q}[x, y]$, with $x > y$. A script running Buchberger's algorithm in exact rational arithmetic gives these reduced bases:

| order | reduced Gröbner basis | standard monomials |
|---|---|---|
| grevlex | $x^2 + y^2 - 4$, $xy - 1$, $y^3 + x - 4y$ | $1, y, y^2, x$ |
| lex | $x + y^3 - 4y$, $y^4 - 4y^2 + 1$ | $1, y, y^2, y^3$ |

So $D = 4$, the four intersection points of a circle and a hyperbola. Conversion from grevlex to lex by FGLM computes normal forms with respect to the grevlex basis, in increasing lex order of monomials:

- $\operatorname{NF}(1) = 1$, $\operatorname{NF}(y) = y$, $\operatorname{NF}(y^2) = y^2$, $\operatorname{NF}(y^3) = -x + 4y$, all linearly independent;
- $\operatorname{NF}(y^4) = y \cdot (-x + 4y)$ reduced, which is $-1 + 4y^2$. This is a linear combination of earlier normal forms: $\operatorname{NF}(y^4) - 4 \operatorname{NF}(y^2) + \operatorname{NF}(1) = 0$, which gives the basis element $y^4 - 4y^2 + 1$;
- $\operatorname{NF}(x) = x = -\operatorname{NF}(y^3) + 4 \operatorname{NF}(y)$, which gives $x + y^3 - 4y$.

Every other monomial is a multiple of $y^4$ or $x$, so the lex basis is complete. The ideal is in shape position, and the solutions are the roots $y$ of $y^4 - 4y^2 + 1$ with $x = 4y - y^3$, which equals $1/y$ on these roots.

## Known algorithms

- **Buchberger or F4 from scratch** for $>_2$, ignoring $G_1$. Correct but often much slower for lex.
- **FGLM** (Faugère, Gianni, Lazard and Mora 1993). Enumerate monomials in increasing $>_2$ order, compute their normal forms modulo $G_1$ as vectors in $K^D$ using the multiplication matrices of $R/I$, and detect linear dependencies by incremental Gaussian elimination. Cost $O(n D^3)$ operations in $K$, proved.
- **Fast FGLM.** For ideals in shape position, compute the powers of the multiplication matrix by $x_n$ applied to a vector by repeated squaring and fast matrix multiplication, in $\tilde{O}(D^\omega)$ operations (Faugère, Gaudry, Huot and Renault 2014), where $\omega$ is the matrix multiplication exponent.
- **Sparse FGLM** (Faugère and Mou 2011, 2017). The multiplication matrices are often sparse; compute the sequence $u^T M^i v$, find the minimal polynomial with [Berlekamp-Massey](/berlekamp-massey/), and recover the other coordinates by solving structured systems, as in the [Wiedemann algorithm](/wiedemann-algorithm/).
- **Gröbner walk** (Collart, Kalkbrener and Mall 1997). Follow a straight path in the Gröbner fan from a weight vector for $>_1$ to one for $>_2$; at each wall, compute a Gröbner basis of the initial ideal, which is small, and lift it. Works in any dimension; the number of walls crossed can be large.
- **Hilbert-driven Buchberger** (Traverso 1997). The Hilbert function of $R/I$ does not depend on the order, so it is known from $G_1$; it tells in each degree how many new leading monomials remain and allows the algorithm to stop reducing pairs as soon as they are found. It requires homogeneous input or homogenization.

## Bounds

- **Zero-dimensional.** FGLM uses $O(n D^3)$ field operations; the fast variant uses $\tilde{O}(D^\omega)$ in shape position. These bounds are proved. With $D \le d^n$, the cost of converting is single exponential in $n$, matching Lakshman's bound $d^{O(n)}$ for computing zero-dimensional Gröbner bases.
- **Output size.** In shape position the lex basis has $n$ polynomials with $D$ coefficients each, so $\Theta(n D)$ field elements are a lower bound for any conversion to lex. Over $\mathbb{Q}$, the lex coefficients are typically much larger than the grevlex ones; this growth is observed, and it motivates the rational univariate representation.
- **Positive-dimensional.** In general the worst case of Gröbner bases is doubly exponential in $n$ (Mayr and Meyer 1982), for any order, and conversion cannot avoid output of that size. The walk has no useful worst-case bound in terms of the input; its practical cost depends on the number of fan walls along the path.
- **Why convert at all.** Computing grevlex first is supported by the observed and partly proved advantage of grevlex: for ideals in generic coordinates its degrees are bounded by the Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity (Bayer and Stillman 1987), while lex degrees can be much larger.

## Reductions

- **Zero-dimensional conversion to linear algebra.** Through the multiplication matrices $M_{x_i}$ of $R/I$, the problem becomes finding linear dependencies among vectors $M^{\alpha} e_1$, so the tools of dense, sparse and structured linear algebra apply.
- **Conversion to minimal polynomial computation.** In shape position the last lex polynomial is the minimal polynomial of $M_{x_n}$, computable by Krylov methods.
- **Elimination and solving to conversion.** Computing an elimination ideal or a triangular description of $V(I)$ reduces to computing $G_1$ in grevlex and converting; see [polynomial system solving](/polynomial-system-solving/).

## Applications

- **Solving polynomial systems:** grevlex by F4 or F5, then FGLM to lex or to a rational univariate representation.
- **Algebraic cryptanalysis,** where the systems are zero-dimensional over finite fields and the conversion step is often a measurable part of the cost.
- **Implicitization and elimination** in geometric modeling, where the walk converts to elimination orders.
- **Computing many orders at once,** as in tropical geometry, by moving through the Gröbner fan.
