A reduced Gröbner basis of a [polynomial ideal](/polynomial-ideal/) $I$ is a [Gröbner basis](/groebner-basis/) $G$ in which each element is monic and no term of any element lies in the ideal generated by the [leading terms](/leading-term/) of the other elements. For a fixed [monomial order](/monomial-ordering/), each ideal has exactly one reduced Gröbner basis. So it is a [canonical form](/canonical-form/) of the ideal: two sets of polynomials generate the same ideal if and only if their reduced Gröbner bases are equal. For linear polynomials it is the [reduced row echelon form](/reduced-row-echelon-form/) of the coefficient matrix.

## Definition

**Notation.** $K$ is a field and $K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is the polynomial ring over $K$. Fix a monomial order $>$, named when it matters: lex, grlex or grevlex. For nonzero $f$, $\operatorname{LT}(f)$, $\operatorname{LM}(f)$ and $\operatorname{LC}(f)$ are the leading term, monomial and coefficient. For an ideal $I$, $\langle \operatorname{LT}(I) \rangle$ is the ideal generated by the leading terms of the nonzero elements of $I$. $\overline{f}^{G}$ is the remainder of $f$ on [division](/multivariate-division-algorithm/) by a list $G$.

**Minimal Gröbner basis.** A Gröbner basis $G$ of $I$ is *minimal* when, for each $g \in G$, $\operatorname{LC}(g) = 1$ and $\operatorname{LT}(g) \notin \langle \operatorname{LT}(G \setminus \{g\}) \rangle$.

**Reduced Gröbner basis.** A Gröbner basis $G$ of $I$ is *reduced* when, for each $g \in G$,

1. $\operatorname{LC}(g) = 1$, and
2. no monomial that appears in $g$ lies in $\langle \operatorname{LT}(G \setminus \{g\}) \rangle$.

Condition 2 says that no term of $g$ is divisible by the leading monomial of another element. A reduced basis is minimal, and each element of it equals its own remainder on division by the others.

**Theorem (existence and uniqueness).** Let $I \ne \{0\}$ be an ideal of $K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ and fix a monomial order. Then $I$ has a reduced Gröbner basis, and it is unique. (The zero ideal has the empty set as its reduced basis.)

*Proof of existence.* Start from any finite Gröbner basis of $I$, which exists by the [Hilbert basis theorem](/hilbert-basis-theorem/) and [Buchberger's algorithm](/buchberger-algorithm/). Divide each element by its leading coefficient. While some $g$ has $\operatorname{LT}(g) \in \langle \operatorname{LT}(G \setminus \{g\}) \rangle$, delete $g$. The set of leading terms still generates $\langle \operatorname{LT}(I) \rangle$, because $\operatorname{LT}(g)$ is a multiple of one of the other leading terms. Each deletion lowers $|G|$ by 1, so this stops with a minimal basis. Now take its elements one at a time, and replace $g$ by $g' = \overline{g}^{\,G \setminus \{g\}}$. Because $\operatorname{LT}(g)$ is not divisible by any other leading monomial, division never touches it, so $\operatorname{LT}(g') = \operatorname{LT}(g)$, the set of leading terms does not change, $G$ stays a minimal Gröbner basis, and no monomial of $g'$ lies in $\langle \operatorname{LT}(G \setminus \{g\}) \rangle$. A later step does not undo this, because it does not change any leading term. After one pass, every element is reduced. $\square$

*Proof of uniqueness.* First, two minimal Gröbner bases $G$ and $\tilde G$ of $I$ have the same set of leading monomials. Take $g \in G$. Because $\tilde G$ is a Gröbner basis, $\operatorname{LM}(\tilde g) \mid \operatorname{LM}(g)$ for some $\tilde g \in \tilde G$. In the same way $\operatorname{LM}(h) \mid \operatorname{LM}(\tilde g)$ for some $h \in G$. Then $\operatorname{LM}(h) \mid \operatorname{LM}(g)$, and minimality of $G$ forces $h = g$, so $\operatorname{LM}(g) = \operatorname{LM}(\tilde g)$. By symmetry the two sets of leading monomials are equal.

Now let $G$ and $\tilde G$ be reduced, and let $g \in G$ and $\tilde g \in \tilde G$ have the same leading monomial. Then $g - \tilde g \in I$, and its leading terms cancel because both are monic. Each monomial of $g - \tilde g$ is a non-leading monomial of $g$ or of $\tilde g$. By condition 2 none of them is divisible by a leading monomial of $G \setminus \{g\}$, nor of $\tilde G \setminus \{\tilde g\}$, which is the same set of monomials. None is divisible by $\operatorname{LM}(g)$ either, because each is smaller than $\operatorname{LM}(g)$ in the order. So $g - \tilde g$ has no term in $\langle \operatorname{LT}(I) \rangle$. A nonzero element of $I$ has its leading term there, so $g - \tilde g = 0$. Hence $G = \tilde G$. $\blacksquare$

## Examples and non-examples

- **Linear equations.** In $\mathbb{Q}[x, y, z]$ with lex and $x > y > z$, the ideal $\langle x + y + z - 6, \; x - y + 2z - 5, \; 2x + y - z - 1 \rangle$ has the reduced basis $\{x - 1, \; y - 2, \; z - 3\}$: the system has the unique solution $(1, 2, 3)$. The ideal $\langle x + 2y - z - 1, \; 2x + 4y + z - 5 \rangle$ has the reduced basis $\{x + 2y - 2, \; z - 1\}$, which is the reduced row echelon form of the two rows, with pivots on $x$ and $z$.
- **Two generators, a smaller basis.** With lex and $x > y$, $\langle x^2 y - 1, \; x y^2 - x \rangle = \langle x^2 - y, \; y^2 - 1 \rangle$, and $\{x^2 - y, \, y^2 - 1\}$ is the reduced basis. The reduced basis does not keep the input generators.
- **The order matters.** For $I = \langle x^2 - y, \; xy - 1 \rangle$ the reduced basis is $\{x - y^2, \; y^3 - 1\}$ for lex with $x > y$, and $\{x^2 - y, \; xy - 1, \; y^2 - x\}$ for grevlex with $x > y$. Both are reduced, for different orders.
- **Edge cases.** The unit ideal $\langle 1 \rangle = K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ has the reduced basis $\{1\}$, for every order. So a system of equations has no solution over the algebraic closure of $K$ exactly when its reduced basis is $\{1\}$, by the [Nullstellensatz](/hilbert-nullstellensatz/). A principal ideal $\langle f \rangle$ with $f \ne 0$ has the reduced basis $\{ f / \operatorname{LC}(f) \}$: for example $\langle 2x^2 + 4xy \rangle$ has $\{x^2 + 2xy\}$.
- **Non-example: a Gröbner basis that is not reduced.** For lex with $x > y$, $\{x^2 - y, \; xy - 1, \; x - y^2, \; y^3 - 1\}$ is a Gröbner basis of $\langle x^2 - y, xy - 1 \rangle$: every S-polynomial of it reduces to zero. It is not minimal, because $\operatorname{LT}(x^2 - y) = x^2$ is a multiple of $\operatorname{LT}(x - y^2) = x$.
- **Non-example: minimal but not reduced.** For lex with $x > y$, the set $\{x + y^3 - y^2 - 1, \; y^3 - 1\}$ lies in the same ideal, because $x + y^3 - y^2 - 1 = (x - y^2) + (y^3 - 1)$, and it has the same leading terms $x$ and $y^3$ as the reduced basis, so it is a minimal Gröbner basis. It is not reduced, because its first element has the term $y^3$, which is divisible by $\operatorname{LT}(y^3 - 1)$.

## Related definitions

- [Gröbner basis](/groebner-basis/) and [S-polynomial](/s-polynomial/): the reduced basis is a special Gröbner basis, and Buchberger's criterion tests it.
- [Ideal membership](/ideal-membership-problem/): with any Gröbner basis, $f \in I$ exactly when $\overline{f}^G = 0$. The reduced basis makes the remainder independent of the order of $G$ and gives one normal form for each class of $K[x_1, \ldots, x_n] / I$.
- [Groebner basis conversion](/groebner-basis-conversion/), [FGLM](/fglm-algorithm/) and the [Gröbner walk](/groebner-walk/): change the reduced basis from one order to another.
- [Elimination theorem](/elimination-theorem/): the reduced lex basis, intersected with $K[x_{k+1}, \ldots, x_n]$, is the reduced basis of the elimination ideal.
- [Reduced row echelon form](/reduced-row-echelon-form/): the case of linear polynomials.
