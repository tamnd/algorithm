A Gröbner basis of a [polynomial ideal](/polynomial-ideal/) $I$ is a finite subset $G \subseteq I$ whose [leading terms](/leading-term/) generate the ideal of all leading terms of $I$, for a fixed [monomial order](/monomial-ordering/). With such a set, [multivariate division](/multivariate-division-algorithm/) gives a unique remainder, so a polynomial lies in $I$ exactly when its remainder is zero. Every ideal has one, and [Buchberger's algorithm](/buchberger-algorithm/) computes one from any finite set of generators. A Gröbner basis plays the role for polynomials in several variables that the echelon form plays for linear equations and the gcd plays for polynomials in one variable.

## Definition

**Notation.** $K$ is a field and $K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is the polynomial ring over $K$. A [monomial](/monomial/) is $x^\alpha = x_1^{\alpha_1} \cdots x_n^{\alpha_n}$ with $\alpha \in \mathbb{N}^n$. Fix a [monomial order](/monomial-ordering/) $>$, named explicitly when it matters: lex, grlex or grevlex. For a nonzero $f = \sum_\alpha c_\alpha x^\alpha$, the leading monomial $\operatorname{LM}(f)$ is the largest $x^\alpha$ with $c_\alpha \ne 0$, the leading coefficient $\operatorname{LC}(f)$ is its coefficient, and the leading term is $\operatorname{LT}(f) = \operatorname{LC}(f) \operatorname{LM}(f)$. For an ideal $I$, $\operatorname{LT}(I) = \{\operatorname{LT}(f) : f \in I, f \ne 0\}$, and $\langle \operatorname{LT}(I) \rangle$ is the ideal that it generates. This ideal is a *monomial ideal*: it is generated by monomials, and a monomial lies in it exactly when some generator divides it.

**Definition.** Let $I \subseteq K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ be an ideal. A finite set $G = \{g_1, \ldots, g_t\} \subseteq I \setminus \{0\}$ is a *Gröbner basis* of $I$ for $>$ when

$$\langle \operatorname{LT}(g_1), \ldots, \operatorname{LT}(g_t) \rangle = \langle \operatorname{LT}(I) \rangle.$$

By convention the empty set is a Gröbner basis of the zero ideal. The inclusion $\subseteq$ always holds, because each $g_i$ is in $I$. So the condition says: the leading term of each nonzero element of $I$ is divisible by $\operatorname{LT}(g_i)$ for some $i$.

**Equivalent conditions.** For a finite $G \subseteq I \setminus \{0\}$ the following are equivalent.

1. $G$ is a Gröbner basis of $I$.
2. For each nonzero $f \in I$, some $\operatorname{LT}(g_i)$ divides $\operatorname{LT}(f)$.
3. For each $f \in K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, the remainder of $f$ on division by $G$ is zero exactly when $f \in I$, whatever the order of the divisors.
4. $G$ generates $I$, and for each pair $i \ne j$ the [S-polynomial](/s-polynomial/) $S(g_i, g_j)$ has remainder zero on division by $G$.

*Proof of 1 ⇔ 2.* A monomial lies in the monomial ideal $\langle \operatorname{LT}(g_1), \ldots, \operatorname{LT}(g_t) \rangle$ exactly when one of the generators divides it (Cox, Little and O'Shea, Chapter 2, Section 4). So 2 says that each element of $\operatorname{LT}(I)$ lies in this ideal, which is 1.

*Proof of 2 ⇒ 3.* Division by $G$ in any order gives $f = q_1 g_1 + \cdots + q_t g_t + r$, where no term of $r$ is divisible by any $\operatorname{LT}(g_i)$. If $r = 0$, then $f \in I$ because each $g_i \in I$. If $f \in I$, then $r = f - \sum q_i g_i \in I$. If $r$ were nonzero, then by 2 some $\operatorname{LT}(g_i)$ would divide $\operatorname{LT}(r)$, which is a term of $r$. That is a contradiction, so $r = 0$.

*Proof of 3 ⇒ 2.* Let $f \in I$ be nonzero. By 3 the division gives $f = \sum q_i g_i$ with remainder zero. The division algorithm also gives $\operatorname{LM}(q_i g_i) \le \operatorname{LM}(f)$ for each $i$ with $q_i g_i \ne 0$. The monomial $\operatorname{LM}(f)$ appears on the left, so it appears on the right, so $\operatorname{LM}(f) = \operatorname{LM}(q_i) \operatorname{LM}(g_i)$ for some $i$. Then $\operatorname{LT}(g_i)$ divides $\operatorname{LT}(f)$.

*1 ⇔ 4* is Buchberger's criterion. Its proof is on the page [Buchberger's criteria](/buchberger-criteria/). The fact that $G$ generates $I$ follows from 3: each $f \in I$ is $\sum q_i g_i$.

**Unique remainder.** If $G$ is a Gröbner basis and $r, r'$ are two remainders of $f$ (from two orders of division), then $r - r' \in I$ and no term of $r - r'$ is divisible by any $\operatorname{LT}(g_i)$. The argument of 2 ⇒ 3 gives $r = r'$. This remainder is the *normal form* of $f$ modulo $I$, written $\overline{f}^G$. It depends on $I$ and $>$ only, not on the choice of $G$. The monomials not divisible by any $\operatorname{LT}(g_i)$, the *standard monomials*, form a basis of the vector space $K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]/I$.

**Existence.** The ideal $\langle \operatorname{LT}(I) \rangle$ is generated by finitely many monomials, by Dickson's lemma, and each of them can be taken as $\operatorname{LT}(g)$ for some $g \in I$. These $g$ form a Gröbner basis. The same argument proves the [Hilbert basis theorem](/hilbert-basis-theorem/) (Cox, Little and O'Shea, Chapter 2, Section 5). This proof does not give an algorithm; [Buchberger's algorithm](/buchberger-algorithm/) does.

## Examples and non-examples

**A basis with grlex.** Take $K = \mathbb{Q}$, $x > y$, the grlex order, and $I = \langle f_1, f_2 \rangle$ with $f_1 = x^3 - 2xy$ and $f_2 = x^2 y - 2y^2 + x$, the running example of Cox, Little and O'Shea, Chapter 2.

- *Non-example.* $\{f_1, f_2\}$ is not a Gröbner basis. The polynomial $y f_1 - x f_2 = -x^2$ lies in $I$, and its leading term $-x^2$ is divisible by neither $x^3$ nor $x^2 y$. Division of $-x^2$ by $\{f_1, f_2\}$ leaves the remainder $-x^2 \ne 0$, although $-x^2 \in I$: condition 3 fails.
- *Example.* Buchberger's algorithm adds $-x^2$, $-2xy$ and $-2y^2 + x$, and stops with five polynomials, all of whose S-polynomials reduce to zero. After it removes redundant elements and makes each leading coefficient 1, the [reduced Gröbner basis](/reduced-groebner-basis/) is $G = \{x^2,\ xy,\ y^2 - \tfrac12 x\}$. So $\langle \operatorname{LT}(I) \rangle = \langle x^2, xy, y^2 \rangle$, the standard monomials are $1, x, y$, and $\dim_{\mathbb{Q}} \mathbb{Q}[x, y]/I = 3$.
- *Membership.* The remainder of $y^3$ on division by $G$ is $0$ (two steps: $y^3 - y(y^2 - \tfrac12 x) = \tfrac12 xy$, then $\tfrac12 xy - \tfrac12 (xy) = 0$), so $y^3 \in I$. The remainder of $x + y$ is $x + y$, so $x + y \notin I$. The remainder of $x^3 y + y^2$ is $\tfrac12 x$, so it is not in $I$ either.

**The order matters.** For the lex order with $x > y$, the reduced Gröbner basis of the same $I$ is $\{x - 2y^2,\ y^3\}$, and $\langle \operatorname{LT}(I) \rangle = \langle x, y^3 \rangle$. The set $\{x^2, xy, y^2 - \tfrac12 x\}$ still generates $I$, but under lex its leading terms are $x^2$, $xy$ and $-\tfrac12 x$. They generate $\langle x \rangle$, which does not contain $y^3$, so it is not a lex Gröbner basis. Division of $y^3 \in I$ by this set under lex leaves $y^3$.

**Order of division.** With lex, $x > y$, divide $f = xy^2 - x$ by $(xy + 1, y^2 - 1)$: the remainder is $-x - y$. Divide by $(y^2 - 1, xy + 1)$: the remainder is $0$. So $\{xy + 1, y^2 - 1\}$ is not a Gröbner basis, because condition 3 fails for the first order. Its reduced Gröbner basis is $\{x + y,\ y^2 - 1\}$, and with it every order gives the remainder $0$.

**Special cases.**

- *One variable.* For $n = 1$, every ideal of $K[x]$ is $\langle h \rangle$ with $h$ the gcd of the generators, and $\{h\}$ is a Gröbner basis. Buchberger's algorithm on two polynomials then does the work of the [Euclidean algorithm](/polynomial-euclidean-algorithm/).
- *Linear polynomials.* If all generators have degree 1, a Gröbner basis for lex is the set of rows of an echelon form of the coefficient matrix, as computed by [Gaussian elimination](/gaussian-elimination/).
- *Principal ideal.* $\{f\}$ is a Gröbner basis of $\langle f \rangle$ for every order, because $\operatorname{LT}(gf) = \operatorname{LT}(g) \operatorname{LT}(f)$.
- *Unit ideal.* $I = K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ exactly when its reduced Gröbner basis is $\{1\}$. A Gröbner basis of the unit ideal must contain a nonzero constant.
- *Monomials.* Each finite set of monomials is a Gröbner basis of the ideal it generates.

### Common errors

- A Gröbner basis is not a basis in the sense of linear algebra: its elements are not independent, and $I$ has many Gröbner bases. Only the reduced one is unique.
- The property depends on the order. A set can be a Gröbner basis for grevlex and not for lex, as above.
- A generating set whose leading terms generate $\langle \operatorname{LT}(f_1), \ldots, \operatorname{LT}(f_s) \rangle$ is not enough. The condition is about the leading terms of all elements of $I$, and these can be smaller than the leading terms of the generators after cancellation.

## Related definitions

- [Monomial order](/monomial-ordering/) and [leading term](/leading-term/): the data that the definition fixes.
- [S-polynomial](/s-polynomial/): the combination of two polynomials that cancels their leading terms. Its remainders are the test of condition 4.
- [Reduced Gröbner basis](/reduced-groebner-basis/): the unique Gröbner basis with leading coefficients 1 and no term of any element divisible by the leading term of another.
- [Ideal membership problem](/ideal-membership-problem/): decided by condition 3.
- [Elimination ideal](/elimination-ideal/) and [elimination theorem](/elimination-theorem/): a lex Gröbner basis contains a Gröbner basis of each elimination ideal.
- [Gröbner basis computation](/groebner-basis-computation/): the problem of finding one, with its cost.
- [Hilbert series](/hilbert-series/): read off from $\langle \operatorname{LT}(I) \rangle$, so from any Gröbner basis.

### History

Bruno Buchberger defined these bases and gave the algorithm in his 1965 PhD thesis at the University of Innsbruck, and named them after his advisor Wolfgang Gröbner. Heisuke Hironaka introduced the same idea for power series rings in 1964 under the name *standard basis*, which is why that name is an alias.
