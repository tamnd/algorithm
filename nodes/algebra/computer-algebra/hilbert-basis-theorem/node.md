The Hilbert basis theorem says that each ideal of the polynomial ring $R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ over a Noetherian ring $R$ is finitely generated. Over a field $K$ this means that each [polynomial ideal](/polynomial-ideal/), and so each system of polynomial equations, however large, is equivalent to a finite one. The proof over a field through Dickson's lemma also shows that each ideal has a finite [Gröbner basis](/groebner-basis/), and the ascending chain condition that follows from the theorem is the reason that [Buchberger's algorithm](/buchberger-algorithm/) stops.

## Statement

A commutative ring $R$ with $1$ is *Noetherian* when each ideal of $R$ is finitely generated. Each field is Noetherian (its only ideals are $\langle 0 \rangle$ and $\langle 1 \rangle$), and so is $\mathbb{Z}$ (each ideal is $\langle m \rangle$).

**Theorem (Hilbert, 1890).** If $R$ is a Noetherian commutative ring, then $R[x]$ is Noetherian. By induction on $n$, so is $R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$. In particular, for a field $K$, each ideal $I \subseteq K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ has the form $I = \langle g_1, \ldots, g_t \rangle$ for finitely many $g_i \in I$.

**Lemma (Dickson, 1913).** Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{N}^n$. The monomial ideal $\langle x^\alpha : \alpha \in A \rangle \subseteq K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is generated by $x^{\alpha(1)}, \ldots, x^{\alpha(s)}$ for finitely many $\alpha(1), \ldots, \alpha(s) \in A$. Equivalently, $A$ has only finitely many elements that are minimal for the componentwise order.

**Corollary (ascending chain condition).** In a Noetherian ring each chain of ideals $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq I_3 \subseteq \cdots$ is constant from some index $N$ on: $I_N = I_{N+1} = \cdots$.

**Corollary (Gröbner bases exist).** Fix a [monomial order](/monomial-ordering/) on $K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$. Each ideal $I \ne \langle 0 \rangle$ has a finite subset $G$ with $\langle \operatorname{LT}(g) : g \in G \rangle = \langle \operatorname{LT}(I) \rangle$, and each such $G$ generates $I$.

## In plain words

You can describe any set of polynomial equations by a finite list of them. Start from an infinite list of equations. The theorem says that some finite part of the list already implies all the others. The same is true for any set of patterns of powers of variables: among them, finitely many are minimal, and every pattern in the set is a multiple of one of these. A process that keeps adding new consequences, each not implied by the earlier ones, must therefore stop after finitely many steps. That is why the standard algorithm for these bases always ends.

## Proof

**The ascending chain condition.** Let $R$ be Noetherian and $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq \cdots$ a chain of ideals. The union $U = \bigcup_k I_k$ is an ideal: two elements of $U$ lie in a common $I_k$, because the chain is increasing. So $U = \langle u_1, \ldots, u_m \rangle$. Each $u_j$ lies in some $I_{k_j}$. Let $N = \max_j k_j$. Then $U \subseteq I_N \subseteq I_{N+k} \subseteq U$ for each $k \ge 0$, so the chain is constant from $N$ on. The converse also holds: if $R$ has an ideal $J$ that is not finitely generated, pick $a_1 \in J$ and then $a_{k+1} \in J \setminus \langle a_1, \ldots, a_k \rangle$, which is never empty. The chain $\langle a_1 \rangle \subsetneq \langle a_1, a_2 \rangle \subsetneq \cdots$ never becomes constant.

**$R$ Noetherian implies $R[x]$ Noetherian.** Assume that an ideal $I \subseteq R[x]$ is not finitely generated. Choose $f_1 \in I$ of smallest degree, and for $k \ge 1$ choose $f_{k+1} \in I \setminus \langle f_1, \ldots, f_k \rangle$ of smallest degree. This set is never empty, because $I$ is not finitely generated. Let $d_k = \deg f_k$ and $a_k = \operatorname{LC}(f_k)$. Then $d_1 \le d_2 \le \cdots$: the polynomial $f_{k+1}$ was also a candidate at step $k$, since it is not in the smaller ideal $\langle f_1, \ldots, f_{k-1} \rangle$, so $d_k \le d_{k+1}$. The chain $\langle a_1 \rangle \subseteq \langle a_1, a_2 \rangle \subseteq \cdots$ in $R$ is constant from some $N$ on, so $a_{N+1} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} r_i a_i$ with $r_i \in R$. Put

$$g = f_{N+1} - \sum_{i=1}^{N} r_i x^{d_{N+1} - d_i} f_i.$$

The exponents $d_{N+1} - d_i$ are $\ge 0$. The coefficient of $x^{d_{N+1}}$ in $g$ is $a_{N+1} - \sum r_i a_i = 0$, so $\deg g < d_{N+1}$ (or $g = 0$). The sum lies in $\langle f_1, \ldots, f_N \rangle$ and $f_{N+1}$ does not, so $g \in I \setminus \langle f_1, \ldots, f_N \rangle$, and in particular $g \ne 0$. This contradicts the choice of $f_{N+1}$ with the smallest degree. So $I$ is finitely generated. Induction on $n$ with $R[x_1, \ldots, x_n] = R[x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1}][x_n]$ gives the multivariate case.

**Dickson's lemma.** Let $I = \langle x^\alpha : \alpha \in A \rangle$. A monomial $x^\beta$ lies in $I$ exactly when some $x^\alpha$ with $\alpha \in A$ divides it: a monomial of $\sum_\alpha h_\alpha x^\alpha$ is a monomial of some $h_\alpha x^\alpha$, so it is divisible by $x^\alpha$. We prove by induction on $n$ that $I$ is generated by finitely many monomials. For $n = 1$, $I = \langle x_1^b \rangle$ where $b$ is the smallest element of $A$. For $n > 1$, write a monomial as $x^\alpha y^m$ with $x^\alpha$ in $x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1}$ and $y = x_n$. Let $J \subseteq K[x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1}]$ be generated by the $x^\alpha$ with $x^\alpha y^m \in I$ for some $m$. By induction $J = \langle x^{\alpha(1)}, \ldots, x^{\alpha(s)} \rangle$, with $x^{\alpha(i)} y^{m_i} \in I$. Let $m = \max_i m_i$. For $0 \le k < m$, let $J_k$ be generated by the $x^\beta$ with $x^\beta y^k \in I$. By induction $J_k = \langle x^{\beta_k(1)}, \ldots, x^{\beta_k(s_k)} \rangle$. We claim that $I$ is generated by the finite list $L$ of the monomials $x^{\alpha(i)} y^m$ and $x^{\beta_k(j)} y^k$, all of which lie in $I$. Let $x^\alpha y^p \in I$. If $p \ge m$, then $x^\alpha \in J$, so some $x^{\alpha(i)}$ divides $x^\alpha$ and $x^{\alpha(i)} y^m$ divides $x^\alpha y^p$. If $p < m$, then $x^\alpha \in J_p$ and some $x^{\beta_p(j)} y^p$ divides $x^\alpha y^p$. So each monomial of $I$ is divisible by an element of $L$. Each $f \in I$ is a combination of monomials of $I$, because each monomial of $\sum_\alpha h_\alpha x^\alpha$ is a multiple of some $x^\alpha$ and lies in $I$. So $I = \langle L \rangle$. Finally each element of $L$ lies in $I$, so it is divisible by $x^{\alpha}$ for some $\alpha \in A$. These finitely many $\alpha$ give generators from $A$. The minimal elements of $A$ are among them, because a minimal element $\alpha$ is divisible only by itself among the elements of $A$.

**Gröbner bases exist, and the field case again.** Let $I \ne \langle 0 \rangle$. By Dickson's lemma $\langle \operatorname{LT}(I) \rangle = \langle \operatorname{LM}(g_1), \ldots, \operatorname{LM}(g_t) \rangle$ for some nonzero $g_i \in I$. Let $f \in I$. [Division](/multivariate-division-algorithm/) by $g_1, \ldots, g_t$ gives $f = \sum q_i g_i + r$, where no term of $r$ is divisible by any $\operatorname{LT}(g_i)$. Then $r \in I$. If $r \ne 0$, then $\operatorname{LT}(r) \in \langle \operatorname{LT}(I) \rangle$ is divisible by some $\operatorname{LT}(g_i)$, which is false. So $r = 0$ and $I = \langle g_1, \ldots, g_t \rangle$. This gives the theorem over a field without the general argument. $\blacksquare$

## Consequences

- **Finite systems.** Each $V(S)$, for any set $S$ of polynomials, equals $V(g_1, \ldots, g_t)$ for finitely many polynomials of the ideal $\langle S \rangle$.
- **Termination of Buchberger's algorithm.** Each new basis element in [Buchberger's algorithm](/buchberger-algorithm/) makes the monomial ideal $\langle \operatorname{LT}(G) \rangle$ strictly larger. The ascending chain condition stops this after finitely many steps.
- **Well-ordering of monomial orders.** Dickson's lemma proves that a total order compatible with multiplication with $1$ smallest is a well-order. See [monomial ordering](/monomial-ordering/).
- **Primary decomposition.** Noetherian induction gives that each ideal is a finite intersection of irreducible ideals, hence of primary ones. See [primary decomposition](/primary-decomposition/).
- **Hilbert series.** Hilbert proved this theorem in the paper where he showed that the Hilbert function is a polynomial for large degrees. See [Hilbert series](/hilbert-series/).

## Tightness

- **The ring must be Noetherian.** Let $R = K[y_1, y_2, y_3, \ldots]$ in infinitely many variables. The ideal $\langle y_1, y_2, \ldots \rangle$ is not finitely generated: finitely many of its elements involve only $y_1, \ldots, y_m$ for some $m$, and every combination of them maps to $0$ under $y_1 = \cdots = y_m = 0$, but $y_{m+1}$ does not. The same chain shows that $R$ fails the ascending chain condition.
- **No bound on the number of generators.** In $K[x, y]$ the ideal $\langle x, y \rangle^d = \langle x^d, x^{d-1} y, \ldots, y^d \rangle$ needs $d + 1$ generators. Let $\mathfrak{m} = \langle x, y \rangle$ and $I = \mathfrak{m}^d$. If $I = \langle f_1, \ldots, f_r \rangle$, then each $\sum h_j f_j$ is congruent to $\sum h_j(0, 0) f_j$ modulo $\mathfrak{m} I$, so the images of the $f_j$ span the vector space $I / \mathfrak{m} I$. This space has the $d + 1$ monomials $x^{d-i} y^i$ as a basis, so $r \ge d + 1$. So the number of generators is not bounded by a function of the number of variables alone.
- **No bound on the degrees from the theorem.** The proof gives no bound on $t$ or on the degrees of the $g_i$. Such bounds come from other arguments: the degrees of a Gröbner basis can be doubly exponential in $n$ (Mayr and Meyer, 1982), see [Gröbner basis](/groebner-basis/).

## History

Hilbert proved the theorem in 1890, in his work on invariants of algebraic forms, with a proof that showed that a finite basis exists without a construction. Dickson proved his lemma in 1913, in a paper on perfect and abundant numbers. The proof of the field case through Dickson's lemma and leading terms, and the use of the lemma for the termination of Buchberger's algorithm, are in Cox, Little and O'Shea, Chapter 2.
