Buchberger's algorithm computes a [Gröbner basis](/groebner-basis/) of the ideal generated by finitely many polynomials over a field $K$, for a fixed [monomial order](/monomial-ordering/). It takes a pair of basis elements, forms their [S-polynomial](/s-polynomial/), and divides it by the current basis with the [multivariate division algorithm](/multivariate-division-algorithm/). A nonzero remainder joins the basis. When every pair leaves remainder zero, [Buchberger's criterion](/buchberger-criteria/) says that the set is a Gröbner basis. The algorithm always stops, but its worst case is doubly exponential in the number of variables, a limit that comes from the problem itself (Mayr and Meyer 1982).

## In plain words

You have a few polynomials in several variables, and you want a set of polynomials with the same common multiples that lets you decide membership by division. Start with the given polynomials. Take two of them and multiply each by the smallest factor that makes their top terms equal, then subtract, so the top terms cancel. Divide the result by all the polynomials you have. If something is left over, it is a polynomial of the ideal whose top term your set does not yet catch, so add it to the set, and pair it with all the others.

Repeat until every pair leaves nothing. Each addition catches a top term that was not caught before, and a chain of such growing sets cannot go on forever. So the method stops.

## The problem

Fix a field $K$, the ring $K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ and a monomial order $>$ (lex, grlex or grevlex in the examples). $\operatorname{LT}$, $\operatorname{LM}$ and $\operatorname{LC}$ are the leading term, monomial and coefficient. The input is a finite list $F = (f_1, \ldots, f_s)$ of polynomials. The output is a Gröbner basis $G$ of the ideal $I = \langle f_1, \ldots, f_s \rangle$ for $>$, with $F \setminus \{0\} \subseteq G$. This is the [Gröbner basis computation](/groebner-basis-computation/) problem. The [reduced Gröbner basis](/reduced-groebner-basis/) follows from $G$ by a final inter-reduction.

## The algorithm

For two nonzero polynomials, $x^{\gamma} = \operatorname{lcm}(\operatorname{LM}(g_i), \operatorname{LM}(g_j))$ and $S(g_i, g_j) = \frac{x^\gamma}{\operatorname{LT}(g_i)} g_i - \frac{x^\gamma}{\operatorname{LT}(g_j)} g_j$. The remainder $\overline{p}^{G}$ is the result of the full division of $p$ by the list $G$.

```pseudo id=main
BUCHBERGER(F, >)                            ▷ F = (f_1, ..., f_s) in K[x_1, ..., x_n]
L1  G ← (g_1, ..., g_t), the nonzero elements of F
L2  B ← {(i, j) : 1 ≤ i < j ≤ t}             ▷ the pairs still to check
L3  while B ≠ ∅ do
L4      remove from B the pair (i, j) whose lcm of LM(g_i), LM(g_j) is smallest
L5      if LM(g_i) and LM(g_j) are coprime then continue      ▷ product criterion
L6      h ← the remainder of S(g_i, g_j) on division by G
L7      if h ≠ 0 then
L8          t ← t + 1, g_t ← h
L9          B ← B ∪ {(u, t) : 1 ≤ u < t}
L10 return G
```

Line L4 is the *normal selection strategy*: the pair with the smallest lcm in the order goes first, with ties broken by the indices. Any choice gives a correct result; this one is usually faster, because a small lcm gives a small S-polynomial. Line L5 is [Buchberger's first criterion](/buchberger-criteria/): such a pair always reduces to zero, so it costs nothing to skip. Line L6 is where almost all of the time goes. The new element $h$ has a leading monomial that no $\operatorname{LM}(g_u)$ divides, because $h$ is a remainder.

## Example

Take $\mathbb{Q}[x, y]$, grlex with $x > y$, $g_1 = x^3 - 2xy$ and $g_2 = x^2 y - 2y^2 + x$ (Cox, Little and O'Shea, Chapter 2, Section 7). Each row is one round of the loop.

| pair | lcm | $S(g_i, g_j)$ | remainder | action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (1, 2) | $x^3 y$ | $-x^2$ | $-x^2$ | add $g_3 = -x^2$ |
| (2, 3) | $x^2 y$ | $-2y^2 + x$ | $-2y^2 + x$ | add $g_4 = -2y^2 + x$ |
| (1, 3) | $x^3$ | $-2xy$ | $-2xy$ | add $g_5 = -2xy$ |
| (4, 5) | $xy^2$ | $-\tfrac12 x^2$ | $0$ | none |
| (2, 5) | $x^2 y$ | $-2y^2 + x$ | $0$ | none |
| (3, 5) | $x^2 y$ | $0$ | $0$ | none |
| (2, 4) | $x^2 y^2$ | $\tfrac12 x^3 - 2y^3 + xy$ | $0$ | none |
| (3, 4) | $x^2 y^2$ | | | skipped by L5 |
| (1, 5) | $x^3 y$ | $-2xy^2$ | $0$ | none |
| (1, 4) | $x^3 y^2$ | | | skipped by L5 |

The output is $G = \{x^3 - 2xy,\ x^2 y - 2y^2 + x,\ -x^2,\ -2y^2 + x,\ -2xy\}$. Ten pairs were created: three gave new elements, five reduced to zero, and two were skipped. The leading monomial ideal grew from $\langle x^3, x^2 y \rangle$ to $\langle x^2, xy, y^2 \rangle$. Inter-reduction gives the reduced basis $\{x^2,\ xy,\ y^2 - \tfrac12 x\}$.

## Correctness

Two invariants hold at line L3. (I1) Each $g_u$ lies in $I$, and $G$ generates $I$. (I2) For each pair $(i, j)$ with $i < j \le t$ that is not in $B$, the polynomial $S(g_i, g_j)$ has an lcm-representation by $G$: it is $\sum_l A_l g_l$ with every $\operatorname{LM}(A_l g_l)$ less than the lcm. A remainder of zero gives such a representation; a nonzero remainder $h$ gives one as soon as $h$ joins $G$, because $\operatorname{LM}(h) \le \operatorname{LM}(S(g_i, g_j))$; and line L5 is covered by the product criterion. When $B$ is empty, (I2) covers every pair, and [Buchberger's criterion](/buchberger-criteria/) says that $G$ is a Gröbner basis.

Termination: each new $h$ makes the monomial ideal $J = \langle \operatorname{LM}(g_1), \ldots, \operatorname{LM}(g_t) \rangle$ strictly larger, because no $\operatorname{LM}(g_u)$ divides $\operatorname{LM}(h)$. By Dickson's lemma a strictly increasing chain of monomial ideals is finite, so line L8 runs finitely often. Between two runs of line L8, each round removes one pair from the finite set $B$. The full proof is below.

## Complexity

The cost model is the number of operations in $K$ (the [Blum-Shub-Smale machine](/blum-shub-smale-machine/) over $K$): each addition, multiplication, division and zero test costs 1. Over $\mathbb{Q}$ the bit cost is also governed by the growth of the coefficients, which this model does not see.

- **No useful upper bound is proved for this form of the algorithm.** The termination proof uses Dickson's lemma, which gives no bound. Bounds exist for the problem: the reduced Gröbner basis of an ideal generated in degree at most $d$ has elements of degree at most $2(d^2/2 + d)^{2^{n-1}}$ (Dubé 1990), and a Gröbner basis can then be found by linear algebra in a number of operations that is doubly exponential in $n$. See [Gröbner basis computation](/groebner-basis-computation/).
- **Lower bound (proved).** Mayr and Meyer (1982) built ideals in $n$ variables with generators of degree at most $d$ for which membership needs cofactors of degree $d^{2^{\Omega(n)}}$. From the same family one gets ideals whose reduced Gröbner bases contain elements of degree $d^{2^{\Omega(n)}}$ (von zur Gathen and Gerhard, Section 21.7), so any algorithm that writes the basis needs that much time and space. The front matter states this bound with an unnamed constant $c > 0$.
- **Better cases (proved).** When the ideal has finitely many common zeros, or when the input is a regular sequence of homogeneous polynomials, the degrees in a grevlex basis are bounded by a single exponential in $n$, and so is the cost of the linear-algebra methods such as [F4](/f4-algorithm/) and [F5](/f5-algorithm/). The exact statements are on the problem page.
- **Observed in practice.** The cost depends strongly on the order. A grevlex basis is usually much cheaper than a lex basis of the same ideal. Most of the time goes into reductions to zero, which is why the criteria and the selection strategy matter. For lex, a common route is grevlex first and then [FGLM](/fglm-algorithm/) or the [Gröbner walk](/groebner-walk/).

## Properties

- **Deterministic.** With the normal strategy and fixed tie-breaking, the output is a fixed list. Other strategies give other Gröbner bases, but the same reduced basis.
- **Not minimal.** The output keeps all inputs and all additions. In the example, $g_1$ and $g_2$ are redundant, since $x^2$ divides both leading monomials.
- **Field operations only.** The algorithm needs exact division by leading coefficients. Over $\mathbb{Z}$ it is run over $\mathbb{Q}$ or modulo primes.
- **Generalizes two classical algorithms.** For one variable it computes the gcd like the [Euclidean algorithm](/polynomial-euclidean-algorithm/). For linear polynomials it is [Gaussian elimination](/gaussian-elimination/).

## Variants and improvements

- **Chain criterion and the Gebauer-Möller installation.** Skip a pair $(i, j)$ when some $\operatorname{LM}(g_k)$ divides the lcm and both pairs with $k$ were treated. The [Gebauer-Möller criteria](/gebauer-moller-criteria/) apply this rule in a safe order.
- **Sugar strategy.** Select pairs by a phantom degree that the homogenized computation would have; see [sugar strategy](/sugar-strategy/). It works better than the normal strategy for orders that are not degree compatible.
- **Inter-reduction.** Remove $g_u$ when the leading monomial of a newer element divides $\operatorname{LM}(g_u)$, and reduce the tails. This keeps the basis small during the run.
- **F4.** Reduce many S-polynomials at once as rows of a sparse matrix ([F4 algorithm](/f4-algorithm/)).
- **Signatures.** Skip all reductions to zero for regular sequences ([F5 algorithm](/f5-algorithm/)).
- **Modular methods.** Over $\mathbb{Q}$, compute modulo several primes and lift the coefficients by Chinese remaindering and rational reconstruction ([modular methods](/modular-methods/)). This avoids the growth of the intermediate coefficients.

## When to use it

Use the plain algorithm for small systems, for teaching, and as a reference against which to test faster code. Use it with the criteria and the sugar strategy for moderate systems. For large systems over finite fields or $\mathbb{Q}$, use [F4](/f4-algorithm/) or a signature algorithm, which do the same mathematics with linear algebra. If you only need the solutions of a zero-dimensional system, compute a grevlex basis and convert it with [FGLM](/fglm-algorithm/).

## Pitfalls

- **Testing only the input pairs.** Line L9 must add the pairs of the new element with all earlier ones. A version that reduces only the pairs of $F$ returns $\{f_1, f_2, -x^2\}$ in the example, which is not a Gröbner basis: $S(g_2, g_3) = -2y^2 + x$ does not reduce to zero.
- **Partial reduction.** Line L6 may stop as soon as the leading term cannot be reduced, since only $\operatorname{LM}(h)$ matters for correctness. But then the basis is larger and the final inter-reduction must do the rest. Mixing the two in a test leads to false failures.
- **Removing pairs in a circle.** The chain criterion is safe only in an order. Removing $(i, j)$ because of $k$, $(i, k)$ because of $j$ and $(j, k)$ because of $i$ is wrong; [Buchberger's criteria](/buchberger-criteria/) gives an example.
- **Float coefficients.** With floating point numbers, a remainder that should be zero becomes small, and the test of line L7 adds noise to the basis. Use exact arithmetic: rationals or $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$.
- **Order of variables.** Lex with $x > y$ and lex with $y > x$ are different orders and give different bases. State the order of the variables with the order.

## History

Bruno Buchberger gave the algorithm and the S-polynomial criterion in his 1965 PhD thesis at the University of Innsbruck, written under Wolfgang Gröbner. He published the two criteria for skipping pairs in 1979. Mayr and Meyer proved in 1982 that ideal membership over $\mathbb{Q}$ is hard for exponential space, which explains the doubly exponential worst case of every Gröbner basis method.
