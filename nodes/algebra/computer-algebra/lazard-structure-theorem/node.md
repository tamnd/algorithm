## Statement

Notation: $K$ is a field, $R = K[x]$, and $I \subseteq K[x, y]$ is an ideal. The monomial order is lex with $y > x$, so the leading monomial of a polynomial is determined first by its degree in $y$ and then by the degree in $x$ of its leading coefficient in $y$. Write $\operatorname{lc}_y(f) \in R$ for the coefficient of the highest power of $y$ in $f$, and $\operatorname{LT}$, $\operatorname{LM}$, $\operatorname{LC}$ for the lex leading term, monomial and coefficient.

**Theorem (Lazard 1985).** Suppose $I \cap K[x] \ne \{0\}$, which holds for every zero-dimensional ideal, and let $f_0, f_1, \ldots, f_k$ be the [reduced lex Gröbner basis](/reduced-groebner-basis/) of $I$, sorted so that $d_i = \deg_y f_i$ satisfies $0 = d_0 < d_1 < \cdots < d_k$. Let $a_i = \operatorname{lc}_y(f_i) \in K[x]$, so that $f_0 = a_0$. Then:

1. $a_k \mid a_{k-1} \mid \cdots \mid a_1 \mid a_0$, and the degrees $\deg a_i$ strictly decrease.
2. **Divisibility.** $a_i$ divides $f_i$: $f_i = a_i H_i$ with $H_i \in K[x][y]$ monic in $y$ of degree $d_i$, and $H_0 = 1$.
3. **Nesting.** For $0 \le i < k$, $H_{i+1}$ lies in the ideal generated by $f_0 / a_i, f_1 / a_i, \ldots, f_i / a_i$, that is, by $\frac{a_j}{a_i} H_j$ for $j \le i$.

Put $G_i = a_{i-1} / a_i$ for $1 \le i \le k$ and $G_{k+1} = a_k$. Then $f_0 = G_1 G_2 \cdots G_{k+1}$ and $f_i = G_{i+1} \cdots G_{k+1} \, H_i$, the form in which Lazard states the theorem. If $I$ is zero-dimensional, then $G_{k+1} = a_k = 1$.

**Corollary (triangular description of the variety).** If $I$ is zero-dimensional and $K$ is algebraically closed, then

$$V(I) = \bigcup_{i=1}^{k} \{ (\alpha, \beta) : G_i(\alpha) = 0, \; a_i(\alpha) \ne 0, \; H_i(\alpha, \beta) = 0 \}.$$

So the points of $V(I)$ split into $k$ groups. In group $i$, the $x$-coordinates are roots of $G_i$, and over each one the $y$-coordinates are the roots of the single polynomial $H_i(\alpha, y)$. Each pair $(G_i, H_i)$ is a triangular set.

## In plain words

A lex Gröbner basis of a system in two unknowns looks like a staircase. The first polynomial involves only $x$, the next has a low power of $y$, and so on up to one that is monic in $y$. Lazard's theorem says each step of the staircase has a clean shape. Its polynomial is a polynomial in $x$ alone times a polynomial monic in $y$, and the $x$-factors divide each other in a chain. Reading the chain from the top tells you which $x$-values belong to which step. Over each such $x$-value, one polynomial in $y$ gives all the matching $y$-values. So the basis splits into simple triangular pieces, each of which you solve by one univariate root finding in $x$ and one in $y$.

## Proof

For $d \ge 0$ let $J_d = \{ \operatorname{lc}_y(f) : f \in I, \deg_y f = d \} \cup \{0\}$. It is an ideal of $R$: the sum of two elements of $I$ of $y$-degree $d$ either has $y$-degree $d$ with the sum of the leading coefficients, or the leading coefficients cancel, and multiplying by $r \in R$ multiplies the leading coefficient by $r$. Multiplying by $y$ shows $J_d \subseteq J_{d+1}$. Since $R$ is a principal ideal domain, $J_d = \langle b_d \rangle$ with $b_d$ monic or $0$, and $b_{d+1} \mid b_d$. By hypothesis $J_0 = I \cap K[x] \ne \{0\}$.

**Leading terms.** Under lex with $y > x$, $\operatorname{LM}(f) = y^{\deg_y f} x^{\deg \operatorname{lc}_y(f)}$. So $y^d x^e \in \langle \operatorname{LT}(I) \rangle$ exactly when some $f \in I$ has $\deg_y f = d' \le d$ and $\deg \operatorname{lc}_y(f) \le e$. Since $J_{d'} \subseteq J_d$, this means $e \ge \deg b_d$. The minimal generators of $\langle \operatorname{LT}(I) \rangle$ are therefore the monomials $y^d x^{\deg b_d}$ at the values of $d$ where $b_d$ changes, $\deg b_d < \deg b_{d-1}$, together with $d = 0$. These are $d_0 = 0 < d_1 < \cdots < d_k$. The reduced basis has one element $f_i$ for each, with $\operatorname{LM}(f_i) = y^{d_i} x^{\deg b_{d_i}}$ and $\operatorname{LC}(f_i) = 1$. Its leading coefficient $a_i = \operatorname{lc}_y(f_i)$ lies in $J_{d_i}$, is monic and has degree $\deg b_{d_i}$, so $a_i = b_{d_i}$. Part 1 follows from $b_{d+1} \mid b_d$ and the choice of the $d_i$.

**Part 2, by induction on $i$.** For $i = 0$, $f_0 = a_0$ and $H_0 = 1$. Let $i \ge 1$ and assume $a_j \mid f_j$ for all $j < i$. Write $f_i = a_i y^{d_i} + r$ with $\deg_y r < d_i$, and let $c = a_{i-1}/a_i \in R$. The polynomial

$$g = c f_i - y^{d_i - d_{i-1}} f_{i-1} = c\, r - y^{d_i - d_{i-1}} \bigl( f_{i-1} - a_{i-1} y^{d_{i-1}} \bigr)$$

lies in $I$ and has $y$-degree less than $d_i$. By induction every coefficient of $f_{i-1}$ is divisible by $a_{i-1} = c\, a_i$, so $g \equiv c\, r$ modulo $c\, a_i K[x, y]$.

Every element $h \in I$ with $\deg_y h < d_i$ lies in $c\, a_i K[x, y]$. Indeed, the [division algorithm](/multivariate-division-algorithm/) by the Gröbner basis gives $h = \sum_j q_j f_j$ with $\operatorname{LM}(q_j f_j) \le \operatorname{LM}(h)$. So only $f_j$ with $d_j \le \deg_y h < d_i$, that is $j < i$, occur. Each such $f_j$ is divisible by $a_j$, and $a_{i-1} \mid a_j$ for $j \le i - 1$.

Apply this to $g$: $c\, a_i$ divides $g$, hence divides $c\, r$, so $a_i \mid r$ and $a_i \mid f_i$. The quotient $H_i = f_i / a_i$ has leading coefficient $1$ in $y$ and degree $d_i$.

**Part 3.** Let $\delta = d_{i+1} - d_i$ and $c' = a_i / a_{i+1}$. Then

$$c' f_{i+1} - y^{\delta} f_i = a_i \bigl( H_{i+1} - y^{\delta} H_i \bigr)$$

lies in $I$ and has $y$-degree less than $d_{i+1}$. As in Part 2, division by the basis writes it as $\sum_{j \le i} q_j f_j = \sum_{j \le i} q_j a_j H_j$. Divide by $a_i$, which divides each $a_j$ with $j \le i$:

$$H_{i+1} = y^{\delta} H_i + \sum_{j \le i} q_j \frac{a_j}{a_i} H_j,$$

which lies in the ideal generated by the $\frac{a_j}{a_i} H_j = f_j / a_i$, $j \le i$.

**The factored form and the zero-dimensional case.** The product $G_1 \cdots G_{k+1}$ telescopes to $a_0 = f_0$, and $G_{i+1} \cdots G_{k+1} = a_i$. If $I$ is zero-dimensional, some power of $y$ lies in $\langle \operatorname{LT}(I) \rangle$, so $\deg b_d = 0$ for large $d$, hence $a_k = 1$.

**The corollary.** Let $(\alpha, \beta) \in \overline{K}^2$ with $f_0(\alpha) = 0$. Since $a_k = 1$, there is a least $i \ge 1$ with $a_i(\alpha) \ne 0$, and then $a_{i-1}(\alpha) = 0$ and $G_i(\alpha) = 0$. For $j < i$, $a_{i-1} \mid a_j$, so $f_j(\alpha, y) = 0$. For $j = i$, $f_i(\alpha, y) = a_i(\alpha) H_i(\alpha, y)$ with $a_i(\alpha) \ne 0$. For $j > i$, Part 3 shows by induction that $H_j(\alpha, y)$ is a multiple of $H_i(\alpha, y)$: in the ideal generated by $\frac{a_l}{a_{j-1}}(\alpha) H_l(\alpha, y)$, $l \le j - 1$, the terms with $l < i$ vanish, because $\frac{a_l}{a_{j-1}}$ is divisible by $G_i$, and the terms with $i \le l \le j - 1$ are multiples of $H_i(\alpha, y)$ by induction. So $V(I) \cap \{x = \alpha\} = \{(\alpha, \beta) : H_i(\alpha, \beta) = 0\}$. Conversely, a point with $G_i(\alpha) = 0 \ne a_i(\alpha)$ and $H_i(\alpha, \beta) = 0$ has $a_{i-1}(\alpha) = 0$, so the least index with $a(\alpha) \ne 0$ is $i$, and the same computation puts it in $V(I)$. $\blacksquare$

## Consequences

**A worked example.** Let $I$ be the ideal of the three points $(0, 0)$, $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 2)$ in $\mathbb{Q}^2$, as $(x, y)$. Its reduced lex basis with $y > x$ is

$$f_0 = x^2 - x, \qquad f_1 = x y - 2x = x\,(y - 2), \qquad f_2 = y^2 - y - 2x.$$

Here $a_0 = x^2 - x$, $a_1 = x$, $a_2 = 1$, so $G_1 = x - 1$, $G_2 = x$, $H_1 = y - 2$ and $H_2 = y^2 - y - 2x$. The triangular pieces are $\{x - 1, y - 2\}$, giving $(1, 2)$, and $\{x, y^2 - y\}$, giving $(0, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$. Part 3 says $H_2 \in \langle x - 1, y - 2 \rangle$, and indeed $H_2(1, 2) = 4 - 2 - 2 = 0$.

**The shape lemma.** If $k = 1$, the basis is $\{G_1(x), H_1(x, y)\}$ with $H_1$ monic in $y$. When $I$ is radical and the points have distinct $x$-coordinates, $H_1 = y - g(x)$, the shape position used by [polynomial system solving](/polynomial-system-solving/) and the [rational univariate representation](/rational-univariate-representation/).

**Triangular decomposition.** The corollary is the two-variable case of Lazard's lextriangular algorithm (1992). It splits a zero-dimensional lex basis in $n$ variables into triangular sets by gcd computations along the factors $G_i$, without factoring. See [triangular decomposition](/triangular-decomposition/) and [regular chains](/regular-chain/).

**Primary decomposition in two variables.** Lazard (1985) uses the structure to compute [primary decompositions](/primary-decomposition/) of ideals in $K[x, y]$ with gcds and univariate factorizations only.

**Specialization in $n$ variables.** Gianni and Kalkbrener showed that for a lex Gröbner basis of a zero-dimensional ideal in $K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, specializing the last variables at a partial solution and taking the first element that does not vanish gives a generator of the specialized ideal. This is the $n$-variable form of the step from the corollary.

## Tightness

- **The hypothesis $I \cap K[x] \ne \{0\}$ is needed for Part 2.** For $I = \langle xy - 1 \rangle$, the reduced lex basis is $\{xy - 1\}$ with leading coefficient $a = x$, and $x \nmid xy - 1$.
- **The $H_i$ need not be irreducible or square-free,** and the $G_i$ need not be square-free. For $I = \langle x^2, y^2 \rangle$, the basis is $\{x^2, y^2\}$ with $G_1 = x^2$ and $H_1 = y^2$. The theorem describes structure, not primality.
- **Positive-dimensional ideals.** If $a_k \ne 1$, the variety contains the lines $x = \alpha$ for the roots of $a_k$, and the corollary describes only the remaining points.

## History

Lazard proved the theorem in 1985 to compute primary decompositions in two variables. Gianni and Kalkbrener (1987 to 1989) found the specialization property of lex bases in $n$ variables. Lazard (1992) built from it the lextriangular decomposition of zero-dimensional systems, a forerunner of the theory of [regular chains](/regular-chain/). Later authors, including Marinari and Mora, extended the structural description to more variables.
