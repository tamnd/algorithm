A monomial ordering is a total order on the [monomials](/monomial/) of $K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ that respects multiplication and has no infinite descending chain. It fixes which term of a polynomial is the [leading term](/leading-term/), and so it fixes how [multivariate division](/multivariate-division-algorithm/) proceeds and what a [Gröbner basis](/groebner-basis/) is. The three standard orders are lex, grlex and grevlex. They give different Gröbner bases of the same ideal, of very different sizes, and each one is good for a different task.

## Definition

**Notation.** $K$ is a field, $x^\alpha = x_1^{\alpha_1} \cdots x_n^{\alpha_n}$ for $\alpha \in \mathbb{N}^n$, and $|\alpha| = \alpha_1 + \cdots + \alpha_n$. We identify a monomial with its exponent vector, so an order on monomials is an order on $\mathbb{N}^n$. The order of the variables is part of the data: we always take $x_1 > x_2 > \cdots > x_n$ unless we say otherwise.

**Definition.** A *monomial ordering* (or *term order*) is a relation $>$ on $\mathbb{N}^n$ such that:

1. $>$ is a [total order](/total-order/): for $\alpha \ne \beta$, exactly one of $\alpha > \beta$ and $\beta > \alpha$ holds, and $>$ is transitive.
2. $>$ is compatible with addition: if $\alpha > \beta$, then $\alpha + \gamma > \beta + \gamma$ for each $\gamma \in \mathbb{N}^n$. In monomials: $x^\alpha > x^\beta$ implies $x^\alpha x^\gamma > x^\beta x^\gamma$.
3. $>$ is a well-ordering: each nonempty subset of $\mathbb{N}^n$ has a smallest element.

Condition 3 is what makes division terminate: the leading monomial of the dividend drops at each step, and a well-order has no infinite descending chain.

**Lemma.** Let $>$ satisfy conditions 1 and 2. Then $>$ is a well-ordering exactly when $\alpha \ge 0$ for each $\alpha \in \mathbb{N}^n$, that is, when $1$ is the smallest monomial.

*Proof.* Assume that $>$ is a well-ordering, and that $0 > \gamma$ for some $\gamma$. Adding $\gamma$ again and again by condition 2 gives $0 > \gamma > 2\gamma > 3\gamma > \cdots$, an infinite descending chain whose set has no smallest element. This is a contradiction. Now assume that $\alpha \ge 0$ for each $\alpha$, and let $A \subseteq \mathbb{N}^n$ be nonempty. By Dickson's lemma (see the [Hilbert basis theorem](/hilbert-basis-theorem/)), the monomial ideal $\langle x^\alpha : \alpha \in A \rangle$ is generated by finitely many of its generators $x^{\alpha(1)}, \ldots, x^{\alpha(s)}$ with each $\alpha(i) \in A$. Let $\alpha(i)$ be the smallest of these finitely many vectors. Each $\beta \in A$ lies in the ideal, so $x^{\alpha(j)}$ divides $x^\beta$ for some $j$, that is, $\beta = \alpha(j) + \gamma$ with $\gamma \in \mathbb{N}^n$. Since $\gamma \ge 0$, condition 2 gives $\beta \ge \alpha(j) \ge \alpha(i)$. So $\alpha(i)$ is the smallest element of $A$. $\square$

**The three standard orders.** Let $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{N}^n$ with $\alpha \ne \beta$.

- *Lexicographic order (lex).* $\alpha >_{\mathrm{lex}} \beta$ when the leftmost nonzero entry of $\alpha - \beta$ is positive.
- *Graded lexicographic order (grlex).* $\alpha >_{\mathrm{grlex}} \beta$ when $|\alpha| > |\beta|$, or $|\alpha| = |\beta|$ and $\alpha >_{\mathrm{lex}} \beta$.
- *Graded reverse lexicographic order (grevlex).* $\alpha >_{\mathrm{grevlex}} \beta$ when $|\alpha| > |\beta|$, or $|\alpha| = |\beta|$ and the rightmost nonzero entry of $\alpha - \beta$ is negative.

*Each is a monomial ordering.* In each case the rule decides between $\alpha$ and $\beta$ from the entries of $\alpha - \beta$ and from $|\alpha| - |\beta| = |\alpha - \beta|$. So it is total, and it is transitive because the sum of two vectors whose leftmost nonzero entry is positive has the same property (and the same for the rightmost entry and for degrees). Adding $\gamma$ to both sides changes neither $\alpha - \beta$ nor $|\alpha| - |\beta|$, so condition 2 holds. For $\alpha \ne 0$: the leftmost nonzero entry of $\alpha - 0$ is positive, so $\alpha >_{\mathrm{lex}} 0$; and $|\alpha| > 0$, so $\alpha > 0$ for the two graded orders. The lemma then gives condition 3.

**Weight and matrix orders.** Let $M$ be a real $m \times n$ matrix whose map $\mathbb{Z}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is injective. Set $\alpha >_M \beta$ when $M\alpha >_{\mathrm{lex}} M\beta$. This relation satisfies conditions 1 and 2, and it is a monomial ordering exactly when the first nonzero entry of each column of $M$ is positive (by the lemma, this says $M e_i >_{\mathrm{lex}} 0$ for each unit vector $e_i$). Lex is $M = I_n$. For $n = 3$, grlex has rows $(1, 1, 1), (1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0)$ and grevlex has rows $(1, 1, 1), (1, 1, 0), (1, 0, 0)$. Robbiano (1985) proved that each monomial ordering arises from some real matrix in this way.

**Elimination orders.** An order is an *elimination order* for $x_1, \ldots, x_k$ when each monomial that contains one of $x_1, \ldots, x_k$ is larger than each monomial in $x_{k+1}, \ldots, x_n$ alone. Lex is an elimination order for each $k$. A *block order* compares the $x_1, \ldots, x_k$ part first, by any order, and breaks ties on the rest by any order. The [elimination theorem](/elimination-theorem/) uses such orders.

## Examples and non-examples

The monomials of $K[x, y, z]$ of total degree at most $2$, from largest to smallest, with $x > y > z$:

| order | the monomials in decreasing order |
|---|---|
| lex | $x^2 > xy > xz > x > y^2 > yz > y > z^2 > z > 1$ |
| grlex | $x^2 > xy > xz > y^2 > yz > z^2 > x > y > z > 1$ |
| grevlex | $x^2 > xy > y^2 > xz > yz > z^2 > x > y > z > 1$ |

Grlex and grevlex differ first at $xz$ against $y^2$. In degree $3$ they differ at $xyz$ against $y^3$: grlex puts $xyz$ first because it has more $x$, and grevlex puts $y^3$ first because it has less $z$.

Under lex, $x > y^{100}$, and there are infinitely many monomials between $x$ and $1$ (all the powers of $y$ and $z$). This is allowed: a well-ordering can have infinite intervals, but it has no infinite descending chain.

Non-examples:

- *Total degree alone.* "$\alpha > \beta$ when $|\alpha| > |\beta|$" is not total: $xy$ and $z^2$ are not comparable.
- *Pure reverse lex.* "$\alpha > \beta$ when the rightmost nonzero entry of $\alpha - \beta$ is negative", without the degree first, satisfies conditions 1 and 2 but not condition 3. In one variable it gives $1 > x > x^2 > x^3 > \cdots$, an infinite descending chain. It is a *local* order, used for computations in power series rings.
- *Degree reversed.* "$\alpha > \beta$ when $|\alpha| < |\beta|$, ties by lex" also makes $1$ the largest monomial, so it fails condition 3.
- *A weight that ignores a variable.* The weight $w = (1, 0)$ on $K[x, y]$ alone does not separate $y$ from $y^2$. It needs a tie-breaker to become a monomial ordering.

## Related definitions

- **Leading term.** $\operatorname{LT}(f)$, $\operatorname{LM}(f)$ and $\operatorname{LC}(f)$ for the chosen order. See [leading term](/leading-term/).
- **The choice of order in practice.** Lex bases solve systems by back substitution, but they are often far larger and slower to compute. Grevlex bases are usually the fastest to compute, an observation that Bayer and Stillman (1987) explain in part: for a homogeneous ideal in generic coordinates over a field of characteristic zero, the largest degree in the reduced grevlex basis equals the Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity of the ideal. A common plan is to compute a grevlex basis and convert it to lex with the [FGLM algorithm](/fglm-algorithm/) (for finitely many solutions) or the [Gröbner walk](/groebner-walk/). See [Gröbner basis conversion](/groebner-basis-conversion/).
- **Elimination ideal.** $I \cap K[x_{k+1}, \ldots, x_n]$, computed with an elimination order. See [elimination ideal](/elimination-ideal/).
- **Local orders.** Orders with $x_i < 1$, used for standard bases in local rings. They satisfy conditions 1 and 2 but not 3.
