Gröbner basis computation is the problem of finding a [Gröbner basis](/groebner-basis/) of the ideal generated by a given finite set of polynomials, for a given [monomial order](/monomial-ordering/). It is the central problem of computational commutative algebra: once a basis is known, [ideal membership](/ideal-membership-problem/), elimination, dimension and the solutions of [polynomial systems](/polynomial-system-solving/) follow by cheap steps. The problem is decidable by [Buchberger's algorithm](/buchberger-algorithm/), but in the worst case the output has doubly exponential degree in the number of variables (Mayr and Meyer 1982), so no algorithm can be fast on all inputs.

## In plain words

You are given a few polynomials in several variables, for example the equations of a geometry problem. Many other polynomials follow from them by adding multiples. You want a special set of polynomials, with the same consequences, that answers questions by plain division: is this polynomial a consequence, which consequences use only some variables, how many common solutions are there.

To compute that set, you combine pairs of polynomials so that their top terms cancel, divide by what you have, and keep what is left. On small inputs this is fast. On some inputs the answer itself is huge, and nothing can avoid that. On typical inputs a careful choice of the order of terms and of the method makes a large difference.

## Definition

**Input.** A field $K$ (in practice $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ or a finite extension of them), a number $n$ of variables, a [monomial order](/monomial-ordering/) $>$ on $K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ (lex, grlex, grevlex, a block order or a weight order), and polynomials $f_1, \ldots, f_s \in K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, each given as a list of terms.

**Output.** A finite set $G \subseteq I = \langle f_1, \ldots, f_s \rangle$ of nonzero polynomials with

$$\langle \operatorname{LT}(g) : g \in G \rangle = \langle \operatorname{LT}(f) : f \in I, f \ne 0 \rangle,$$

where $\operatorname{LT}$ is the leading term for $>$. The empty set is the answer for $I = \{0\}$. In the *reduced* form of the problem the output is the [reduced Gröbner basis](/reduced-groebner-basis/): each $\operatorname{LC}(g) = 1$, and no term of any $g$ is divisible by $\operatorname{LT}(g')$ for another $g' \in G$. This output is unique, so the reduced problem is a function problem with one answer.

**Size measures.** $n$ is the number of variables, $s$ the number of generators, $d$ the largest total degree of a generator, and $D$ the largest degree of an element of the output. Costs are counted in operations of $K$ unless stated otherwise. Over $\mathbb{Q}$ the bit cost also depends on the size of the coefficients.

## Variants

- **Decision: is $G$ a Gröbner basis?** By [Buchberger's criterion](/buchberger-criteria/) it is enough to divide $\binom{|G|}{2}$ S-polynomials by $G$. The divisions themselves can be long.
- **Is $I$ the unit ideal?** The reduced basis is $\{1\}$ exactly when $I = K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, which by the [Nullstellensatz](/hilbert-nullstellensatz/) means that the system has no solution over the algebraic closure of $K$.
- **Homogeneous input, truncated output.** For homogeneous generators, the elements of degree at most $D$ of a Gröbner basis depend only on the generators of degree at most $D$, and they can be computed degree by degree. This is the setting of the [Macaulay matrix](/macaulay-matrix/) methods.
- **Zero-dimensional ideals.** When the system has finitely many solutions, $K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]/I$ has finite dimension, and much better bounds hold (below).
- **Change of order.** Given a basis for one order, find one for another: [Gröbner basis conversion](/groebner-basis-conversion/).
- **Other rings.** Over $\mathbb{Z}$ or a Euclidean domain (strong Gröbner bases), for modules over $K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, and for some noncommutative algebras. For general noncommutative polynomial rings a finite basis need not exist, and the problem is not decidable, as the [word problem](/word-problem/) for finitely presented monoids shows.

## Examples

All examples are over $\mathbb{Q}$.

| generators | order | reduced Gröbner basis |
|---|---|---|
| $x^3 - 2xy,\ x^2 y - 2y^2 + x$ | grlex, $x > y$ | $x^2,\ xy,\ y^2 - \tfrac12 x$ |
| $x^3 - 2xy,\ x^2 y - 2y^2 + x$ | lex, $x > y$ | $x - 2y^2,\ y^3$ |
| $x + y + z,\ xy + yz + zx,\ xyz - 1$ | lex, $x > y > z$ | $x + y + z,\ y^2 + yz + z^2,\ z^3 - 1$ |
| $xy - 1,\ x$ | any | $1$ |
| $x^2 + y,\ y^2 + x$ | grlex, $x > y$ | $x^2 + y,\ y^2 + x$ |

The third row is the "cyclic 3" system. The lex basis is triangular: the last polynomial involves only $z$, the one before only $y$ and $z$. So the solutions are found one variable at a time: $z$ is a cube root of 1, $y$ solves $y^2 + yz + z^2 = 0$, and $x = -y - z$. The fourth row is the unit ideal: $1 = y \cdot x - (xy - 1)$. In the last row the input is already a Gröbner basis, because the leading monomials $x^2$ and $y^2$ are coprime.

## Known algorithms

| algorithm | idea | notes |
|---|---|---|
| [Buchberger's algorithm](/buchberger-algorithm/) | add nonzero remainders of S-polynomials | the base method, 1965 |
| Buchberger with the [Gebauer-Möller criteria](/gebauer-moller-criteria/) and the [sugar strategy](/sugar-strategy/) | skip useless pairs, choose pairs well | the standard form in most systems before 2000 |
| Linear algebra on [Macaulay matrices](/macaulay-matrix/) (Lazard 1983), [XL](/xl-algorithm/) | Gaussian elimination on all multiples up to a degree | gives the proved bounds |
| [F4](/f4-algorithm/) (Faugère 1999) | reduce many S-polynomials as one sparse matrix | the fastest method in practice for many inputs |
| [F5](/f5-algorithm/) and signature algorithms (Faugère 2002) | avoid reductions to zero with signatures | no reduction to zero on regular sequences |
| [FGLM](/fglm-algorithm/) and the [Gröbner walk](/groebner-walk/) | change of order | used to get lex bases |
| [Modular methods](/modular-methods/) | compute mod primes, then lift | controls coefficient growth over $\mathbb{Q}$ |

## Bounds

**Worst case, upper bound.** Dubé (1990) proved that each element of the reduced Gröbner basis of an ideal generated in degree at most $d$ in $n$ variables has degree at most

$$2 \left( \frac{d^2}{2} + d \right)^{2^{n-1}},$$

for any monomial order. For homogeneous generators, Gaussian elimination on the matrices of all products $x^\alpha f_i$ of each degree up to $D$ yields all elements of degree up to $D$ of a Gröbner basis (Lazard 1983). A general input is first homogenized with one more variable, and the result is dehomogenized. The matrices have at most $\binom{n + 1 + D}{n + 1}$ columns, so with Dubé's bound for $D$ the cost is $D^{O(n)}$ field operations, which is $d^{2^{O(n)}}$. Kühnle and Mayr (1996) showed that the reduced basis can be computed in exponential space.

**Worst case, lower bound.** Mayr and Meyer (1982) built ideals in $O(n)$ variables, generated by polynomials of degree at most $d$, in which some polynomial of low degree is a member but every representation $\sum q_i f_i$ needs cofactors of degree $d^{2^{\Omega(n)}}$. Their construction also shows that ideal membership over $\mathbb{Q}$ is hard for exponential space. From the same family one gets reduced Gröbner bases with elements of degree $d^{2^{\Omega(n)}}$ (von zur Gathen and Gerhard, Section 21.7). So the doubly exponential worst case is a property of the problem, not of a method.

**Better cases.**

- *One variable.* The reduced basis is the monic gcd of the generators. It costs $O(M(d) \log d)$ operations in $K$ per gcd with the [half-gcd](/half-gcd/) method, where $M(d)$ is the cost of multiplying two polynomials of degree $d$.
- *Linear generators.* The problem is [Gaussian elimination](/gaussian-elimination/): an echelon form of an $s \times (n + 1)$ matrix.
- *Zero-dimensional ideals.* When the system has finitely many solutions over the algebraic closure, a Gröbner basis can be computed in $d^{O(n)}$ field operations (Lakshman 1991). The number of solutions, counted with multiplicity, is at most $d^n$ by Bézout's theorem.
- *Regular sequences.* For a homogeneous regular sequence of $n$ polynomials of degrees $d_1, \ldots, d_n$ in $n$ variables, every element of the grevlex basis has degree at most the Macaulay bound $\sum_{i=1}^n (d_i - 1) + 1$ (Lazard 1983). Bardet, Faugère and Salvy (2015) bound the cost of F5 on regular sequences by a polynomial in $\binom{n + D}{n}$, where $D$ is the degree of regularity. For $n$ generic polynomials of degree $d$ this is single exponential in $n$.

**Observed in practice.** The cost depends on the order more than on anything else: grevlex is usually the cheapest, lex the most expensive. Most of the time of Buchberger-type methods goes into reductions that end in zero. Over $\mathbb{Q}$, the coefficients of intermediate polynomials grow much faster than those of the output, which is the reason for [modular methods](/modular-methods/).

## Reductions

- **Ideal membership reduces to it.** With a Gröbner basis, $f \in I$ exactly when the remainder of $f$ by the basis is zero ([ideal membership problem](/ideal-membership-problem/)). So the lower bound of Mayr and Meyer for membership carries over to every method that decides membership by a Gröbner basis.
- **It reduces to linear algebra.** For homogeneous input and a large enough degree $D$, the rows of a reduced echelon form of the Macaulay matrix in degree $D$ contain a Gröbner basis. This is the reduction behind F4, F5 and XL, and behind the upper bound above.
- **SAT reduces to the unit ideal test.** Encode a CNF formula over Boolean variables $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ by the polynomials $x_i^2 - x_i$ and one polynomial per clause that vanishes exactly at the assignments that satisfy the clause, for example $(1 - x_1) x_2$ for the clause $x_1 \lor \lnot x_2$. The formula is unsatisfiable exactly when the reduced Gröbner basis is $\{1\}$. So deciding whether $I$ is the unit ideal is [NP-hard](/np-hard/), even for zero-dimensional ideals over $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Q}$.

## Applications

- **Solving systems.** A lex basis of a zero-dimensional system is triangular, as in the cyclic 3 example. See [polynomial system solving](/polynomial-system-solving/) and the [elimination theorem](/elimination-theorem/).
- **Elimination and implicitization.** A basis for an elimination order gives the polynomials in a subset of the variables, for example the implicit equation of a curve given by a parametrization ([elimination ideal](/elimination-ideal/)).
- **Invariants of an ideal.** Dimension, degree and the [Hilbert series](/hilbert-series/) are read from the leading terms.
- **Algebraic cryptanalysis.** Many attacks write a cipher or a signature scheme as a polynomial system over a finite field and solve it with F4 or F5 ([cryptanalysis](/cryptanalysis/)).
- **Geometry and robotics.** Automatic proofs of geometry theorems and the inverse kinematics of robot arms (Cox, Little and O'Shea, Chapter 6).
