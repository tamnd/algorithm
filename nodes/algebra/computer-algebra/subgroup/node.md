A subgroup of a [group](/finite-group/) $G$ is a subset $H \subseteq G$ that is a group under the operation of $G$: it holds the identity, and it is closed under products and under inverses. The even integers form a subgroup of the integers under addition, and the [cyclic subgroup](/cyclic-group/) $\langle g \rangle$ of all powers of one element is a subgroup of every group that holds $g$. In a finite group the order of a subgroup divides the order of the group, by [Lagrange's theorem](/lagranges-theorem-group-theory/). In computer algebra a subgroup is almost always given by a finite list of generators, and the basic tasks are to find its order and to test membership.

## Definition

Let $(G, \cdot)$ be a group with identity $e$. A subset $H \subseteq G$ is a **subgroup** of $G$, written $H \le G$, when

1. $e \in H$,
2. $a, b \in H$ implies $ab \in H$ (closure under the operation),
3. $a \in H$ implies $a^{-1} \in H$ (closure under inverses).

Then $H$ with the restriction of $\cdot$ to $H \times H$ is a group: associativity holds in $H$ because it holds in $G$. The subgroups $\{e\}$ and $G$ are the **trivial** subgroup and the **improper** subgroup. A subgroup $H \ne G$ is a **proper** subgroup.

**One-step test.** A subset $H \subseteq G$ is a subgroup if and only if $H \ne \emptyset$ and $ab^{-1} \in H$ for all $a, b \in H$.

*Proof.* A subgroup is nonempty because it holds $e$, and it holds $ab^{-1}$ by conditions 3 and 2. For the converse, take any $a \in H$. Then $e = aa^{-1} \in H$. For $b \in H$, $b^{-1} = e b^{-1} \in H$. For $a, b \in H$, we now have $b^{-1} \in H$, so $ab = a (b^{-1})^{-1} \in H$. $\square$

**Finite test.** If $H$ is a finite nonempty subset of $G$ and $H$ is closed under the operation, then $H$ is a subgroup.

*Proof.* Take $a \in H$. The powers $a, a^2, a^3, \ldots$ all lie in $H$, which is finite, so $a^i = a^j$ for some $i < j$. Cancel $a^i$ to get $a^{j-i} = e$ with $j - i \ge 1$. So $e = a^{j-i} \in H$, and $a^{-1} = a^{j-i-1} \in H$ (for $j - i = 1$ this is $a^0 = e = a^{-1}$). Conditions 1 to 3 hold. $\square$

The finiteness is necessary: the natural numbers $\{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$ are closed under addition in $\mathbb{Z}$, but they are not a subgroup.

**Generated subgroup.** The intersection of any family of subgroups of $G$ is a subgroup, because each of the three conditions holds in each member of the family. For a subset $S \subseteq G$, the **subgroup generated by $S$** is

$$\langle S \rangle = \bigcap \{\, H : S \subseteq H \le G \,\},$$

the smallest subgroup that contains $S$. It equals the set of all finite products $s_1^{\varepsilon_1} s_2^{\varepsilon_2} \cdots s_k^{\varepsilon_k}$ with $k \ge 0$, $s_i \in S$ and $\varepsilon_i = \pm 1$ (the empty product is $e$). This set holds $S$, it passes the one-step test, and each subgroup that holds $S$ holds each such product. When $S = \{g\}$, $\langle S \rangle = \langle g \rangle = \{ g^k : k \in \mathbb{Z} \}$. In a finite group the inverses are not needed: $s^{-1} = s^{m-1}$ where $m$ is the [order](/multiplicative-order/) of $s$, so $\langle S \rangle$ is the set of products of elements of $S$.

## Examples and non-examples

- **Multiples in the integers.** For each $n \ge 0$, $n\mathbb{Z} = \{ nk : k \in \mathbb{Z} \}$ is a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, because $na - nb = n(a - b)$. These are all the subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}$: a nonzero subgroup $H$ has a smallest positive element $n$, and division with remainder shows $H = n\mathbb{Z}$.
- **The integers modulo 12.** The group $(\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}, +)$ has exactly 6 subgroups, one for each divisor $d$ of 12, of sizes 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12. The subgroup of size 4 is $\{0, 3, 6, 9\} = \langle 3 \rangle$.
- **The symmetric group $S_3$.** The 6 permutations of $\{1, 2, 3\}$ have 6 subgroups: the trivial one, three of size 2 (each generated by one transposition), the alternating group $A_3$ of size 3, and [$S_3$](/symmetric-group/) itself. No subgroup has size 4 or 5, as Lagrange's theorem requires.
- **Units modulo 15.** In the [multiplicative group modulo 15](/multiplicative-group-modulo-n/), $\{1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 11, 13, 14\}$, the powers of 2 are $1, 2, 4, 8$, and then $2^4 \equiv 1$. So $\langle 2 \rangle = \{1, 2, 4, 8\}$ is a subgroup of size 4.
- **Non-example: no inverses.** The natural numbers in $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ hold $0$ and are closed under addition, but $1$ has no inverse in the set.
- **Non-example: no identity.** The odd integers are not a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$: they do not hold $0$, and the sum of two odd integers is even.
- **Non-example: a union.** In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ the union $\{0, 2, 4\} \cup \{0, 3\} = \{0, 2, 3, 4\}$ of two subgroups is not closed: $2 + 3 = 5$. In general $H \cup K$ is a subgroup only when $H \subseteq K$ or $K \subseteq H$. If $h \in H \setminus K$ and $k \in K \setminus H$, then $hk \in H$ would give $k \in H$, and $hk \in K$ would give $h \in K$, so $hk \notin H \cup K$.
- **Non-example: a different operation.** The set $\{1, -1\}$ is a group under multiplication, but it is not a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, because the operation is not the one of the larger group.

## Related definitions

- **Cyclic subgroup and order.** $\langle g \rangle$ is the subgroup of the powers of $g$. In a finite group its size is the order of $g$, the least $m \ge 1$ with $g^m = e$. See [cyclic group](/cyclic-group/) and [multiplicative order](/multiplicative-order/).
- **Cosets and index.** For $H \le G$ the left cosets $aH$ partition $G$ into sets of size $|H|$. Their number is the index $[G : H]$, and $|G| = [G : H] \, |H|$ for finite $G$. This is [Lagrange's theorem](/lagranges-theorem-group-theory/).
- **Normal subgroup.** $H$ is normal when $aHa^{-1} = H$ for all $a \in G$. Then the cosets form the quotient group $G/H$. Every subgroup of an abelian group is normal.
- **Subgroups in computation.** A subgroup is given by generators. For a [permutation group](/symmetric-group/) the Schreier-Sims method computes the order and tests membership in time polynomial in the degree and the number of generators. Subgroups of automorphisms appear in [graph isomorphism](/graph-automorphism-group/), and small subgroups of the multiplicative group of a field are the target of the [small subgroup attack](/small-subgroup-attack/) in cryptography.
- **Related structures.** A subring and a subfield are defined in the same way, with closure under both operations. An ideal of a ring is an additive subgroup with an extra absorption rule; see [polynomial ideal](/polynomial-ideal/).
