A finite group is a group with finitely many elements: a finite set $G$ with an associative operation, an identity element, and an inverse for each element. The number of elements $|G|$ is its *order*. The groups $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ under addition, the units $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ under multiplication and the symmetric groups $S_n$ of permutations are the finite groups that algorithms meet most. Finiteness gives the facts that number-theoretic algorithms use: every element has a finite order that divides $|G|$, so $g^{|G|} = e$, which is the root of Fermat's and [Euler's theorem](/euler-theorem/), of primality tests, and of the [discrete logarithm problem](/discrete-logarithm-problem/).

## Definition

A **group** is a set $G$ with a map $G \times G \to G$, written $(a, b) \mapsto ab$, such that

1. *associativity*: $(ab)c = a(bc)$ for all $a, b, c \in G$;
2. *identity*: there is $e \in G$ with $ea = ae = a$ for all $a \in G$;
3. *inverses*: for each $a \in G$ there is $a^{-1} \in G$ with $a a^{-1} = a^{-1} a = e$.

So a group is a [monoid](/monoid/) in which every element is invertible. The identity and each inverse are unique. A group is **finite** if the set $G$ is finite; its **order** is $|G|$. It is **abelian** if $ab = ba$ for all $a, b$; abelian groups are often written additively, with $a + b$, $0$ and $-a$.

The **order of an element** $g$ is the least $k \ge 1$ with $g^k = e$. In a finite group it always exists, because the powers $g, g^2, g^3, \ldots$ lie in a finite set, so $g^i = g^j$ for some $i < j$, and then $g^{j-i} = e$.

**Three facts.**

1. *Element orders divide the group order.* The powers of $g$ form the [cyclic](/cyclic-group/) [subgroup](/subgroup/) $\langle g \rangle = \{e, g, \ldots, g^{k-1}\}$, with exactly $k = \operatorname{ord}(g)$ elements. By [Lagrange's theorem](/lagranges-theorem-group-theory/), $k$ divides $|G|$. Hence $g^{|G|} = e$ for every $g \in G$.
2. *Finite subgroup test.* A nonempty subset $H$ of a finite group that is closed under the operation is a subgroup. Proof: for $h \in H$ all powers of $h$ lie in $H$. With $k = \operatorname{ord}(h)$, $e = h^k \in H$ and $h^{-1} = h^{k-1} \in H$, where $h^0 = e$.
3. *Finite cancellative monoids are groups.* If $M$ is a finite monoid in which $ax = ay$ implies $x = y$, and $xa = ya$ implies $x = y$, then $M$ is a group. Proof: for fixed $a$ the map $x \mapsto ax$ is injective on the finite set $M$, so it is onto, and $ax = e$ for some $x$. In the same way $ya = e$ for some $y$. Then $y = y(ax) = (ya)x = x$, so $x$ is an inverse of $a$.

Fact 3 explains why $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is a group for a prime $p$: multiplication modulo $p$ cancels nonzero factors, since $p \mid a(x - y)$ and $p \nmid a$ imply $p \mid x - y$.

## Examples and non-examples

**Integers modulo $n$ under addition.** $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = \{0, 1, \ldots, n - 1\}$ with addition modulo $n$ is an abelian group of order $n$, with identity 0 and $-a = n - a$ for $a \ne 0$. It is cyclic, generated by 1.

**Units modulo $n$.** $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$, the residues $a$ with $\gcd(a, n) = 1$ under multiplication modulo $n$, is an abelian group of order $\varphi(n)$. Inverses are computed by the extended Euclidean algorithm, as a [modular inverse](/modular-inverse/). For $n = 7$ the group is cyclic: the powers of 3 are $3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1$, so 3 is a [primitive root](/primitive-root/) and has order $6 = |G|$. For $n = 8$ the group $\{1, 3, 5, 7\}$ is not cyclic: $3^2 \equiv 5^2 \equiv 7^2 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$, so every element other than 1 has order 2. With fact 1, $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$, which is Euler's theorem.

**Symmetric groups.** The [permutations](/permutation/) of $\{1, \ldots, n\}$ under composition form the [symmetric group](/symmetric-group/) $S_n$ of order $n!$. It is not abelian for $n \ge 3$: writing permutations as functions applied right to left, $(1\,2)(2\,3) = (1\,2\,3)$ but $(2\,3)(1\,2) = (1\,3\,2)$. Every finite group of order $m$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $S_m$ (Cayley's theorem), so permutation groups are a universal representation.

**Groups from geometry and fields.** The symmetries of a regular $n$-gon form the dihedral group of order $2n$. The invertible $k \times k$ matrices over a finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$ form $\mathrm{GL}_k(\mathbb{F}_q)$, of order $(q^k - 1)(q^k - q) \cdots (q^k - q^{k-1})$. The nonzero elements of a finite field form a cyclic group, and the points of an elliptic curve over a finite field form a finite abelian group; both serve as groups for cryptography.

**Non-examples.**

- $\mathbb{Z}$ under addition is a group but not finite.
- $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ under multiplication is a finite monoid, not a group: $0$, $2$, $3$ and $4$ have no inverse.
- $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ under multiplication modulo 6 is not even closed: $2 \cdot 3 \equiv 0$.
- The set of all maps from $\{1, 2\}$ to itself under composition is a finite monoid of 4 elements, but the two constant maps are not invertible.

### How computer algebra represents finite groups

A **Cayley table** lists all $|G|^2$ products and is usable only for small groups. A **permutation group** is given by a few generating permutations of $\{1, \ldots, m\}$; its order can be exponential in the input size, and the Schreier-Sims algorithm computes it, and tests membership, in time polynomial in $m$ and the number of generators (Holt, Eick and O'Brien, Chapter 4). **Matrix groups** over finite fields and **polycyclic presentations** of solvable groups are other standard forms. In a **black-box group** one can only multiply, invert and compare elements; generic algorithms such as [baby-step giant-step](/baby-step-giant-step/) and [Pohlig-Hellman](/pohlig-hellman/) for discrete logarithms work in this model.

## Related definitions

- [Monoid](/monoid/): drop the inverse axiom. A finite cancellative monoid is a group, by fact 3.
- [Subgroup](/subgroup/) and [Lagrange's theorem](/lagranges-theorem-group-theory/): the order of a subgroup divides the order of the group.
- [Cyclic group](/cyclic-group/): generated by one element; every group of prime order is cyclic, by fact 1.
- [Symmetric group](/symmetric-group/) and [permutation](/permutation/): the universal finite groups, by Cayley's theorem.
- [Euler's theorem](/euler-theorem/) and [primitive root](/primitive-root/): fact 1 applied to $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$.
- [Chinese remainder theorem](/chinese-remainder-theorem/): $(\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z})^\times \cong (\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^\times \times (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ for coprime $m, n$.
- [Discrete logarithm problem](/discrete-logarithm-problem/): given $g$ and $h = g^x$ in a finite cyclic group, find $x$.
