A syzygy of a list of polynomials $f_1, \ldots, f_s$ is a list of polynomial multipliers $(h_1, \ldots, h_s)$ with $h_1 f_1 + \cdots + h_s f_s = 0$. The syzygies form a module, and its generators describe all the ways in which the $f_i$ depend on each other. Gröbner basis algorithms are built on them: an [S-polynomial](/s-polynomial/) comes from a syzygy of two leading terms, the [Buchberger criteria](/buchberger-criteria/) are statements about which syzygies are redundant, and [F5](/f5-algorithm/) skips useless work by predicting syzygies before it meets them.

## In plain words

Take a few polynomials. Sometimes you can multiply each one by another polynomial so that the products add up to zero. Such a choice of multipliers is a syzygy. It is a dependency between the polynomials, like a dependency between the rows of a matrix, but the multipliers may be polynomials and not only numbers.

Every pair always has one cheap dependency: multiply the first by the second, and the second by the first, and subtract. The interesting question is which other dependencies exist. A Gröbner basis computation creates a new polynomial for each dependency between the leading terms. When a dependency is already a combination of others, the work it would cause is wasted, and the good algorithms use this to skip it.

## The idea

**Notation.** $R = K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ for a field $K$. $R^s$ is the free module of column vectors of length $s$ with standard basis $e_1, \ldots, e_s$. A monomial order on $R$ is fixed when leading terms appear (lex, grlex or grevlex); $\operatorname{LT}$, $\operatorname{LM}$ and $\operatorname{LC}$ are the leading term, monomial and coefficient.

**Definition.** For $F = (f_1, \ldots, f_s) \in R^s$, the syzygy module is

$$\operatorname{Syz}(F) = \{ (h_1, \ldots, h_s) \in R^s : h_1 f_1 + \cdots + h_s f_s = 0 \}.$$

It is the kernel of the $R$-linear map $R^s \to R$, $e_i \mapsto f_i$, so it is a submodule of $R^s$. By the Hilbert basis theorem for modules it is finitely generated. The same definition works for a list of vectors in $R^m$: then $\operatorname{Syz}$ is the kernel of the matrix with columns $f_i$, and over $K$ instead of $R$ it is the ordinary null space.

**Koszul syzygies.** For each pair $i < j$, $f_j e_i - f_i e_j$ is a syzygy, because $f_j f_i - f_i f_j = 0$. These are the trivial or Koszul syzygies.

**Two coprime polynomials.** If $f_1, f_2 \ne 0$ have no common factor, then $\operatorname{Syz}(f_1, f_2)$ is generated by the Koszul syzygy $(f_2, -f_1)$. *Proof.* If $h_1 f_1 = -h_2 f_2$, then $f_2$ divides $h_1 f_1$, and since $R$ is a unique factorization domain and $\gcd(f_1, f_2) = 1$, $f_2$ divides $h_1$. Write $h_1 = q f_2$. Then $q f_1 f_2 = -h_2 f_2$, so $h_2 = -q f_1$ and $(h_1, h_2) = q (f_2, -f_1)$. $\square$ With a common factor $d = \gcd(f_1, f_2)$ the generator is $(f_2 / d, \, -f_1 / d)$ by the same argument.

**Syzygies of terms.** For terms $m_i = c_i x^{\alpha_i}$, a syzygy $(h_1, \ldots, h_s)$ is *homogeneous of degree* $x^\delta$ when each $h_i$ is a constant times $x^{\delta - \alpha_i}$ (zero when $x^{\alpha_i} \nmid x^\delta$). For $i < j$ let $x^{\gamma_{ij}} = \operatorname{lcm}(x^{\alpha_i}, x^{\alpha_j})$ and

$$S_{ij} = \frac{x^{\gamma_{ij}}}{m_i} e_i - \frac{x^{\gamma_{ij}}}{m_j} e_j.$$

**Proposition.** The $S_{ij}$ generate $\operatorname{Syz}(m_1, \ldots, m_s)$. *Proof.* Each syzygy is the sum of its homogeneous parts, because multiplying $m_i$ by a monomial $x^\beta$ gives a single term $x^{\beta + \alpha_i}$, so the relation $\sum h_i m_i = 0$ splits by the monomial $x^\delta$ of the products. A homogeneous syzygy of degree $x^\delta$ is $\sum_i c'_i x^{\delta - \alpha_i} e_i$ with $\sum_i c'_i c_i = 0$, over the indices $i$ with $x^{\alpha_i} \mid x^\delta$. Let $i_1 < \cdots < i_k$ be these indices. The same summation by parts as in the cancellation lemma of [S-polynomial](/s-polynomial/) writes it as $\sum_{l < k} a_l \, x^{\delta - \gamma_{i_l i_{l+1}}} S_{i_l i_{l+1}}$ with constants $a_l$, because $x^{\gamma_{i_l i_{l+1}}}$ divides $x^\delta$. $\square$

An S-polynomial is what $S_{ij}$ gives when it is applied to the polynomials instead of their leading terms: $S(f_i, f_j) = \frac{x^{\gamma_{ij}}}{\operatorname{LT}(f_i)} f_i - \frac{x^{\gamma_{ij}}}{\operatorname{LT}(f_j)} f_j$. This is why Buchberger's criterion only looks at S-polynomials. The refined criterion of Cox, Little and O'Shea goes further: a set $G$ is a Gröbner basis when $S \cdot G$ has a standard representation for each $S$ in *some* homogeneous generating set of the syzygies of the leading terms. Any $S_{ij}$ that is a combination of others can be dropped, and this is the chain criterion.

**Schreyer's theorem.** Let $G = (g_1, \ldots, g_t)$ be a Gröbner basis. For each pair, division gives a standard representation $S(g_i, g_j) = \sum_k a_{ijk} g_k$. Then the vectors

$$s_{ij} = \frac{x^{\gamma_{ij}}}{\operatorname{LT}(g_i)} e_i - \frac{x^{\gamma_{ij}}}{\operatorname{LT}(g_j)} e_j - \sum_k a_{ijk} e_k$$

generate $\operatorname{Syz}(g_1, \ldots, g_t)$. So one Gröbner basis computation gives the syzygies of its output for free, and a second one on these vectors (with a module order) gives the next step of a free resolution. The proof is in Cox, Little and O'Shea, Using Algebraic Geometry, Chapter 5, Section 3.

**Hilbert's syzygy theorem.** Each finitely generated $R$-module $M$ has a free resolution $0 \to F_l \to \cdots \to F_1 \to F_0 \to M \to 0$ with $l \le n$, the number of variables. Taking syzygies of syzygies stops after at most $n$ steps. The graded version of this resolution gives the [Hilbert series](/hilbert-series/) of $M$.

## Examples

- **Three monomials.** For $m_1 = xy$, $m_2 = yz$, $m_3 = xz$, the S-syzygies are $S_{12} = (z, -x, 0)$, $S_{23} = (0, x, -y)$ and $S_{13} = (z, 0, -y)$. Each is a syzygy: $z \cdot xy - x \cdot yz = 0$, and so on. Here $S_{13} = S_{12} + S_{23}$, so $S_{13}$ is redundant. In Buchberger's algorithm on three polynomials with these leading monomials, the pair $(1, 3)$ can be skipped once the pairs $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 3)$ are done. This is the chain criterion on the lcm $xyz$.
- **A Koszul syzygy is all there is.** In $\mathbb{Q}[x, y]$, $f_1 = x^2 - y$ and $f_2 = xy - 1$ have no common factor, so $\operatorname{Syz}(f_1, f_2)$ is generated by $(xy - 1, \, -(x^2 - y))$. In bounded degree this can be seen by linear algebra: among multipliers $(h_1, h_2)$ of degree at most 2, the solutions of $h_1 f_1 + h_2 f_2 = 0$ form a space of dimension 1, spanned by the Koszul syzygy.
- **Schreyer on a lex basis.** For lex with $x > y$, $G = (g_1, g_2) = (x - y^2, \, y^3 - 1)$ is a Gröbner basis. The S-polynomial is $S(g_1, g_2) = y^3 g_1 - x g_2 = x - y^5$, and division gives $x - y^5 = 1 \cdot g_1 - y^2 \cdot g_2$. The Schreyer syzygy is $s_{12} = (y^3 - 1, \; -x + y^2)$, and indeed $(y^3 - 1)(x - y^2) + (y^2 - x)(y^3 - 1) = 0$. It equals the Koszul syzygy $(g_2, -g_1)$, as it must, because the leading monomials $x$ and $y^3$ are coprime.
- **Linear algebra.** Over $K$ instead of $R$, for the vectors $(1, 2)$, $(2, 4)$ and $(0, 1)$ in $K^2$, the syzygies are the null space of the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, spanned by $(2, -1, 0)$.

## Where it is used

- [Buchberger's algorithm](/buchberger-algorithm/) and the [Buchberger criteria](/buchberger-criteria/): each S-polynomial is a syzygy of leading terms applied to the basis. The product criterion says that the S-polynomial of coprime leading terms reduces to zero, which is the case where the syzygy lifts to a Koszul syzygy. The chain criterion drops S-syzygies that are combinations of others, and the [Gebauer-Möller criteria](/gebauer-moller-criteria/) organize this.
- [F5](/f5-algorithm/): it tracks a signature for each polynomial and discards a pair whose signature is the leading term of a Koszul syzygy. For a regular sequence these are all the syzygies, so F5 makes no reduction to zero.
- Free resolutions, Betti numbers and the [Hilbert series](/hilbert-series/) in commutative algebra.
- [Macaulay matrices](/macaulay-matrix/): the rows of a Macaulay matrix are products $m f_i$ of monomials and generators, so a linear dependency among its rows is a syzygy with multipliers of bounded degree. These dependencies are the rows that reduce to zero in the linear algebra of XL and F4.
- Elimination and implicitization: the syzygies of a parametrization give the moving lines and moving curves used to find implicit equations.
