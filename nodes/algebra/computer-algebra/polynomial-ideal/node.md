A polynomial ideal is a set $I$ of polynomials in $K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ that contains $0$, is closed under addition, and is closed under multiplication by any polynomial. Each ideal is the set of all combinations $h_1 f_1 + \cdots + h_s f_s$ of finitely many generators $f_i$, by the [Hilbert basis theorem](/hilbert-basis-theorem/). The ideal of a system of polynomial equations holds every equation that follows from the system by these operations, so it has the same solutions. Computer algebra works with ideals through a good generating set, the [Gröbner basis](/groebner-basis/).

## In plain words

Take a few polynomial equations that are all equal to zero. If you multiply one of them by any polynomial, the result is still zero at each common solution. If you add two of them, the result is also still zero there. The ideal is the set of all polynomials that you can make from the given ones with these two steps. It is a large, infinite set, but a short list of polynomials describes it.

Two different lists can make the same ideal. Then the two systems of equations have the same solutions. Many questions about a system are questions about its ideal: is a given polynomial a consequence of the system, are there any solutions at all, how many solutions are there. Computer algebra answers them with a special list of generators that makes these questions easy.

## The idea

**Notation.** $K$ is a field and $K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is the polynomial ring. Some statements hold over any commutative ring $R$ with $1$; we say so where it matters. $\bar K$ is an algebraic closure of $K$.

**Definition.** A subset $I \subseteq R$ of a commutative ring is an *ideal* when $0 \in I$, $f + g \in I$ for $f, g \in I$, and $hf \in I$ for $f \in I$ and $h \in R$. For $f_1, \ldots, f_s \in K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, the ideal they generate is

$$\langle f_1, \ldots, f_s \rangle = \Big\{ \sum_{i=1}^{s} h_i f_i : h_1, \ldots, h_s \in K[x_1, \ldots, x_n] \Big\}.$$

It is the smallest ideal that contains each $f_i$, and $\{f_1, \ldots, f_s\}$ is a *basis* of it. A basis is not unique, and the number of elements of a basis is not fixed either.

**Ideals and solutions.** The *variety* of $I$ is $V(I) = \{a \in \bar K^n : f(a) = 0 \text{ for each } f \in I\}$. If $I = \langle f_1, \ldots, f_s \rangle$, then $V(I)$ is the set of common zeros of $f_1, \ldots, f_s$: each $\sum h_i f_i$ vanishes where all $f_i$ vanish. So two bases of the same ideal define the same system of equations up to equivalence. In the other direction, for a set $V \subseteq \bar K^n$, $I(V) = \{f : f(a) = 0 \text{ for each } a \in V\}$ is an ideal. The [Nullstellensatz](/hilbert-nullstellensatz/) gives the exact relation: $I(V(I)) = \sqrt{I}$ when $K$ is algebraically closed.

**One variable against several.** $K[x]$ is a principal ideal domain: each ideal is $\langle g \rangle$ for one polynomial $g$, and $\langle f_1, \ldots, f_s \rangle = \langle \gcd(f_1, \ldots, f_s) \rangle$. So in one variable the [polynomial gcd](/polynomial-gcd/) solves the main questions: $f \in \langle g \rangle$ exactly when $g$ divides $f$. With two or more variables this fails. The ideal $\langle x, y \rangle \subseteq K[x, y]$ is not principal: if $\langle x, y \rangle = \langle h \rangle$, then $h$ divides $x$ and $y$, so $h$ is a nonzero constant and $1 \in \langle x, y \rangle$, but every element of $\langle x, y \rangle$ vanishes at $(0, 0)$ and $1$ does not. The tool for several variables is the [Gröbner basis](/groebner-basis/), with [multivariate division](/multivariate-division-algorithm/) in place of division by the gcd.

**Kinds of ideals.**

- *Radical:* $f^m \in I$ for some $m \ge 1$ implies $f \in I$. The radical $\sqrt{I} = \{f : f^m \in I \text{ for some } m\}$ is the smallest radical ideal that contains $I$.
- *Prime:* $fg \in I$ implies $f \in I$ or $g \in I$, and $I \ne \langle 1 \rangle$. Over $\bar K$, prime ideals correspond to irreducible varieties.
- *Maximal:* $I \ne \langle 1 \rangle$ and no ideal lies strictly between $I$ and $\langle 1 \rangle$. Over an algebraically closed field the maximal ideals are exactly $\langle x_1 - a_1, \ldots, x_n - a_n \rangle$.
- *Primary:* $fg \in I$ implies $f \in I$ or $g^m \in I$ for some $m$. Each ideal is a finite intersection of primary ideals. See [primary decomposition](/primary-decomposition/).
- *Zero-dimensional:* $V(I)$ is finite. This holds exactly when the quotient $K[x_1, \ldots, x_n] / I$ has finite dimension as a $K$-vector space.
- *Homogeneous:* generated by homogeneous polynomials. Its [Hilbert series](/hilbert-series/) counts the dimensions of the graded parts of the quotient.
- *The unit ideal:* $\langle 1 \rangle = K[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$. Then $V(I)$ is empty, and by the weak Nullstellensatz over $\bar K$ this is the only way to have no solutions.

**Operations.** For ideals $I = \langle f_1, \ldots, f_s \rangle$ and $J = \langle g_1, \ldots, g_r \rangle$:

| operation | definition | a basis, or how to compute one | zero set |
|---|---|---|---|
| sum | $I + J = \{f + g : f \in I, g \in J\}$ | $f_1, \ldots, f_s, g_1, \ldots, g_r$ | $V(I) \cap V(J)$ |
| product | $IJ$, generated by all $fg$ | all $f_i g_j$ | $V(I) \cup V(J)$ |
| intersection | $I \cap J$ | eliminate $t$ from $tI + (1 - t)J$ | $V(I) \cup V(J)$ |
| quotient | $I : J = \{h : hJ \subseteq I\}$ | intersections and divisions | contains the closure of $V(I) \setminus V(J)$, with equality when $I$ is radical |
| elimination | $I \cap K[x_{k+1}, \ldots, x_n]$ | a lex [Gröbner basis](/groebner-basis/) of $I$ | closure of the projection |

The rows on intersection, quotient and elimination need Gröbner bases. Cox, Little and O'Shea give each one in Chapter 3, Section 1 and Chapter 4, Sections 3 and 4. The geometric column for the quotient and the elimination holds over an algebraically closed field, with the Zariski closure.

## Examples

- $\langle x^4 - 1, x^6 - 1 \rangle = \langle x^2 - 1 \rangle$ in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$, because $\gcd(x^4 - 1, x^6 - 1) = x^2 - 1$. The common zeros are $1$ and $-1$.
- $\langle x + xy, y + xy, x^2, y^2 \rangle = \langle x, y \rangle$ in $\mathbb{Q}[x, y]$. The inclusion $\subseteq$ holds because each generator on the left is a multiple of $x$ or of $y$. For $\supseteq$: $x = (1 - y)(x + xy) + x \cdot y^2$, and the same with $x$ and $y$ swapped gives $y$. Four generators and two generators make the same ideal.
- $I = \langle x^2 + y^2 - 1, x - y \rangle \subseteq \mathbb{Q}[x, y]$ has the reduced lex Gröbner basis $\{x - y, y^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}\}$. So $V(I) = \{(a, a) : a^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\}$ has two points, and $\dim_{\mathbb{Q}} \mathbb{Q}[x, y]/I = 2$, with basis $1, y$.
- $\langle x^2, xy \rangle$ is not radical: $x \notin \langle x^2, xy \rangle$, but $x^2$ is in it. Its radical is $\langle x \rangle$. Its zero set is the line $x = 0$, the same as for $\langle x \rangle$. The extra point $(0, 0)$ in the primary decomposition $\langle x \rangle \cap \langle x^2, y \rangle$ is an embedded component.
- $\langle x \rangle \cap \langle y \rangle = \langle xy \rangle$, and $\langle x^2 \rangle \cap \langle xy \rangle = \langle x^2 y \rangle$. Both come from the elimination of $t$ in the table above.
- $\langle x^2 + 1 \rangle \subseteq \mathbb{R}[x]$ is a proper ideal whose zero set in $\mathbb{R}$ is empty. Over $\mathbb{C}$ it has the zeros $\pm i$. This is why $V(I)$ is taken over the algebraic closure.

## Where it is used

- **Ideal membership.** Decide whether a polynomial is a consequence of a system: the [ideal membership problem](/ideal-membership-problem/), solved by a Gröbner basis and division.
- **Solving systems.** [Polynomial system solving](/polynomial-system-solving/) works with the ideal, often through a lex basis, [triangular decomposition](/triangular-decomposition/) or the [rational univariate representation](/rational-univariate-representation/).
- **Elimination and implicitization.** The [elimination ideal](/elimination-ideal/) gives the equations of a projection, or the implicit equation of a parametric curve or surface.
- **Structure.** [Primary decomposition](/primary-decomposition/) splits an ideal into pieces, and the [Hilbert series](/hilbert-series/) gives its dimension and degree.
- **Linear algebra in disguise.** For a bounded degree, membership is a linear system in the coefficients of the $h_i$. The [Macaulay matrix](/macaulay-matrix/) is this system, and the [F4 algorithm](/f4-algorithm/) and [XL algorithm](/xl-algorithm/) work on it.
