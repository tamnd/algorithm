---
title: After the last change, reliable flooding gives every router the same map, and the Dijkstra next hops are loop-free shortest paths for all pairs
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

**Setting.** Routers are the vertices of an undirected graph $G = (V, E)$ with link costs $w(u, v) = w(v, u) > 0$. Links can lose messages, but a message that is sent again and again over a working link eventually arrives (fair loss). Each router $o$ numbers its LSAs with a counter that only grows, and it originates a new LSA, listing its working links and their costs, after every change of a local link. Sequence numbers are compared as integers, nobody forges an LSA, and no LSA ages out during the period considered (in OSPF the refresh time, 30 minutes, is far below MaxAge, one hour). Consider a time $T_0$ after which the topology does not change, and let $L_o$ be the last LSA that $o$ originates. Each router $y$ runs the handlers of the main pseudocode: install a received LSA and flood it to the other neighbours when it is newer than the stored copy (lines L4, L5, L7), acknowledge every LSA (line L3), and retransmit until acknowledged (line L8).

**Lemma 1 (no newer copy exists).** After $o$ originates $L_o$, every copy of an LSA of $o$ anywhere in the network has sequence number at most $\mathrm{seq}(L_o)$, because $o$'s counter only grows and copies are made only by forwarding. So once a router installs $L_o$, it never replaces it: line L5 needs a strictly newer number.

**Lemma 2 (every router in the component of $o$ installs $L_o$).** Let $C$ be the connected component of $o$ after $T_0$. By induction on the hop distance $k$ from $o$ in $C$. For $k = 0$, $o$ installs $L_o$ when it creates it (line L1). Let $z$ be at distance $k + 1$, with a neighbour $y$ at distance $k$ that installs $L_o$ at some time. When $y$ installs $L_o$, it sends $L_o$ to each neighbour except the one it came from (line L7), and it keeps resending it to $z$ until $z$ acknowledges it (line L8). If $L_o$ came to $y$ from $z$, then $z$ already had it. Otherwise, by fair loss, $z$ eventually receives $L_o$. On receipt, either $z$'s copy is older and $z$ installs $L_o$ (line L5), or $z$'s copy is not older, and by Lemma 1 it is $L_o$ itself. In both cases $z$ holds $L_o$, and by Lemma 1 it keeps it.

**Lemma 3 (the flood ends).** Each router installs $L_o$ at most once, so it floods $L_o$ at most once to each neighbour; each of these transmissions is retransmitted only until its acknowledgement arrives, which happens after finitely many tries by fair loss. Older LSAs of $o$ behave the same way, and only finitely many were originated. Line L6 sends a router's copy back only to a neighbour with an older one, and that neighbour then installs it, so it fires finitely often. Hence after some time $T_1 \ge T_0$ no LSA is in flight.

**Lemma 4 (the computed graph is the true component).** After $T_1$, let $y \in C$. By Lemma 2, $y$ holds $L_u$ for every $u \in C$. Line L10 puts a link $(u, v)$ into $y$'s graph only if both $u$ and $v$ list it. For $u, v \in C$, both latest LSAs list exactly the working links, so the link is in the graph if and only if it works, with its true cost. For $u \in C$ and $v \notin C$, the link does not work (otherwise $v$ would be in $C$), so $L_u$ does not list it, and it is left out even if a stale LSA of $v$ still lists it. Links between routers outside $C$ are not reachable from $y$ in the computed graph. So the part of $y$'s graph reachable from $y$ is exactly $C$ with its costs, the same at every router of $C$.

**Theorem.** After $T_1$, for every pair $s, t$ in one component, the distance that $s$ computes is the true distance $\delta(s, t)$, and following the next hops from $s$ reaches $t$ along a path of cost $\delta(s, t)$, without visiting a router twice.

*Proof.* By Lemma 4 and the correctness of [Dijkstra's algorithm](/dijkstra/) with positive costs, each router $x$ computes $\delta(x, \cdot)$ on $C$. Let $h = \mathrm{next}[x][t]$ for $x \ne t$. It is the first hop of a shortest path from $x$ to $t$ in $x$'s tree, so $\delta(x, t) = w(x, h) + \delta(h, t)$. Router $h$ uses its own tree, which may break ties in another way, but its next hop $h'$ again satisfies $\delta(h, t) = w(h, h') + \delta(h', t)$. So along the walk $x, h, h', \dots$ the value $\delta(\cdot, t)$ falls by at least the least link cost at each step. It cannot repeat a router, it must reach $t$ (the only router with value 0) after at most $n - 1$ steps, and the costs add up to $\delta(x, t)$. $\blacksquare$

**Remarks.** The theorem needs only that all routers compute exact distances on the same map; it does not need them to choose the same shortest paths. Before $T_1$, two routers can hold different maps, and then a packet can loop between a router with the old map and one with the new map; this lasts at most until the flood ends. If sequence numbers are compared circularly, Lemma 1 fails: the three numbers 8, 40 and 44 in a 6-bit space are each newer than another, no copy is final, and the flood need not end. This happened in the ARPANET on 27 October 1980.
