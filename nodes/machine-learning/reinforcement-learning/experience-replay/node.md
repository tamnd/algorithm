Experience replay stores the transitions $(s, a, r, s', \text{done})$ that an agent sees in a buffer, and trains the learner on minibatches drawn from the buffer instead of on the newest transition only. Lin (1992) introduced it for [Q-learning](/q-learning/) agents; the [deep Q-network](/dqn/) made it standard. The usual buffer is a [circular buffer](/circular-buffer/) of the last $N$ transitions with uniform sampling. Each transition is used many times, consecutive updates come from different parts of the experience, and since the stored data came from older policies, the learning rule must be off-policy.

## In plain words

An agent that learns only from what just happened throws each experience away after one look. It also sees long runs of almost identical situations, one after another, and a learner trained on such runs forgets what it learned in other places.

Experience replay keeps a memory of recent experiences. When the memory is full, the oldest experience is dropped to make room. To learn, the agent picks a handful of experiences at random from the memory and learns from them, as a student who shuffles a deck of flash cards instead of reading the notes in order. A rare and useful experience is then studied many times, and two experiences in one study batch usually come from different moments.

The memory holds what older versions of the agent did. So the agent must use a learning rule that can learn from someone else's choices.

## When it applies

The setting is an unknown MDP $(S, A, P, R, \gamma)$ with samples, under the discounted criterion with $\gamma < 1$. Experience replay helps when:

- the learner is a function approximator trained by [stochastic gradient descent](/stochastic-gradient-descent/), which assumes roughly independent samples, while the online stream of transitions is strongly correlated in time;
- samples are expensive (a robot, a simulator that is slow) compared with gradient steps, so reuse pays;
- the update rule is off-policy, so that a transition generated by an old policy still gives an unbiased target. One-step [Q-learning](/q-learning/) qualifies: its target $r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a')$ does not depend on the action the behavior policy would take in $s'$. [SARSA](/sarsa/) and on-policy policy gradient methods do not qualify without a correction such as [importance sampling](/importance-sampling-off-policy/);
- the environment is stationary, so that old transitions are still samples of the same $P$ and $R$.

## The template

The hole UPDATE is the learning rule, for example a Q-learning step or a gradient step on a minibatch loss. The buffer $D$ has capacity $N$; *next* is the slot of the next write.

```pseudo id=main
LEARN-WITH-REPLAY(N, B, UPDATE)
L1  D ← an empty buffer of capacity N, next ← 0
L2  for each step: act with the behavior policy and observe (s, a, r, s′, done)
L3      if |D| < N then append the transition else D[next] ← the transition
L4      next ← (next + 1) mod N
L5      when it is time to learn (for example every step, after a warm-up):
L6          draw B indices i_1, ..., i_B uniformly from 0 .. |D| − 1
L7          UPDATE with the transitions D[i_1], ..., D[i_B]
```

Lines L3 and L4 are the write of a [circular buffer](/circular-buffer/): once the buffer is full, slot *next* holds the oldest transition, so each write replaces the oldest one. Line L6 draws with replacement; drawing without replacement inside one minibatch is a common variant and changes little when $B \ll |D|$. The warm-up of line L5 fills the buffer with some transitions before the first update, so that early minibatches are not all copies of a few transitions.

Two counts describe a replay setup (Fedus et al., 2020): the *replay capacity* $N$, which bounds the age of the oldest transition, and the *replay ratio*, the number of transitions sampled for learning per transition collected. DQN uses $N = 10^6$, $B = 32$ and one update every 4 steps, so the replay ratio is 8.

**Example (the buffer).** Capacity $N = 4$, and the transitions are numbered 1 to 7 in the order they arrive. After the seventh write the slots hold $[5, 6, 7, 4]$ and $\textit{next} = 3$: transition 4 is the oldest and is the next to go. Read from slot *next* onward, the buffer holds $4, 5, 6, 7$, the last four transitions. A uniform draw picks each slot with probability $1/4$; over 60000 draws from a full buffer of 6 transitions, each count is Binomial$(60000, 1/6)$, with mean 10000 and standard deviation $\sqrt{60000 \cdot \tfrac16 \cdot \tfrac56} \approx 91.3$.

**Example (reuse makes the update off-policy).** On a chain of 5 states with actions left and right, where moving right from the last state ends the episode with reward 1 and $\gamma = 0.9$, a uniformly random behavior policy collects 3000 transitions into a buffer of capacity 500. Then 3000 minibatches of 32 Q-learning updates with step size $0.2$, all drawn from the buffer, give $Q(s, \text{right}) = 0.9^{4-s}$ and $Q(s, \text{left}) = 0.9^{5 - \max(s-1, 0)}$ to within $10^{-6}$. These are the optimal values $Q^*$, although no transition came from the greedy policy. The buffer held only the last 500 transitions, but these covered every state and action, which is what Q-learning needs here.

**Cost.** A write is $O(1)$, a uniform minibatch is $O(B)$ index draws, and the memory is $N$ transitions. With images, memory dominates: DQN stores each frame once and builds the stacks of four frames from neighbouring slots.

## Classic examples

- **Lin's agents (1992).** Lin replayed past experiences, and also lessons given by a teacher, to Q-learning and actor-critic agents with neural networks, and found that replay made learning faster in his navigation tasks.
- **[DQN](/dqn/).** The deep Q-network samples minibatches of 32 from the last $10^6$ transitions. Mnih et al. (2015) report that removing replay hurt the scores on the Atari games they tested.
- **[Double DQN](/double-dqn/), [DDPG](/ddpg/) and [soft actor-critic](/soft-actor-critic/).** Every off-policy deep method of this family keeps a replay buffer of this kind.
- **[Prioritized experience replay](/prioritized-experience-replay/).** It replaces the uniform draw of line L6 by a draw in proportion to a power of the TD error, with importance weights to correct the bias.
- **[Dyna-Q](/dyna-q/).** Dyna learns a model and plans with simulated transitions. A replay buffer is a non-parametric model of the same kind: it can only replay transitions that happened, but it never makes up a wrong one.
- **[Fitted Q-iteration](/fitted-q-iteration/) and [offline RL](/offline-reinforcement-learning/).** If the buffer is never refreshed, replay becomes learning from a fixed data set, which is the offline setting.

## Variations

- **Prioritized replay.** Sample transition $i$ with probability proportional to $p_i^\alpha$, for example $p_i = |\delta_i| + \epsilon$ with the TD error $\delta_i$, using a sum tree for $O(\log N)$ draws ([prioritized experience replay](/prioritized-experience-replay/)).
- **Combined experience replay.** Always add the newest transition to each minibatch (Zhang and Sutton, 2017), so that a very large buffer does not delay the use of new data.
- **Hindsight experience replay.** For goal-conditioned tasks, store each transition again with the goal replaced by a state that was reached later, so that failed episodes still carry reward signal.
- **Reservoir buffers.** Keep a uniform sample of all past transitions instead of the last $N$, with [reservoir sampling](/reservoir-sampling/). This helps when old data must not be forgotten, for example in continual learning and some multi-agent methods.
- **Distributed replay.** Many actors write into one shared buffer and one learner samples from it, as in Ape-X and R2D2; the recurrent variants store short sequences instead of single transitions.

## Pitfalls

- **Using replay with an on-policy rule.** A SARSA or policy gradient update on old transitions estimates the value of the old behavior policies, not of the current one. Use an off-policy target or correct with importance weights.
- **A buffer that is too small or too large.** With a small buffer, consecutive minibatches are again correlated and the learner forgets rare states. With a very large buffer, most samples come from much older policies, and new data takes a long time to matter. Zhang and Sutton (2017) show that performance can be sensitive to $N$ in both directions.
- **Learning before the buffer has data.** Sampling from a buffer of a few transitions gives minibatches of copies. Start the updates after a warm-up.
- **Wrong terminal handling.** The target of a transition that ends an episode is $r$, not $r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a')$. Store the done flag with the transition; a time limit that cuts an episode is not a true terminal state.
- **Nonstationary tasks.** If $P$ or $R$ changes, old transitions are samples of a different MDP, and replay slows adaptation.
- **Replay and divergence.** Replay with function approximation and bootstrapping is an instance of the [deadly triad](/deadly-triad/); it reduces correlation but gives no convergence guarantee.

## Exercises

1. A full buffer of capacity $N$ receives one new transition per step, and each step draws one minibatch of $B$ transitions uniformly with replacement. Show that a transition is sampled $B$ times in expectation during its life in the buffer, whatever $N$ is.
2. In the same buffer, find the probability that a given transition is never sampled during its life, and its limit as $N \to \infty$ with $B$ fixed. (Answer: $(1 - 1/N)^{NB} \to e^{-B}$.)
3. Write the target of an $n$-step return computed from a replayed sequence. Explain why it is biased when the stored actions differ from those of the current greedy policy.
