The Robbins-Monro conditions on a sequence of step sizes $a_n \ge 0$ are $\sum_{n} a_n = \infty$ and $\sum_n a_n^2 < \infty$. Under them, a [stochastic approximation](/stochastic-approximation/) iteration $x_{n+1} = x_n + a_n(h(x_n) + M_{n+1})$ with a stable mean field $h$ and zero-mean noise converges to the root of $h$ almost surely. The first condition lets the iterate travel any distance; the second makes the total injected noise finite. Steps $a_n = 1/n^p$ meet both for $\frac12 < p \le 1$, and a constant step meets only the first, so it leaves a noise floor.

## Statement

**Setting.** Let $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be fixed, let $a_n \ge 0$ be deterministic steps, and let

$$x_{n+1} = x_n + a_n\bigl(h(x_n) + M_{n+1}\bigr), \qquad n \ge 0.$$

Let $\mathcal{F}_n$ be the $\sigma$-algebra generated by $M_1, \ldots, M_n$, so $x_n$ is $\mathcal{F}_n$-measurable. Assume:

- **(H1) strong stability.** There are $x^* \in \mathbb{R}^d$, $\mu > 0$ and $L \ge 0$ with $\langle h(x), x - x^*\rangle \le -\mu\lVert x - x^*\rVert^2$ and $\lVert h(x)\rVert \le L\lVert x - x^*\rVert$ for all $x$.
- **(H2) martingale difference noise.** $\mathbb{E}[M_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[\lVert M_{n+1}\rVert^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_n] \le \sigma^2$ for all $n$.

The **Robbins-Monro conditions** are

$$\text{(RM1)}\quad \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n = \infty, \qquad\qquad \text{(RM2)}\quad \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n^2 < \infty.$$

**Theorem.**

1. **(Sufficiency.)** Under (H1), (H2), (RM1) and (RM2), $x_n \to x^*$ almost surely.
2. **(RM1 cannot be dropped.)** If $\lVert h(x) + M_{n+1}\rVert \le B$ for all $x$ and $n$, then $\lVert x_n - x_0\rVert \le B\sum_{k<n} a_k$ for all $n$. So if $\sum_k a_k < \infty$ and $\lVert x_0 - x^*\rVert > B\sum_k a_k$, the iterates never reach $x^*$.
3. **(RM2 cannot be dropped in general.)** Take $d = 1$, $h(x) = x^* - x$, independent noise $M_{n+1}$ with mean $0$ and variance $\sigma^2 > 0$, and a constant step $a \in (0, 1]$. Then $\mathbb{E}(x_n - x^*)^2 \to a\sigma^2/(2 - a) > 0$, and $x_n$ does not converge almost surely.
4. **(Power steps.)** The steps $a_n = (n + 1)^{-p}$ with $p > 0$ meet (RM1) if and only if $p \le 1$, and (RM2) if and only if $p > \frac12$.

## In plain words

You move toward a target in many small steps, and each step is pushed a little off course by noise. Two things must hold for you to arrive. First, the step lengths must add up to an infinite distance, or else you might run out of steps before you reach the target. Second, the squares of the step lengths must add up to a finite number. The noise in each step is proportional to its length, and the squares measure how much noise piles up, so a finite total means the noise cannot keep you away forever. Steps that shrink like one over the step number do both. Steps that never shrink keep you bouncing around the target, and steps that shrink too fast stop you short.

## Proof

**Part 1.** Write $V_n = \lVert x_n - x^*\rVert^2$ and $y_n = h(x_n) + M_{n+1}$. Expand the square:

$$V_{n+1} = V_n + 2a_n\langle y_n, x_n - x^*\rangle + a_n^2\lVert y_n\rVert^2.$$

Take $\mathbb{E}[\cdot \mid \mathcal{F}_n]$. By (H2) the noise drops out of the middle term, and the cross term $\langle h(x_n), M_{n+1}\rangle$ in $\lVert y_n\rVert^2$ also has conditional mean $0$. With (H1),

$$\mathbb{E}[V_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] \le (1 + L^2a_n^2)V_n + \sigma^2a_n^2 - 2\mu a_nV_n. \tag{1}$$

By induction on $n$, $\mathbb{E}V_n < \infty$ for every $n$.

*Lemma (Robbins and Siegmund, with deterministic $\beta_n, \xi_n$).* Let $V_n \ge 0$ and $\zeta_n \ge 0$ be adapted and integrable, and let $\beta_n, \xi_n \ge 0$ be numbers with $\sum\beta_n < \infty$ and $\sum\xi_n < \infty$. If $\mathbb{E}[V_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] \le (1 + \beta_n)V_n + \xi_n - \zeta_n$ for all $n$, then $V_n$ converges almost surely to a finite limit, and $\sum_n\zeta_n < \infty$ almost surely.

*Proof of the lemma.* Let $\pi_n = \prod_{k<n}(1 + \beta_k)$. It increases to $\pi_\infty \le \exp(\sum_k\beta_k) < \infty$. Define

$$U_n = \frac{V_n}{\pi_n} + \sum_{k<n}\frac{\zeta_k}{\pi_{k+1}} + \sum_{k \ge n}\frac{\xi_k}{\pi_{k+1}} \ge 0.$$

Dividing the hypothesis by $\pi_{n+1} = (1 + \beta_n)\pi_n$ gives $\mathbb{E}[V_{n+1}/\pi_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] \le V_n/\pi_n + (\xi_n - \zeta_n)/\pi_{n+1}$. Add the two sums of $U_{n+1}$: the term $\zeta_n/\pi_{n+1}$ cancels, and $\xi_n/\pi_{n+1}$ joins the tail. So $\mathbb{E}[U_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] \le U_n$, and $U_n$ is a nonnegative supermartingale. By Doob's supermartingale convergence theorem (see [martingale](/martingale/)), $U_n \to U_\infty < \infty$ almost surely. The middle sum $S_n = \sum_{k<n}\zeta_k/\pi_{k+1}$ is nondecreasing and at most $U_n$, so it converges to a finite $S_\infty$, and $\sum_k\zeta_k \le \pi_\infty S_\infty < \infty$. The tail sum of the $\xi_k$ tends to $0$. Hence $V_n/\pi_n \to U_\infty - S_\infty$, and $V_n \to \pi_\infty(U_\infty - S_\infty)$, a finite limit. $\square$

Apply the lemma to (1) with $\beta_n = L^2a_n^2$, $\xi_n = \sigma^2a_n^2$ and $\zeta_n = 2\mu a_nV_n$. Both $\sum\beta_n$ and $\sum\xi_n$ are finite by (RM2). So $V_n \to V_\infty$ almost surely and $\sum_n a_nV_n < \infty$ almost surely. On the event $\{V_\infty > 0\}$, $V_n \ge V_\infty/2$ for all large $n$, so $\sum_n a_nV_n = \infty$ by (RM1). This event therefore has probability $0$, and $V_n \to 0$ almost surely. $\blacksquare$

**Part 2.** $x_n - x_0 = \sum_{k<n}a_k y_k$, and $\lVert y_k\rVert \le B$, so the triangle inequality gives $\lVert x_n - x_0\rVert \le B\sum_{k<n}a_k$. Then $\lVert x_n - x^*\rVert \ge \lVert x_0 - x^*\rVert - B\sum_k a_k > 0$ for all $n$. $\blacksquare$

**Part 3.** Let $d_n = x_n - x^*$. Then $d_{n+1} = (1 - a)d_n + aM_{n+1}$, and since $M_{n+1}$ is independent of $d_n$ with mean $0$, $e_n = \mathbb{E}d_n^2$ satisfies $e_{n+1} = (1 - a)^2e_n + a^2\sigma^2$. The fixed point is $e = a^2\sigma^2/(1 - (1 - a)^2) = a\sigma^2/(2 - a)$, and $e_{n+1} - e = (1 - a)^2(e_n - e)$ with $(1 - a)^2 < 1$, so $e_n \to e > 0$. For the almost sure claim, suppose $x_n \to x^*$ on an event $E$. On $E$, $aM_{n+1} = d_{n+1} - (1 - a)d_n \to 0$. But the $M_{n+1}$ are independent and identically distributed with positive variance, so some $\varepsilon > 0$ has $q = \Pr(\lvert M\rvert > \varepsilon) > 0$, and by the second Borel-Cantelli lemma $\lvert M_{n+1}\rvert > \varepsilon$ for infinitely many $n$ almost surely. Hence $\Pr(E) = 0$. $\blacksquare$

**Part 4.** For $q > 0$, the function $t^{-q}$ is decreasing, so $\int_1^{N+1}t^{-q}\,dt \le \sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-q} \le 1 + \int_1^{N}t^{-q}\,dt$. The integral stays bounded as $N \to \infty$ exactly when $q > 1$. With $q = p$ this gives (RM1) if and only if $p \le 1$, and with $q = 2p$ it gives (RM2) if and only if $p > \frac12$. $\blacksquare$

## Consequences

- **Standard schedules.** $a_n = c/(n + n_0)$ meets both conditions for any $c > 0$ and $n_0 > 0$, and so does $a_n = c(n + 1)^{-p}$ for $\frac12 < p \le 1$. With $a_n = 1/(n + 1)$ and $h(x) = \mathbb{E}Z - x$, the iterate is the sample mean, and the theorem becomes a [law of large numbers](/law-of-large-numbers/) for martingale differences.
- **Per-component steps in reinforcement learning.** [Q-learning](/q-learning/) and tabular [temporal difference learning](/temporal-difference-learning/) update one state, or one state and action pair, at a time, with a step that depends on how often it was visited. Their convergence proofs need, for each pair $(s, a)$, $\sum_t\alpha_t(s, a) = \infty$ and $\sum_t\alpha_t(s, a)^2 < \infty$ with probability one (Jaakkola, Jordan and Singh 1994; Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis 1996, Chapter 4). The first condition forces each pair to be visited infinitely often. The step $\alpha = 1/N(s, a)$, with $N(s, a)$ the visit count, meets both on that event. See [tabular Q-learning convergence](/tabular-q-learning-convergence/).
- **Linear TD and gradient TD.** The convergence theorems of TD with linear [value function approximation](/value-function-approximation/) (Tsitsiklis and Van Roy 1997) and of [gradient temporal difference](/gradient-temporal-difference/) methods assume these conditions, the latter for each of its two step sequences.
- **Stochastic gradient descent.** For a strongly convex objective with bounded gradient noise, (H1) holds with $h = -\nabla f$, and Part 1 gives almost sure convergence of [stochastic gradient descent](/stochastic-gradient-descent/).

## Tightness

Together the two conditions are sufficient. Only the first is necessary in general.

- **(RM1) is necessary in a strong sense.** Part 2 holds for any bounded field and noise: no noise model can repair summable steps.
- **(RM2) is not necessary.** In the example of Part 3 with steps $a_n \to 0$ and $\sum a_n = \infty$, $e_n \to 0$ even when $\sum a_n^2 = \infty$. Proof: for $a_n \le 1$, $e_{n+1} \le (1 - a_n)e_n + a_n(a_n\sigma^2)$. Given $\varepsilon > 0$, pick $N$ with $a_n\sigma^2 \le \varepsilon$ for $n \ge N$. Then $e_{n+1} - \varepsilon \le (1 - a_n)(e_n - \varepsilon)$, so $\max(e_n - \varepsilon, 0) \le \prod_{k=N}^{n-1}(1 - a_k)\max(e_N - \varepsilon, 0) \to 0$. What a constant step lacks is $a_n \to 0$. Kushner and Yin (2003, Chapter 5) give noise conditions under which $a_n \to 0$ and (RM1) alone give almost sure convergence. (RM2) is a simple condition that works for every martingale difference noise with bounded conditional variance, as Part 1 shows.

With $a_n = 1/(n + 1)$, $e_n = \sigma^2/n$ exactly for $n \ge 1$, which is the [variance](/variance/) of a mean of $n$ samples and the best possible rate. The constant step $0.1$ stops at its floor $0.1/1.9 = 1/19$. Over 400 seeds with Gaussian noise, the empirical mean of $(x_{1000} - x^*)^2$ is $0.00097$ for $1/(n + 1)$ and $0.0510$ for the constant step. The summable steps $2^{-(n + 1)}$ stop short even with no noise: then $x_n - x^* = \prod_{k=1}^{n}(1 - 2^{-k})(x_0 - x^*)$, and the product tends to $0.28879$.

The constant step is still useful. Its floor $a\sigma^2/(2 - a)$ is small for small $a$, and a constant step forgets old data at a fixed rate, so it can follow a target that moves. For this reason many programs run [Q-learning](/q-learning/) with a constant step and accept the floor. Sutton and Barto (2018, Section 2.5) discuss this trade-off.

## History

Robbins and Monro (1951) introduced the iteration together with the two step conditions and proved convergence in mean square. Blum (1954) proved convergence with probability one. Robbins and Siegmund (1971) proved the almost supermartingale theorem, a general form of the lemma above, which became the standard tool. In reinforcement learning, Jaakkola, Jordan and Singh (1994) and Tsitsiklis (1994) used the conditions for each state and action separately to prove the convergence of Q-learning and TD($\lambda$).
