Policy evaluation is the problem of computing the [state value function](/state-value-function/) $V^\pi$ of a fixed [policy](/policy/) $\pi$ in a [Markov decision process](/markov-decision-process/): for each state, the expected [discounted return](/discounted-return/) when the agent starts there and follows $\pi$. Sutton and Barto also call it the prediction problem. With a known model and $\gamma < 1$ it is the solution of one linear system, the [Bellman expectation equation](/bellman-expectation-equation/), so it costs one [Gaussian elimination](/gaussian-elimination/). It is the evaluation step of [policy iteration](/policy-iteration/), and without a model it is the prediction task that Monte Carlo and temporal difference methods solve from samples.

## In plain words

Someone gives you a fixed rule for acting, and you want to know how good it is. For each starting place, you want the total reward you can expect if you follow the rule from there, with later rewards counted a little less.

You do not change the rule. You only measure it. If you know the odds of every move, the values of all places are tied together by one set of equations, one for each place, and you can solve them. If you do not know the odds, you can still estimate the values by watching what happens when someone follows the rule.

## Definition

**Input.** A finite MDP $(S, A, P, R, \gamma)$ with $0 \le \gamma < 1$ and a stationary policy that takes action $a$ in state $s$ with probability $\pi(a \mid s)$.

**Output.** The vector $V^\pi$ with

$$V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\sum_{t \ge 0} \gamma^t r_{t+1} \,\Big|\, s_0 = s\Big].$$

Let $P_\pi(s, s') = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s)P(s' \mid s, a)$ and $r_\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s)R(s, a)$. Then $V^\pi$ is the unique solution of

$$(I - \gamma P_\pi)\,V = r_\pi,$$

so $V^\pi = (I - \gamma P_\pi)^{-1} r_\pi = \sum_{t \ge 0} \gamma^t P_\pi^t r_\pi$. The matrix $I - \gamma P_\pi$ is invertible because its rows are strictly diagonally dominant when $\gamma < 1$.

**Sample version.** The model is unknown. The input is a stream of transitions $(s_t, a_t, r_{t+1}, s_{t+1})$ generated by following $\pi$, and the output is an estimate of $V^\pi$ that converges to it as the number of samples grows.

## Variants

- **Action values.** Compute the [action value function](/action-value-function/) $Q^\pi(s, a) = R(s, a) + \gamma\sum_{s'}P(s' \mid s, a)V^\pi(s')$. It is needed for improvement without a model.
- **Episodic, no discount.** With $\gamma = 1$ and terminal states that the policy reaches with probability 1, $V^\pi$ is the expected total reward to termination, the solution of the same system restricted to the nonterminal states.
- **Average reward.** Compute the gain (the long-run average reward) and the bias of the policy. They solve a singular system with one extra normalization.
- **Off-policy.** Evaluate $\pi$ from data generated by another policy: [off-policy evaluation](/off-policy-evaluation/).
- **Function approximation.** With many states, find the best value function within a parametric class, for example a linear one. [Least squares TD](/least-squares-td/) solves the projected Bellman equation for linear features.

## Examples

- **Two states.** In the example of [value iteration](/value-iteration/), with $\gamma = 0.9$, the policy that moves in L and stays in H has $V(\mathrm{H}) = 2/(1 - 0.9) = 20$ and $V(\mathrm{L}) = 0.9(V(\mathrm{H}) + V(\mathrm{L}))/2$, so $V(\mathrm{L}) = 180/11$.
- **Gridworld.** In the 4 by 4 grid of Sutton and Barto (Example 4.1), with reward $-1$ per move and the uniform random policy, $V^\pi(s)$ is minus the expected number of moves to a corner, from $-14$ next to a corner to $-22$ in the two far corners. See [iterative policy evaluation](/iterative-policy-evaluation/).
- **A Markov reward process.** A policy turns an MDP into a Markov chain with rewards. Evaluation of a policy is the computation of the discounted reward of this [Markov chain](/markov-chain/).

## Known algorithms

| algorithm | model | cost or guarantee |
| --- | --- | --- |
| direct solve by [Gaussian elimination](/gaussian-elimination/) | known | exact; $O(\lvert S\rvert^3)$ after $O(\lvert S\rvert^2\lvert A\rvert)$ to build the system |
| [Iterative policy evaluation](/iterative-policy-evaluation/) | known | error $\gamma^k$ times the first error after $k$ sweeps |
| [Monte Carlo policy evaluation](/monte-carlo-policy-evaluation/) | samples | unbiased averages of observed returns |
| [Temporal difference learning](/temporal-difference-learning/), [TD(λ)](/td-lambda/) | samples | converges with suitable step sizes |
| [Least squares TD](/least-squares-td/) | samples | solves the sample version of the projected linear system |

## Bounds

**Exact, with a model.** The direct solve needs $O(|S|^3)$ arithmetic operations, or $O(|S|^\omega)$ with fast matrix multiplication, where $\omega < 2.372$. Building $P_\pi$ and $r_\pi$ needs $O(|S|^2|A|)$ in the worst case. Because the matrix is strictly diagonally dominant, elimination never meets a zero pivot, even without row exchanges.

**Iterative, with a model.** Each sweep costs $O(|S|^2|A|)$ in the worst case, and $O(\ln(1/\varepsilon)/(1-\gamma))$ sweeps reach error $\varepsilon$ relative to the start. This is cheaper than a direct solve when the transitions are sparse and $1/(1-\gamma)$ is small compared with $|S|$.

**From samples.** The error of a sample estimate falls like one over the square root of the number of samples, with a constant that grows with the variance of the returns and with $1/(1-\gamma)$.

## Reductions

- **To a linear system.** Policy evaluation is the solution of $(I - \gamma P_\pi)V = r_\pi$, a system with a diagonally dominant M-matrix.
- **From MDP planning.** Every iteration of [policy iteration](/policy-iteration/) solves one policy evaluation problem, so [MDP planning](/mdp-planning/) reduces to a sequence of them.
- **Markov chains.** Expected hitting times and absorption probabilities of a [Markov chain](/markov-chain/) are policy evaluation problems with one action per state and $\gamma = 1$.

## Applications

Policy evaluation is the critic in [actor-critic](/actor-critic/) methods, the evaluation step of policy iteration, and the tool for comparing a new policy with an old one before deployment. In operations research it gives the long-run cost of a fixed rule, such as a fixed inventory rule or a fixed maintenance schedule.
