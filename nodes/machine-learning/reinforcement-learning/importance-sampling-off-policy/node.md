Importance sampling for off-policy learning estimates the value of a target policy $\pi$ from episodes generated by a different behavior policy $\mu$. Each return is reweighted by the importance sampling ratio $\rho_{0:T-1} = \prod_t \pi(A_t \mid S_t)/\mu(A_t \mid S_t)$, the relative probability of its action sequence under the two policies. The transition probabilities cancel, so the ratio needs no model. The ordinary estimator, the mean of $\rho G$, is unbiased but can have very large, even infinite, variance. The weighted estimator divides by the sum of the ratios instead of the count; it is biased but consistent and usually far less variable. The per-decision estimator of Precup, Sutton and Singh (2000) weights each reward only by the ratios of the actions before it. It is the general [importance sampling](/importance-sampling/) method applied to trajectories.

## In plain words

You want to know how well a new way of acting would do, but all your records come from an old way of acting. Some records are useful: the old way happened to make choices the new way would also make. The method counts each record more or less, by how much more or less likely the new way was to make the same choices.

A record whose choices the new way would never make counts for nothing. A record whose choices the new way likes much more counts a lot. Over many records the weighted average comes out right. But a few records with very large weights can swing the answer, so a second version divides by the total weight rather than the number of records. It is a little off for small samples, and much steadier.

## When it applies

- You have episodes, or n-step pieces of trajectories, from a behavior policy $\mu$, and you want values of a different policy $\pi$: a greedy policy in control, a candidate policy before it is deployed, or many policies from one stream of experience.
- You know the action probabilities $\mu(a \mid s)$, or can estimate them, and $\pi(a \mid s)$.
- Coverage holds: $\pi(a \mid s) > 0$ implies $\mu(a \mid s) > 0$. Without it the actions that $\pi$ takes and $\mu$ never takes are invisible, and no reweighting can recover their value.

The setting on this page is: episodes $S_0, A_0, R_1, S_1, \ldots, S_T$ from $\mu$, with a finite end time $T$; the return $G = \sum_{t<T}\gamma^t R_{t+1}$; and the ratio $\rho_{0:t} = \prod_{k=0}^{t}\pi(A_k \mid S_k)/\mu(A_k \mid S_k)$. The target is the [state value](/state-value-function/) $v_\pi(s_0)$ of the start state.

## The template

```pseudo id=main
OFF-POLICY-IS(episodes from μ, π, μ, γ)
L1  n ← 0;  S_ord ← 0;  S_w ← 0;  S_pd ← 0
L2  for each episode (S₀, A₀, R₁, …, S_T) do
L3      ρ ← 1;  G ← 0;  G_pd ← 0
L4      for t ← 0 to T − 1 do
L5          ρ ← ρ · π(Aₜ | Sₜ) / μ(Aₜ | Sₜ)          ▷ now ρ = ρ_{0:t}
L6          G ← G + γᵗ Rₜ₊₁;  G_pd ← G_pd + γᵗ ρ Rₜ₊₁
L7      n ← n + 1;  S_ord ← S_ord + ρ G;  S_w ← S_w + ρ;  S_pd ← S_pd + G_pd
L8  return S_ord / n (ordinary),  S_ord / S_w (weighted),  S_pd / n (per-decision)
```

The cost is $O(1)$ per step of data and $O(1)$ memory. For values of every state, run the same sums from each visit, as in [Monte Carlo policy evaluation](/monte-carlo-policy-evaluation/), and for incremental updates write the weighted estimate in the form $V \leftarrow V + \frac{W}{C}(G - V)$ with $C$ the running sum of the weights $W$ (Sutton and Barto 2018, Section 5.6).

**Why the ratio works.** The probability of an episode under a policy is the product of the policy terms and the transition terms. The transition terms are the same under $\pi$ and $\mu$, so

$$\frac{\Pr_\pi(\text{episode})}{\Pr_\mu(\text{episode})} = \prod_{t<T}\frac{\pi(A_t \mid S_t)}{\mu(A_t \mid S_t)} = \rho_{0:T-1}, \qquad \mathbb{E}_\mu[\rho_{0:T-1}G] = \mathbb{E}_\pi[G] = v_\pi(s_0).$$

So the ordinary estimate is unbiased. Its variance is $\mathbb{E}_\mu[\rho^2G^2] - v_\pi^2$, and $\rho$ is a product of $T$ factors, so the variance can grow exponentially with the length of the episodes.

**Per-decision weights.** The reward $R_{t+1}$ depends only on the history up to $A_t$. The factors of $\rho$ after time $t$ have conditional mean $1$ given that history: $\mathbb{E}_\mu[\pi(A_k \mid S_k)/\mu(A_k \mid S_k) \mid S_k] = \sum_a \pi(a \mid S_k) = 1$. So $\mathbb{E}_\mu[\rho_{0:T-1}R_{t+1}] = \mathbb{E}_\mu[\rho_{0:t}R_{t+1}]$, and the per-decision estimator $\sum_t \gamma^t\rho_{0:t}R_{t+1}$ is also unbiased. It drops factors that only add noise, which often lowers the variance, though not always.

**Weighted importance sampling.** The weighted estimate $\sum_i \rho_i G_i/\sum_i \rho_i$ is a ratio of two unbiased means. With one episode it returns $G$ itself, whose expectation is $v_\mu(s_0)$ when $\pi$ has full support, not $v_\pi(s_0)$. As the number of episodes grows, both sums divided by $n$ converge to their means, $v_\pi(s_0)$ and $1$, so the estimate is consistent: the bias goes to $0$. Its value lies between the smallest and largest observed return, so a single huge ratio cannot push it out of range. In practice it is usually preferred (Sutton and Barto 2018, Section 5.5).

**A worked example: infinite variance.** Sutton and Barto (2018, Example 5.5) use one nonterminal state with two actions. The action *left* returns to the state with probability $0.9$ and reward $0$, and ends the episode with reward $+1$ with probability $0.1$. The action *right* ends the episode with reward $0$. The target policy always takes *left*, so $v_\pi(s) = 1$; the behavior policy takes each action with probability $1/2$; and $\gamma = 1$. An episode that takes *left* $k + 1$ times and ends with reward $+1$ has probability $0.5^{k+1}0.9^k0.1$ under $\mu$ and ratio $2^{k+1}$; every episode that takes *right* has ratio $0$. So

$$\mathbb{E}_\mu[\rho G] = \sum_{k \ge 0} 0.5^{k+1}0.9^k0.1 \cdot 2^{k+1} = \sum_{k \ge 0} 0.1 \cdot 0.9^k = 1, \qquad \mathbb{E}_\mu[(\rho G)^2] = \sum_{k \ge 0} 0.5^{k+1}0.9^k0.1 \cdot 4^{k+1} = \sum_{k \ge 0} 0.2 \cdot 1.8^k = \infty.$$

The ordinary estimate is unbiased with infinite variance. The weighted estimate is exactly $1$ as soon as one episode ends with *left*, because every episode with $\rho > 0$ has return $1$. Runs with seed 0:

| episodes | ordinary IS | weighted IS |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0 | 0 (no episode with $\rho > 0$ yet) |
| 100 | 0.36 | 1 |
| 1000 | 0.74 | 1 |
| 10000 | 0.85 | 1 |
| 100000 | 1.30 | 1 |

The ordinary estimate still has not settled after $10^5$ episodes, and it never will in the usual sense: the [law of large numbers](/law-of-large-numbers/) still gives convergence with probability one, but there is no $1/\sqrt{n}$ rate.

## Classic examples

- **Off-policy Monte Carlo control.** [Monte Carlo control](/monte-carlo-control/) learns a greedy target policy from an exploratory behavior policy, with weighted importance sampling. Because $\pi$ is greedy, the ratio is $0$ after the first non-greedy action, and only the tails of episodes are used.
- **n-step off-policy TD.** The $n$-step return is weighted by $\rho_{t:t+n-1}$, which needs only $n$ ratios (Sutton and Barto 2018, Section 7.3); see [n-step TD](/n-step-td/).
- **Off-policy eligibility traces.** Precup, Sutton and Singh (2000) write the per-decision estimator as an eligibility trace algorithm, whose trace is multiplied by the ratio at each step.
- **Off-policy evaluation of logged decisions.** In recommendation and advertising, logged data from a deployed policy are reweighted to estimate a new policy; see [off-policy evaluation](/off-policy-evaluation/).

## Variations

- **Truncated and clipped ratios.** Replace $\rho$ by $\min(\bar{c}, \rho)$ to bound the variance at the price of bias. [Retrace](/retrace/) uses $\lambda\min(1, \rho)$ in its traces, and [V-trace](/v-trace/) clips the ratios at $\bar{\rho}$ and $\bar{c}$.
- **Doubly robust estimators.** Subtract a learned value as a control variate, so the ratios only correct its errors; see [off-policy evaluation](/off-policy-evaluation/).
- **Discounting-aware importance sampling.** Treat the discount as a probability of ending, so that the ratios after the effective end of the return are not used (Sutton and Barto 2018, Section 5.8).
- **Weighted importance sampling with linear features.** Mahmood, van Hasselt and Sutton (2014) extend the weighted estimator to linear function approximation.
- **Tree backup.** Avoid ratios entirely by weighting each later action by $\pi(a \mid s)$, which has no variance blow-up but cuts traces short when $\mu$ is far from $\pi$.

## Pitfalls

- **Missing coverage.** If $\mu(a \mid s) = 0$ for an action with $\pi(a \mid s) > 0$, the estimators are biased and nothing in the data shows it.
- **Exponential variance in the horizon.** A product of $T$ ratios, each with mean $1$, can have a second moment that grows like $c^T$. Long episodes need per-decision weights, truncation or bootstrapping.
- **Estimated behavior probabilities.** When $\mu$ is estimated from logs, errors in small values of $\mu$ become large errors in the ratios.
- **Reading the weighted estimate as unbiased.** For a few episodes the weighted estimate is pulled toward the behavior policy's value. Compare estimators at the same sample size, and report the [effective sample size](/effective-sample-size/) $(\sum\rho)^2/\sum\rho^2$.

## Exercises

1. Show that the transition probabilities cancel in $\Pr_\pi(\text{episode})/\Pr_\mu(\text{episode})$.
2. Prove that the weighted estimate computed from one episode equals the return of that episode, and find its expectation when $\pi(a \mid s) > 0$ for every $a$ and $s$.
3. In Example 5.5, change the behavior policy to take *left* with probability $p$. For which $p$ is the variance of the ordinary estimator finite?
4. Give a two-step example in which the per-decision estimator has a larger variance than the ordinary estimator.
