We use the notation of the page. $f$ is an integrable density on $\mathbb{R}^3$, $R_1, \dots, R_m$ are rotations, and image $i$ is $P_i(x, y) = \int f(R_i (x, y, z)^T)\, dz$. The columns of $R_i$ are the image axes and the beam direction $\nu_i = R_i e_3$. For a unit vector $c$ in the image plane, $p_i(c, s) = \int P_i(s c + t c^\perp)\, dt$ is the line projection of image $i$ in the direction $c$, where $c^\perp$ is $c$ turned by 90 degrees; this is one line of the sinogram of $P_i$. We write $\hat c = (c_1, c_2, 0)^T$, and $\mathcal{R} f(\omega, s) = \int_{x \cdot \omega = s} f\, dA$ for the 3D Radon transform (integrals over planes).

The code only finds the common lines approximately, on a grid of $a$ angles with a parabola refinement, and the match of the lines is not a theorem; the page gives measurements for it. What is proved here is the part after the lines: the statements that make the search meaningful (Lemma 1) and the exactness of lines L4 to L9 (Theorems 3 and 4).

**Lemma 1 (common lines).** If $\nu_i \ne \pm \nu_j$, let $q = (\nu_i \times \nu_j)/|\nu_i \times \nu_j|$, $c_{ij} = (R_i^T q)_{1,2}$ and $c_{ji} = (R_j^T q)_{1,2}$. Then $c_{ij}$ and $c_{ji}$ are unit vectors, $R_i \hat c_{ij} = R_j \hat c_{ji} = q$, and
$$p_i(c_{ij}, s) = p_j(c_{ji}, s) = \mathcal{R} f(q, s) \quad \text{for all } s.$$
Also $p_i(-c, s) = p_i(c, -s)$, so the line of image $j$ in the direction $-c_{ji}$ is the line in the direction $c_{ji}$ read in reverse.

*Proof.* The third coordinate of $R_i^T q$ is $\nu_i \cdot q = 0$, so $R_i^T q = \hat c_{ij}$, $|c_{ij}| = |q| = 1$ and $R_i \hat c_{ij} = q$; the same holds for $j$. For a unit $c$ in the image plane, the vectors $\hat c$, $\hat c^\perp$ and $e_3$ are an orthonormal basis, and $R_i$ maps it to the orthonormal basis $R_i \hat c$, $R_i \hat c^\perp$, $\nu_i$. So
$$p_i(c, s) = \iint f\big(s R_i \hat c + t R_i \hat c^\perp + z\, \nu_i\big)\, dt\, dz = \mathcal{R} f(R_i \hat c, s),$$
because $(t, z) \mapsto s R_i \hat c + t R_i \hat c^\perp + z \nu_i$ is an isometry onto the plane $\{x : x \cdot R_i \hat c = s\}$. With $c = c_{ij}$ this is $\mathcal{R} f(q, s)$, and the same computation in image $j$ gives the same value. The last claim is the change $t \mapsto -t$, since $(-c)^\perp = -c^\perp$. $\square$

By the [Fourier slice theorem](/fourier-slice-theorem/), with the convention $\mathcal{F} g(\xi) = \int g(x) e^{-2\pi i x \cdot \xi}\, dx$, the 1D transform of $s \mapsto \mathcal{R} f(q, s)$ is $\rho \mapsto \mathcal{F} f(\rho q)$, so Lemma 1 says the same thing in Fourier space: the 2D transforms of $P_i$ and $P_j$ agree on the central lines in the directions $c_{ij}$ and $c_{ji}$, which are the two images of the line where the central planes $\nu_i^\perp$ and $\nu_j^\perp$ meet.

**Lemma 2 (frames).** For linearly independent $u, v \in \mathbb{R}^3$, $\mathrm{FRAME}(u, v)$ is the matrix with the columns $e_1 = u/|u|$, $e_2 = $ the unit vector along $v - (v \cdot e_1) e_1$, and $e_3 = e_1 \times e_2$. It is a rotation. For every rotation $T$, $\mathrm{FRAME}(Tu, Tv) = T\, \mathrm{FRAME}(u, v)$. For the reflection $J = \mathrm{diag}(1, 1, -1)$, $\mathrm{FRAME}(Ju, Jv) = J\, \mathrm{FRAME}(u, v)\, J$.

*Proof.* The columns are orthonormal by construction and $e_3 = e_1 \times e_2$, so the determinant is $+1$. An orthogonal $T$ keeps lengths and dot products, so the first two columns of $\mathrm{FRAME}(Tu, Tv)$ are $T e_1$ and $T e_2$. For any orthogonal $T$, $Ta \times Tb = \det(T)\, T(a \times b)$. For a rotation the third column is $T e_3$. For $J$, with $\det J = -1$, it is $-J e_3$, and changing the sign of the third column is multiplication by $J$ on the right. $\square$

**Theorem 3 (placing one image, line L9).** Let $G$ be a rotation and let $\epsilon$ be either the identity or the map $X \mapsto J X J$. Suppose the placed images $u$ and $v$ have $\hat R_u = \epsilon(G R_u)$ and $\hat R_v = \epsilon(G R_v)$, the common lines are exact, and the beams $\nu_k$, $\nu_u$, $\nu_v$ are linearly independent. Then
$$\hat R_k = \mathrm{FRAME}(\hat R_u \hat c_{uk}, \hat R_v \hat c_{vk})\, \mathrm{FRAME}(\hat c_{ku}, \hat c_{kv})^T = \epsilon(G R_k).$$

*Proof.* Let $q_{uk} = R_u \hat c_{uk} = R_k \hat c_{ku}$ and $q_{vk} = R_v \hat c_{vk} = R_k \hat c_{kv}$ (Lemma 1). They are not parallel: a vector parallel to both would be orthogonal to $\nu_k$, $\nu_u$ and $\nu_v$, which span $\mathbb{R}^3$. Write $F = \mathrm{FRAME}(\hat c_{ku}, \hat c_{kv})$. Take first $\epsilon$ the identity. Then $\hat R_u \hat c_{uk} = G q_{uk} = G R_k \hat c_{ku}$, and in the same way $\hat R_v \hat c_{vk} = G R_k \hat c_{kv}$. By Lemma 2 with $T = G R_k$, the first frame is $G R_k F$, and $\hat R_k = G R_k F F^T = G R_k$. For $\epsilon(X) = J X J$, use $J \hat c = \hat c$ for vectors in the image plane: $\hat R_u \hat c_{uk} = J G R_u J \hat c_{uk} = J G q_{uk}$, and the same for $v$. By Lemma 2 (both parts) the first frame is $J\, G R_k F\, J$. The first two columns of $F$ lie in the image plane and the third is $\pm e_3$, so $F J F^T$ is the reflection in the image plane, which is $J$. So $\hat R_k = J G R_k F J F^T = J G R_k J$. $\square$

**Theorem 4 (the anchor triangle, lines L5 to L7).** Let images $a$, $b$, $d$ have linearly independent beams and exact common lines. Lines L5 to L7 return $\hat R_a = I$ and $\hat R_b$, $\hat R_d$ with
$$\hat R_i = G R_i \ \text{ for } i \in \{a, b, d\}, \qquad \text{or} \qquad \hat R_i = J G R_i J \ \text{ for } i \in \{a, b, d\},$$
where $G = R_a^T$. The first case holds when the third coordinate of $G q_{bd}$ is positive, the second when it is negative, and it is never zero.

*Proof.* Put $q'_{xy} = G q_{xy}$ for the three common directions $q_{ab}$, $q_{ad}$, $q_{bd}$ of Lemma 1. By Lemma 1, $q'_{ab} = R_a^T R_a \hat c_{ab} = \hat c_{ab}$ and $q'_{ad} = \hat c_{ad}$, which is line L5. They are not parallel, by the argument of Theorem 3, so the $2 \times 2$ determinant in the code is not zero. The unknown $q'_{bd}$ is a unit vector with
$$q'_{bd} \cdot q'_{ab} = q_{bd} \cdot q_{ab} = R_b \hat c_{bd} \cdot R_b \hat c_{ba} = c_{bd} \cdot c_{ba}, \qquad q'_{bd} \cdot q'_{ad} = c_{db} \cdot c_{da}.$$
Since $q'_{ab}$ and $q'_{ad}$ span the plane $z = 0$, these two equations fix the first two coordinates $(x, y)$ of $q'_{bd}$, and $|q'_{bd}| = 1$ fixes the third up to its sign, $\pm\sqrt{1 - x^2 - y^2}$. The third coordinate is $q'_{bd} \cdot e_3 = G q_{bd} \cdot G \nu_a = q_{bd} \cdot \nu_a$, which is not zero, because $q_{bd}$ is orthogonal to $\nu_b$ and $\nu_d$, and a vector orthogonal to all three independent beams is zero. Line L6 takes the positive sign.

If the positive sign is the true one, line L6 returns $q'_{bd}$, and line L7 computes $\hat R_b = \mathrm{FRAME}(G q_{ab}, G q_{bd})\, \mathrm{FRAME}(\hat c_{ba}, \hat c_{bd})^T = G R_b$ by Lemma 2 with $T = G R_b$, as in Theorem 3. The same holds for $d$, and $\hat R_a = I = G R_a$. If the negative sign is the true one, line L6 returns $J q'_{bd}$, and $q'_{ab} = J q'_{ab}$, $q'_{ad} = J q'_{ad}$ because they lie in the plane $z = 0$. So line L7 builds its frames from $J G q_{ab}$, $J G q_{bd}$, and the second half of the proof of Theorem 3 gives $\hat R_b = J G R_b J$, and the same for $d$. Also $I = J G R_a J$. $\square$

**Corollary 5 (what the code returns from exact lines).** Suppose no two beams are equal or opposite, the beams of some three images are linearly independent, and the common lines of all pairs are exact. Then `rotations_from_common_lines` returns $\hat R_i = G R_i$ for all $i$, or $\hat R_i = J G R_i J$ for all $i$, with one rotation $G$.

*Proof.* For images $k$, $u$, $v$, the two common lines $c_{ku}$ and $c_{kv}$ in image $k$ are parallel exactly when $q_{ku} \parallel q_{kv}$, that is, when one unit vector is orthogonal to $\nu_k$, $\nu_u$ and $\nu_v$, that is, when the three beams are linearly dependent. Line L4 picks the triple with the largest value of the smallest $|\sin|$ between the two common lines in each of its images. Some triple has independent beams and so a positive value, hence the chosen triple has a positive value and independent beams, and Theorem 4 places it. At each step of line L8, take any unplaced $k$. Among the pairs of the anchor triple $a$, $b$, $d$, at least one forms an independent triple with $\nu_k$: if $\nu_k$ lay in the plane of every two of $\nu_a$, $\nu_b$, $\nu_d$, it would lie in the intersection of the planes spanned by $\nu_a, \nu_b$ and by $\nu_a, \nu_d$, which is the line of $\nu_a$, so $\nu_k = \pm \nu_a$, against the hypothesis. So the largest $|\sin|$ found at line L8 is positive, its triple is independent, and Theorem 3 places $k$ with the same $G$ and the same $\epsilon$. By induction every image gets $\epsilon(G R_i)$. In floating point the code raises an error when the best value at line L4 or line L8 is below $10^{-9}$, instead of returning a meaningless answer. $\square$

**Lemma 6 (the reflection cannot be resolved).** Let $f_J(x) = f(J x)$. The images of $f_J$ at the rotations $J R_i J$ are the images of $f$ at the rotations $R_i$.

*Proof.* $\int f_J(J R_i J (x, y, z)^T)\, dz = \int f(R_i (x, y, -z)^T)\, dz = P_i(x, y)$. $\square$

So no method can tell the two answers of Corollary 5 apart from the images alone: the second one is the correct answer for the mirror image $f_J$ of the molecule, rotated by $J G J$. The same lemma with a global rotation shows that $G$ cannot be fixed either: the images of $f(G^T \cdot)$ at $G R_i$ are the images of $f$ at $R_i$. Corollary 5 and Lemma 6 together say that three or more images in general position fix the orientations exactly up to these two ambiguities. The function `align_error` measures the error after removing both. The tests check Corollary 5 on random rotations and the hand example of the page, Lemma 2, Lemma 6, and the error raised for single-axis tilts, where all beams lie in one plane.
