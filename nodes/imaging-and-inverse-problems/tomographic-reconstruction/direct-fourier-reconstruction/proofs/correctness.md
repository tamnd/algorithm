We use the conventions of the page. $\omega(\theta) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, $\mathcal{R}f(\theta, s) = \int f(s\,\omega + t\,\omega^\perp)\,dt$ for $\theta \in [0, \pi)$, and $\mathcal{F}f(\xi) = \int f(x) e^{-2\pi i x\cdot\xi}\,dx$. The image has the pixel size $h = 2/n$ with $n$ even, and pixel $(i, j)$ has its center at $x_j = -1 + (2j+1)/n$ and $y_i = 1 - (2i+1)/n$. The Cartesian grid has $N = 2n$ points on each axis and the spacing $\Delta\xi = 1/(Nh)$.

The proof has three parts, one for each stage of the algorithm. Lines L2 and L3 give exact samples of $\mathcal{F}f$ on the polar grid when the projections are band-limited (Lemma 1). Lines L8 and L9 give the exact Riemann sum of the inverse Fourier integral at the pixel centers, and that sum equals $f$ when the spectrum is known on the whole lattice (Lemmas 2 and 3). Lines L4 to L7 connect the two grids by interpolation, which is the main approximation of the method.

**Lemma 1 (the polar samples).** Let $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^2)$. Fix $\theta_k$ and put $p(s) = \mathcal{R}f(\theta_k, s)$ and $c_0 = (d-1)/2$. Assume that $p$ is continuous, that its transform $P$ vanishes for $|\nu| \ge 1/(2\tau)$, and that $p(s_j) = 0$ for every $s_j = (j - c_0)\tau$ with $j < 0$ or $j \ge d$. Then for every $l$ with signed frequency $|\sigma_l| < 1/(2\tau)$, where $\sigma_l = l/(L\tau)$ for $l < L/2$ and $(l - L)/(L\tau)$ otherwise,
$$S_k[l] := \tau\, e^{2\pi i c_0 \tau \sigma_l} \sum_{j=0}^{d-1} p(s_j)\, e^{-2\pi i j l/L} = \mathcal{F}f(\sigma_l\,\omega(\theta_k)).$$

*Proof.* Because $j$ is an integer and $\sigma_l L\tau \equiv l \pmod L$, we have $e^{-2\pi i j l/L} = e^{-2\pi i j\tau\sigma_l}$. So $S_k[l] = \tau \sum_j p(s_j) e^{-2\pi i s_j \sigma_l}$, and the terms with $j$ outside $0, \ldots, d-1$ are zero by assumption. Let $\tilde p(s) = p(s - c_0\tau)$, so that $\tilde p(j\tau) = p(s_j)$ and $\tilde P(\nu) = e^{-2\pi i c_0\tau\nu} P(\nu)$. The function $\tilde P$ is continuous, vanishes outside $(-B, B)$ with $B = 1/(2\tau)$, and its periodic extension with period $1/\tau$ has the Fourier coefficients $\tau\,\tilde p(j\tau)$. As in the proof for [filtered back-projection](/filtered-back-projection/), this gives $\tilde P(\nu) = \tau\sum_j \tilde p(j\tau)\, e^{-2\pi i j\tau\nu}$ for $|\nu| < B$, where the sum has finitely many terms. Hence $\tau\sum_j p(s_j) e^{-2\pi i s_j\sigma} = e^{2\pi i c_0\tau\sigma}\,\tilde P(\sigma) = P(\sigma)$ for $|\sigma| < B$, and the [Fourier slice theorem](/fourier-slice-theorem/) gives $P(\sigma) = \mathcal{F}f(\sigma\,\omega(\theta_k))$. Line L3 computes the left side, because the DFT of the zero-padded projection is $\sum_{j=0}^{d-1} p(s_j)\, e^{-2\pi i j l/L}$. $\square$

The lemma holds for every $L \ge d$. The choice $L \ge 4d$ in line L1 does not change the samples. It makes the radial grid four times finer than the natural spacing $1/(d\tau)$, which reduces the error of the radial interpolation in line L6.

**Lemma 2 (the grid to the image).** Let $F$ be any function on the lattice $\{(u\Delta\xi, v\Delta\xi)\}$ and let $G[v \bmod N][u \bmod N] = F(u\Delta\xi, v\Delta\xi)\, e^{\pi i (u+v)/N}\,\Delta\xi^2$ for $u, v = -N/2, \ldots, N/2 - 1$, as in line L7. Let $g = N^2\,\mathrm{IDFT2}_N(G)$, as in line L8. Then for each pixel $(i, j)$, with $m_x = j - n/2$ and $m_y = n/2 - 1 - i$,
$$g[m_y \bmod N][m_x \bmod N] = \sum_{u, v = -N/2}^{N/2-1} F(u\Delta\xi, v\Delta\xi)\, e^{2\pi i (x_j u + y_i v)\Delta\xi}\,\Delta\xi^2.$$

*Proof.* By the definition of the inverse 2D DFT, $g[r][c] = \sum_{v', u'=0}^{N-1} G[v'][u']\, e^{2\pi i (v' r + u' c)/N}$. Each pair $(u', v')$ is $(u \bmod N, v \bmod N)$ for exactly one pair $(u, v)$ in the centered range, and $e^{2\pi i u' c/N} = e^{2\pi i u c/N}$ because the two exponents differ by a multiple of $2\pi i$. The same holds for $r$ in place of $m_y \bmod N$. So
$$g[m_y \bmod N][m_x \bmod N] = \sum_{u, v} F(u\Delta\xi, v\Delta\xi)\,\Delta\xi^2\, e^{2\pi i ((m_x + 1/2)u + (m_y + 1/2)v)/N}.$$
Now $x_j = -1 + (2j+1)/n = (m_x + 1/2)h$ and $y_i = 1 - (2i+1)/n = (m_y + 1/2)h$, and $h\Delta\xi = 1/N$. So $(m_x + 1/2)u/N = x_j u \Delta\xi$ and $(m_y + 1/2)v/N = y_i v\Delta\xi$. $\square$

Line L9 takes the real part. For a real $f$ the full lattice sum is real, because $F(-\xi) = \overline{F(\xi)}$. The truncated sum of Lemma 2 is real as well when the spectrum array is Hermitian; the interpolation of lines L5 and L6 keeps this property, because the value at $-\xi$ is read from the same samples at the same weights with the radius negated, and $S_k$ of a real projection satisfies $S_k[-l] = \overline{S_k[l]}$. The real part then removes only rounding errors.

**Lemma 3 (the lattice sum is the image).** Let $f$ be continuous with support in $[-1, 1]^2$, and assume that $\sum_{u, v \in \mathbb{Z}} |\mathcal{F}f(u\Delta\xi, v\Delta\xi)| < \infty$. If $1/\Delta\xi > 2$, then for every $x \in [-1, 1]^2$
$$\sum_{u, v \in \mathbb{Z}} \mathcal{F}f(u\Delta\xi, v\Delta\xi)\, e^{2\pi i x\cdot(u, v)\Delta\xi}\,\Delta\xi^2 = f(x).$$

*Proof.* Let $T = 1/\Delta\xi$ and let $f_T(x) = \sum_{q \in \mathbb{Z}^2} f(x + qT)$ be the periodization of $f$ with period $T$ in each coordinate. Since $T > 2$, at most one term is nonzero at each point, so $f_T$ is continuous. Its Fourier coefficient for the frequency $(u, v)/T$ is $T^{-2}\int_{[0,T]^2} f_T(x)\, e^{-2\pi i x\cdot(u,v)/T}\,dx = \Delta\xi^2\,\mathcal{F}f(u\Delta\xi, v\Delta\xi)$. By assumption these coefficients are absolutely summable, so the Fourier series of $f_T$ converges uniformly to $f_T$. For $x \in [-1, 1]^2$ and $q \ne 0$, the point $x + qT$ has a coordinate of absolute value more than $T - 1 > 1$, so $f(x + qT) = 0$ and $f_T(x) = f(x)$. $\square$

With $N = 2n$ the period is $T = Nh = 4$, so the copies of the image in Lemma 3 do not overlap the square $[-1, 1]^2$. With $N = n$ the period would be 2, and the copies of an object that fills the square would touch it.

**The errors that remain.** Lemmas 1 to 3 show that the output equals $f$ at the pixel centers when three conditions hold: the projections are band-limited and zero outside the detector (Lemma 1), the spectrum outside the box $|u|, |v| < N/2$ is zero (Lemma 2 sums only over the box), and the values on the Cartesian lattice are exact. Lines L5 and L6 do not give exact values: they interpolate bilinearly in the angle and the signed radius between the four nearest polar samples. On a polar grid the angular step at the radius $\rho$ is $\pi\rho/a$, so the interpolation error grows with the frequency. For a radial spectrum, such as that of a centered Gaussian, the angular part of the interpolation is exact and only the radial part remains. An object with sharp edges has a spectrum that oscillates in the angle as well as in the radius, and the interpolation error then spreads over the whole image, because one wrong value in the Fourier plane changes every pixel. This is the reason for the gridding methods of the variants.

**The interpolation step is well defined.** Line L5 maps $\xi = \rho(\cos\phi, \sin\phi)$ with $\phi \in (-\pi, \pi]$ to $(\theta, \sigma) = (\phi, \rho)$ for $\phi \in [0, \pi)$ and to $(\phi + \pi, -\rho)$ for $\phi < 0$, and $\phi = \pi$ goes to $(0, -\rho)$. In each case $\theta \in [0, \pi)$ and $\sigma\,\omega(\theta) = \xi$. The upper angle neighbor of $\theta \in ((a-1)\pi/a, \pi)$ is the angle $\pi$, which is not a row of the sinogram. Line L6 reads it as the row of the angle 0 at the radius $-\sigma$, because $\sigma\,\omega(\pi) = -\sigma\,\omega(0)$. So the interpolated value is continuous across the negative $x$ axis.
