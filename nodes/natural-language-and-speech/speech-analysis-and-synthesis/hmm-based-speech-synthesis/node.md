HMM-based speech synthesis is the classic method of statistical parametric [text-to-speech](/text-to-speech/). It does not store speech to replay, as [unit selection](/unit-selection-synthesis/) does. It stores a statistical model of speech parameters. Each phone in its context has a small left-to-right [hidden Markov model](/hidden-markov-model/), whose states give Gaussian distributions for the spectrum, the pitch and their [delta features](/delta-features/), plus a Gaussian for the duration of each state. There are far too many contexts to train each one, so [decision trees](/phonetic-decision-tree-state-tying/) cluster them, separately for each kind of parameter. To speak a sentence, the system builds the chain of state models for its context labels, chooses how many frames each state lasts, generates the smooth parameter trajectory of highest likelihood with [maximum likelihood parameter generation](/maximum-likelihood-parameter-generation/) (MLPG), and drives a vocoder with it. The HTS toolkit, built on HTK, is the reference implementation, which is why the method is often called HTS.

## In plain words

Record a speaker and measure, for every short frame, the shape of the sound and its pitch. Then learn a simple average model of how each speech sound behaves, given the sounds around it, the stress and the place in the sentence. Each sound is split into a few parts, and each part stores an average, a spread, and how long it usually lasts.

To say a new sentence, look up the parts for each sound, decide how many frames each part lasts, and draw the most likely smooth path of sound shapes and pitch through them. A vocoder, a simple model of the voice, turns that path into audio.

The voice is small, smooth and easy to change, for example to a new speaker with little data. But the averaging makes it sound muffled next to real recordings.

## The problem

**Input.** A text, turned by a front end into a sequence of full-context labels, one per phone. A label holds the phone, its neighbors, its position in the syllable, word and phrase, stress, part of speech and similar facts. The trained model has, for each of the $S = 5$ emitting states of a phone model, a decision tree for each stream: mel-cepstral coefficients with their deltas, $\log F_0$ with its deltas, and the state duration. Each leaf of a tree holds a Gaussian.

**Output.** A waveform whose parameters, frame by frame, have high likelihood under the model for the given labels.

**Training, in short.** Extract mel-cepstral coefficients and $\log F_0$ at a fixed frame rate, add delta and delta-delta features, and train the context-dependent HMMs with the [Baum-Welch algorithm](/baum-welch-algorithm/). Cluster the states by decision trees grown with questions about the context, separately for each stream, until a stopping rule such as minimum description length says stop. Fit the duration Gaussians from the state occupancies. $F_0$ is not defined in unvoiced frames, so the pitch stream uses a multi-space distribution: a weight for voiced and unvoiced, and a Gaussian for $\log F_0$ in the voiced space.

## The algorithm

```pseudo id=main
HMM-SYNTHESIZE(text, trees, rho)
L1  labels <- FRONT-END(text)                      // one full-context label per phone
L2  for each label l, for each state k = 1 .. 5 of its phone model do
L3      (m, v) <- duration Gaussian of the leaf of the duration tree for (l, k)
L4      G[k] <- leaves of the spectrum and F0 trees for (l, k)
L5      d <- max(1, round(m + rho * v))            // rho = 0 gives the mean durations
L6      append d copies of G[k] to the frame sequence
L7  voiced <- frames whose voiced weight is above 1/2
L8  c <- MLPG(means and variances of the spectrum stream, static, delta and delta-delta windows)
L9  f <- MLPG(the same for log F0, over each voiced region)
L10 excitation <- pulses at F0 = exp(f) in voiced frames, white noise in unvoiced frames
L11 return VOCODER-FILTER(excitation, c)          // for example an MLSA filter
```

Line L5 sets the duration of each state. If the total length $T$ of the utterance is given, the choice $\rho = (T - \sum_k m_k) / \sum_k v_k$ over all states of the utterance gives the durations of highest likelihood with that total; $\rho$ then works as a speaking rate control. Lines L8 and L9 are MLPG, one banded linear solve per parameter dimension. Line L11 is a [source-filter](/source-filter-model/) vocoder: the excitation is the source and the mel-cepstrum sets the filter.

## Example

**Durations.** Take three states with duration means $m = (3, 5, 2)$ frames and variances $v = (1, 4, 1)$. With $\rho = 0$ the durations are the means, $(3, 5, 2)$, 10 frames. For a slower rendering with $T = 16$ frames, $\rho = (16 - 10) / (1 + 4 + 1) = 1$ and the durations are $(3 + 1, 5 + 4, 2 + 1) = (4, 9, 3)$, which sum to 16. The state with the largest variance takes most of the extra time, because stretching it costs the least likelihood. For $T = 7$, $\rho = -3 / 6 = -0.5$ and the durations are $(2.5, 3, 1.5)$ before rounding.

**Trajectory.** With the durations fixed, each frame has the Gaussians of its state. If two states of 3 frames each have static means 0 and 1, static variance 1 and a delta variance of 1/4 with zero delta means, the frame-wise means form a step $(0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1)$. MLPG turns this into the smooth curve $(0.2, 0.3, 0.3, 0.7, 0.7, 0.8)$, as worked out on the [MLPG page](/maximum-likelihood-parameter-generation/).

**A toy.** The code with this page is a toy, not HTS. It runs lines L1 to L11 on one-dimensional streams with hand-made trees: a phone list as the front end, durations by line L5, MLPG solved by dense elimination, and a pulse or noise source through a first-order all-pole filter as the vocoder. It trains nothing.

## Correctness

The whole system has no single correct output; its quality is measured by listening tests. Three of its steps are exact for the model.

**Duration choice (theorem).** Treat the state durations as real numbers $d_1, \ldots, d_N$ with Gaussian log-likelihood $\sum_k \log \mathcal{N}(d_k; m_k, v_k)$ and the constraint $\sum_k d_k = T$. The objective is strictly concave, so a stationary point of the Lagrangian $\sum_k -\frac{(d_k - m_k)^2}{2 v_k} + \rho (\sum_k d_k - T)$ is the unique maximum. Setting the derivative to 0 gives $d_k = m_k + \rho v_k$, and the constraint gives $\rho = (T - \sum_k m_k) / \sum_k v_k$. Rounding to integers and the lower bound of 1 frame on line L5 make the result approximate.

**Parameter generation (theorem).** For the fixed state sequence, lines L8 and L9 return the trajectory of highest likelihood under the static and delta Gaussians, by the proof on the [MLPG page](/maximum-likelihood-parameter-generation/).

**Training (theorem).** Each Baum-Welch iteration does not lower the training likelihood, as for any [EM](/expectation-maximization/) algorithm. Decision tree clustering is greedy and gives no optimality guarantee.

The step from durations to trajectory is a heuristic. The best pair of a state sequence and a trajectory, and the best trajectory over all state sequences, are harder problems; the fixed durations of line L5 replace them.

## Complexity

The tree lookups of lines L3 and L4 cost $O(h)$ for each of the $N$ states, for each of the three streams. Line L6 writes $T$ frames. MLPG solves one banded system for each of the $M$ static dimensions in $O(T)$ time for fixed window widths, so lines L8 and L9 cost $O(T M)$. The vocoder filter of line L11 costs a constant per sample for each cepstral coefficient, so it is linear in the length of the audio. The total is $O(N h + T M)$ plus the vocoder, and the memory is $\Theta(T M)$ for the frame means, variances and outputs.

A voice is small: one Gaussian per leaf of each tree, against hours of stored audio for a unit selection voice.

## Properties

- **Deterministic.** For a given text and $\rho$ the output is fixed. The model can be sampled, but the mean trajectory sounds better.
- **Smooth.** The delta features and MLPG remove the jumps at state boundaries.
- **Over-smoothed.** Averaging in training and the mean trajectory in synthesis lower the variance of the generated parameters below that of natural speech. This is the main reason for the muffled sound.
- **Flexible.** Every part of the voice is a parameter, so speaker adaptation, speaking rate, pitch shifts and interpolation between voices are linear operations on means and variances.
- **Robust to sparse data.** Context clustering shares data among similar contexts, so unseen contexts still get a model.

## Variants and improvements

- **Hidden semi-Markov models.** Training the state durations inside the model, as a [hidden semi-Markov model](/hidden-semi-markov-model/), in place of fitting them afterward from the HMM occupancies.
- **Global variance.** Adding a term to the generation objective that keeps the variance of each generated parameter over the utterance close to that of natural speech, which reduces over-smoothing at the cost of an iterative solve.
- **Better vocoders.** Mixed excitation and spectral analysis such as STRAIGHT in place of the simple pulse or noise source.
- **Speaker adaptation.** Moving the Gaussians of an average voice toward a new speaker with a few minutes of data, with linear transforms such as [MLLR](/maximum-likelihood-linear-regression/).
- **Neural acoustic models.** Replacing the decision trees by a neural network that maps context features to the frame-wise means directly, with MLPG kept as the last step. Later, end-to-end models such as [FastSpeech](/fastspeech/) kept the duration idea and dropped the HMMs.

## When to use it

Use it where the voice must be small, fast and controllable, and where some loss of naturalness is acceptable: embedded devices, many voices from little data, or research on prosody and adaptation where every parameter must be visible. Where naturalness matters most, neural acoustic models and vocoders such as [WaveNet](/wavenet/) are better in most published tests, and unit selection is better for a large single-speaker database in a narrow domain.

## Pitfalls

- **Bad alignments in training.** Errors in the labels or the forced alignment put frames in the wrong states and blur the models. Check the labels against the audio.
- **Unvoiced frames.** $\log F_0$ is undefined in unvoiced frames. Interpolating it, or using a single Gaussian over both spaces, gives wrong pitch at voicing boundaries; use the multi-space distribution and run MLPG over each voiced region alone.
- **Rounding of durations.** Rounding each state alone can change the total length by up to half a frame per state. If the total matters, round the cumulative sums.
- **Zero or tiny variances.** A leaf with very few frames gets a tiny variance and dominates the weighted least squares. Floor the variances.
- **Expecting the mean to sound natural.** The trajectory of highest likelihood is smoother than any real utterance. Without global variance or a similar fix, the voice sounds muffled even when the models are accurate.

## History

Tokuda and colleagues worked on generating speech parameters from HMMs with dynamic features in the 1990s. Yoshimura, Tokuda, Masuko, Kobayashi and Kitamura (1999) put spectrum, pitch and duration into one framework of context-dependent HMMs clustered by decision trees. Tokuda and colleagues (2000) gave the parameter generation algorithms used at synthesis time. The HTS toolkit made the method widely available. The survey of Zen, Tokuda and Black (2009) compares it with unit selection and describes the remaining problems: the vocoder, the accuracy of the acoustic models, and over-smoothing. Later, neural networks replaced the decision trees as the acoustic model, and then neural vocoders such as [WaveNet](/wavenet/) (2016) and end-to-end models replaced most of the pipeline.
