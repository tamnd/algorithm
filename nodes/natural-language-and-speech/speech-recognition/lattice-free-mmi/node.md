Lattice-free MMI (LF-MMI) trains the neural network of an HMM-based recognizer directly with the [MMI](/maximum-mutual-information-training/) criterion, from a random start, with no cross-entropy training first. Classic MMI needs a word lattice of competing hypotheses for every utterance, generated by an earlier model. LF-MMI replaces all those lattices by one small graph: a phone n-gram language model composed with the HMM topology. The sum over all its paths is the denominator, for every utterance alike. The forward-backward pass over this graph runs on the GPU on short chunks of audio, in probability space with a scale per frame. Together with a topology that can be traversed in one frame and a frame rate reduced 3-fold, this makes sequence training about as cheap as frame-level training.

## In plain words

To train a recognizer with MMI, you compare the score of the right phone sequence with the total score of all phone sequences. The old way listed the likely wrong sentences for each recording, which was slow and needed an older recognizer.

LF-MMI does not list them. It builds one small network of phones, where each phone can follow another with a probability learned from the training transcripts. Every phone sequence is a path through this network. For each short piece of audio, a fast pass over the network adds up the scores of all its paths. The training then pushes up the phone units of the right sequence and pushes down the units that the network as a whole prefers. Because the network is small and the same for every recording, the work runs well on a GPU.

## The problem

The network reads the acoustic features and outputs, for each output frame $t = 1, \ldots, T$ and each pdf $p$ (a tied HMM state), a number $y_t(p)$. LF-MMI uses $y_t(p)$ directly as a log likelihood: there is no division by the pdf priors and no acoustic scale. A training example is a chunk of audio with its phone sequence. The objective of one chunk is

$$F = \ln Z_{\mathrm{num}} - \ln Z_{\mathrm{den}} - \frac{c}{2} \sum_{t,p} y_t(p)^2,$$

where $Z_G$ sums, over the state paths of the graph $G$, the product of the graph weights and $e^{y_t(\mathrm{pdf}(q_t))}$. The numerator graph holds the paths of the reference phone sequence, and the denominator graph the paths of all phone sequences. The last term is an l2 penalty on the outputs. The task is to compute $F$ and $\partial F / \partial y_t(p)$, which the network then backpropagates.

**The topology.** Each phone has a first state, entered once, and a loop state with a different pdf that may repeat. A phone can therefore take a single frame. The output frame rate is one third of the usual 100 frames per second.

**The denominator graph.** A phone language model, estimated from the phone alignments of the training data, is composed with the topology; every transition of the topology has probability 0.5. The initial probabilities come from running the graph for 100 steps from its start state and averaging the state distributions, so that a chunk may begin anywhere in an utterance. Every final probability is 1. The leaky HMM adds, at every frame, a small probability $\eta$ of jumping from any state to any state $j$ in proportion to $\mathrm{init}(j)$, which lets a chunk recover from a bad start.

**The numerator graph.** It is the topology of the reference phone sequence, with each phone allowed to move up to 50 ms from its position in a reference alignment. In the code its weights are those of the same states and arcs of the denominator, so every numerator path is a denominator path and $F \le 0$.

## The algorithm

$\oplus$ is log_add. All logs are natural logs. The numerator is small and uses log space. The denominator uses probabilities, divided at each frame by their sum $c_t$, as on a GPU.

```pseudo id=main
LF-MMI-GRADIENT(y, G_num, G_den, η, c)   ▷ y: T × P network outputs
L1  (ln Z_num, γ^num) ← log-space forward-backward on G_num, with y = -∞ outside each phone's window
L2  m_t ← max over p of y_t(p);  e_t(j) ← exp(y_t(pdf j) - m_t) for each state j of G_den
L3  a(j) ← init(j) e_1(j) at t = 1;  a(j) ← (Σ over arcs i→j of α̂_{t-1}(i) w(i,j) + η init(j) Σ_i α̂_{t-1}(i)) e_t(j) for t ≥ 2
L4  c_t ← Σ_j a(j);  α̂_t ← a / c_t
L5  ln Z_den ← Σ_t (ln c_t + m_t) + ln Σ_j α̂_T(j) final(j)
L6  β̂_T(j) ← final(j);  β̂_t(i) ← (Σ over arcs i→j of w(i,j) e_{t+1}(j) β̂_{t+1}(j) + η Σ_j init(j) e_{t+1}(j) β̂_{t+1}(j)) / c_{t+1}
L7  γ^den_t(p) ← Σ over states j with pdf j = p of α̂_t(j) β̂_t(j) / Σ_j α̂_T(j) final(j)
L8  F ← ln Z_num - ln Z_den - (c/2) Σ y²;  g_t(p) ← γ^num_t(p) - γ^den_t(p) - c y_t(p)
L9  return F and g
```

The leaky term in lines L3 and L6 costs $O(N)$ per frame, the same as an arc from every state to every state would cost with a single shared target distribution. Povey et al. (2016) also add a second output layer trained with cross-entropy against the numerator posteriors, with weight 0.1, which this page does not treat further.

## Example

Take two phones a and b, with pdfs a1, a2 (first and loop state of a) and b1, b2. The phone bigram is uniform: each phone follows each phone with probability 0.5, and a chunk starts with a or b with probability 0.5 each. From either state of a phone, the graph stays in its loop state with probability 0.5, or moves to the first state of a phone $x$ with $0.5 \cdot 0.5 = 0.25$.

**Initial probabilities.** At step 0, the first states have 0.5 each. From step 1 on, each first state gets $0.25$ (a quarter of the total mass) and each loop state gets $0.5 \cdot 0.5 = 0.25$. Averaged over 100 steps, $\mathrm{init} = (0.2525, 0.2475, 0.2525, 0.2475)$ for (a1, a2, b1, b2).

**One chunk.** Let $T = 3$, the reference "a b", and $e^{y_t}$ as in this table:

| frame | a1 | a2 | b1 | b2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.6 | 0.1 | 0.2 | 0.1 |
| 2 | 0.2 | 0.3 | 0.4 | 0.1 |
| 3 | 0.1 | 0.1 | 0.3 | 0.5 |

The numerator has two paths in 3 frames: a1 b1 b2 and a1 a2 b1. Both have the graph weight $0.2525 \cdot 0.25 \cdot 0.5$, and the outputs give $0.6 \cdot 0.4 \cdot 0.5 = 0.12$ and $0.6 \cdot 0.3 \cdot 0.3 = 0.054$. So $Z_{\mathrm{num}} = 0.2525 \cdot 0.125 \cdot 0.174 = 0.005491875$, and at frame 2 the numerator posterior is 0.690 for b1 and 0.310 for a2. The denominator sums over all paths: $Z_{\mathrm{den}} = 0.01597125$ with $\eta = 0$, so $F = \ln(0.005491875 / 0.01597125) = -1.067521$. With $\eta = 0.1$, $Z_{\mathrm{den}} = 0.019400699$ and $F = -1.262039$. At frame 1, with $\eta = 0$, the gradient is $(+0.3834, -0.1007, -0.1897, -0.0930)$: the numerator is surely in a1, while the denominator puts 0.6166 there. The tests check these numbers.

## Correctness

The proof is in [proofs/correctness.md](proofs/correctness.md). By induction on $t$, the scaled forward values $\hat\alpha_t$ equal the true forward sums divided by $\prod_{s \le t} c_s e^{m_s}$, and the scaled backward values equal the true ones divided by $\prod_{s > t} c_s e^{m_s}$. So line L5 is $\ln Z_{\mathrm{den}}$, and in line L7 the scale factors cancel and give the exact posteriors. The leaky jumps are arcs whose weights do not depend on $y$, so they change $Z$ but not the form of the gradient. The derivative of $\ln Z$ with respect to $y_t(p)$ is the posterior of pdf $p$ at frame $t$, which gives line L8 (a theorem). When the numerator paths are denominator paths, $F \le 0$.

## Complexity

Each frame visits every state and arc of the denominator graph once, forward and backward, and line L7 adds $O(N + P)$, so a chunk costs $\Theta(T (N + E + P))$ operations; the numerator graph is much smaller. Space is $\Theta(T N)$ for the scaled tables and $\Theta(T P)$ for the posteriors. On Switchboard, Povey et al. (2016) report a denominator graph of about 24 thousand states and 220 thousand arcs. Its size is the same for every chunk, and the work for many chunks of 1.5 s runs in parallel on a GPU without log arithmetic.

## Properties

- **Exact for the given graphs.** The objective and gradient are exact; the approximations are in the choice of the graphs (a phone language model instead of words, chunks instead of utterances).
- **No lattices.** The denominator does not depend on an earlier model, so there is no lattice generation and no stale lattice.
- **No prior division and no acoustic scale.** The outputs are trained to be used as they are in decoding.
- **Sequence training from scratch.** No cross-entropy model is needed first, although the cross-entropy output layer is kept as a regularizer.
- **Low frame rate.** With one output every 30 ms and a topology that can be traversed in one frame, the network output layer and the decoder handle a third of the frames that a 10 ms rate needs.

## Variants and improvements

- **Phone language model order.** Povey et al. (2016) use a 4-gram, with no smoothing or pruning at and below the trigram level, keep 2000 4-gram states, and allow no backoff to lower orders. The code uses a bigram.
- **Regularization.** Their recipe combines the cross-entropy output layer (weight 0.1), the l2 penalty on the outputs ($c = 0.0005$) and the leaky HMM ($\eta = 0.1$).
- **LF-MMI followed by sMBR.** A pass of lattice-based state-level minimum Bayes risk training after LF-MMI gave a further small gain on Switchboard.
- **End-to-end LF-MMI.** Later work removed the alignments and the decision tree and trained with a full numerator graph, closer to [CTC](/connectionist-temporal-classification/).

## When to use it

Use it to train the acoustic model of a hybrid HMM recognizer with a decoding graph such as [HCLG](/hclg-decoding-graph/). On the Switchboard Hub5'00 set, Povey et al. (2016) report word error rates of 18.2% (cross-entropy), 16.9% (cross-entropy then sMBR), 15.5% (LF-MMI) and 15.1% (LF-MMI then sMBR), with 12.5%, 11.4%, 10.2% and 10.0% on its Switchboard part. That is about 11.5% relative better than cross-entropy and 8% better than cross-entropy followed by sMBR. Their Table 4 gives 4.28% on LibriSpeech and 11.2% on TED-LIUM.

## Pitfalls

- **Underflow.** The denominator recursion must be scaled at every frame (or run in log space). A product of hundreds of output probabilities underflows.
- **Chunk boundaries.** A chunk starts in the middle of an utterance. Without the averaged initial probabilities and the final probabilities of 1, the first and last frames are forced into wrong states.
- **Over-fitting.** Without the regularizers the outputs grow and the model over-fits; the l2 penalty and the cross-entropy output keep them in range.
- **Mismatched frame rates.** The numerator windows, the topology and the decoding graph must all use the reduced frame rate.
- **Using the cross-entropy output in decoding.** Decode with the chain output, not the regularizing one.

## Implementations

The code takes the network outputs as given and computes the LF-MMI objective and gradient for small graphs, with a phone bigram, the averaged initial probabilities, the leaky HMM and the scaled denominator recursion; it does not train a network. The tests compare both recursions with a sum over all paths, the gradient with finite differences, and the scaled pass with the log-space pass on long chunks.

## History

MMI training with lattices became practical for large vocabulary recognition with Woodland and Povey (2002), and was later used to train the networks of hybrid models after cross-entropy training. Povey, Peddinti, Galvez, Ghahremani, Manohar, Na, Wang and Khudanpur (2016) removed the lattices by using a phone language model graph as the denominator, computed it on the GPU, and trained from scratch. The method is in the Kaldi toolkit under the name chain models.
