IBM Model 2 is the second of the translation models of Brown et al. (1993). It keeps the word translation table $t(f \mid e)$ of [IBM Model 1](/ibm-model-1/) and replaces the uniform choice of the aligned position with an alignment distribution $a(i \mid j, l, m)$: the probability that source word $j$ is generated by target word $i$, given the two sentence lengths. So the model can learn that words near the same relative position tend to be translations, which Model 1 cannot. The sum over all alignments still factors, so [EM](/expectation-maximization/) costs $\Theta(l m)$ per sentence pair. Unlike Model 1, the log likelihood is not concave, and the result depends on the start, which is usually the trained Model 1 table.

## The machine

**Variables.** A pair is a source sentence $f = f_1 \ldots f_m$ and a target sentence $e = e_1 \ldots e_l$, with the null word $e_0$. An alignment is $a = a_1 \ldots a_m$ with $a_j \in \{0, \ldots, l\}$: source word $j$ is generated by target word $a_j$. The model is written in the direction $p(f \mid e)$ of Brown et al. The reverse direction is a different model and gives different alignments.

**Parameters.**

- $t(f \mid e)$, with $\sum_f t(f \mid e) = 1$ for each target word $e$ and for the null word;
- $a(i \mid j, l, m)$ for $0 \le i \le l$ and $1 \le j \le m$, with $\sum_{i=0}^{l} a(i \mid j, l, m) = 1$ for each $(j, l, m)$;
- a length distribution $\varepsilon = p(m \mid e)$, a constant for EM.

**How it makes a sentence.** Given $e$, draw $m$; for each $j$, draw $a_j$ from $a(\cdot \mid j, l, m)$ and then $f_j$ from $t(\cdot \mid e_{a_j})$. So

$$p(f, a \mid e) = \varepsilon \prod_{j=1}^{m} a(a_j \mid j, l, m) \, t(f_j \mid e_{a_j}).$$

**Standing assumptions.** The positions $a_1, \ldots, a_m$ are independent of each other given the lengths. Each $a_j$ depends on $j$, $l$ and $m$ only, not on the words. Each $f_j$ depends only on $e_{a_j}$. With $a(i \mid j, l, m) = 1/(l + 1)$ the model is Model 1.

**Training.** EM is the Model 1 algorithm with one more table of counts.

```pseudo id=main
IBM-MODEL-2-EM(corpus, rounds)
L1  t ← the table of Model 1 after a few rounds; a(i | j, l, m) ← 1 / (l + 1)
L2  repeat rounds times
L3      c(f, e) ← 0 and c(i | j, l, m) ← 0 for all arguments
L4      for each pair (f, e) in the corpus and each j ← 1 to m do
L5          Z ← Σ_{i=0..l} a(i | j, l, m) t(f_j | e_i)
L6          for i ← 0 to l do p ← a(i | j, l, m) t(f_j | e_i) / Z; c(f_j, e_i) += p; c(i | j, l, m) += p
L7      t(f | e) ← c(f, e) / Σ_f' c(f', e); a(i | j, l, m) ← c(i | j, l, m) / Σ_i' c(i' | j, l, m)
L8  return t and a
```

Line L5 is the normalizer of the posterior of $a_j$, and line L6 adds that posterior to both tables of counts. Line L7 is the M step: each table is normalized over its own outcome. A shape $(j, l, m)$ that does not occur in the corpus keeps its row of $a$; the code keeps the old values for it.

**Example.** On the three pairs of the Model 1 page (the house | das Haus, the book | das Buch, a book | ein Buch, no null word), start from the Model 1 table after two rounds and a uniform $a$. All three pairs have $l = m = 2$, so only the four values $a(i \mid j, 2, 2)$ are used, and by symmetry $a(1 \mid 1, 2, 2) = a(2 \mid 2, 2, 2)$.

| round | a(1 \| 1, 2, 2) | t(the \| das) | t(house \| Haus) | likelihood |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1/2 | 7/11 = 0.6364 | 4/7 = 0.5714 | 0.006730 |
| 1 | 45671/64206 = 0.7113 | 0.7479 | 0.6534 | 0.032437 |
| 2 | 0.9016 | 0.9286 | 0.8538 | 0.306533 |
| 3 | 0.9914 | 0.9956 | 0.9833 | 0.902577 |

Round 1 moves $t$ exactly as round 3 of Model 1 does, because $a$ is still uniform during its E step. After that, the alignment table learns that the first word goes with the first word and the second with the second, and the likelihood grows towards 1. Model 1 cannot get near 1 on this corpus. Under Model 1 the two source words of a pair use the same two rows of $t$ with equal weights, so the two per-word sums add up to at most 2, each pair has probability at most $1/4$, and the corpus at most $1/64 = 0.0156$.

## What it counts

The probability of a pair is

$$p(f \mid e) = \varepsilon \prod_{j=1}^{m} \sum_{i=0}^{l} a(i \mid j, l, m) \, t(f_j \mid e_i),$$

because each factor of $p(f, a \mid e)$ depends on one $a_j$ only, and the sum over the $(l + 1)^m$ alignments factors by the distributive law. The posterior of each link is $p(a_j = i \mid f, e) = a(i \mid j, l, m) \, t(f_j \mid e_i) / Z_j$, and the $a_j$ are independent given the pair. One step is one arithmetic operation on real numbers, as on the [real RAM](/real-ram/).

| Task | Cost per pair |
|---|---|
| $p(f \mid e)$ | $\Theta(l m)$ instead of $\Theta(m (l + 1)^m)$ |
| E step (lines L4 to L6) | $\Theta(l m)$ |
| best alignment | $\Theta(l m)$: the best $i$ for each $j$ alone |

The table $a$ has one row for every $(j, l, m)$ in the corpus. For sentences up to length $L$ that is $\Theta(L^4)$ numbers, and most of them are seen in few pairs. This is the weak point that [fast_align](/fast-align/) removes with one parameter. For long sentences the product over $j$ underflows; the code sums logarithms of the inner sums.

## Relation to other models

- **IBM Model 1.** Model 1 is Model 2 with $a(i \mid j, l, m) = 1/(l + 1)$ (Brown et al. 1993, Section 4.2).
- **fast_align.** [fast_align](/fast-align/) fixes the shape of $a$: a null probability $p_0$ and, for the other positions, weights $\exp(-\lambda |i/l - j/m|)$. One number $\lambda$ replaces the whole table, and its normalizer has a closed form.
- **HMM alignment model.** The [HMM alignment model](/hmm-alignment-model/) makes $a_j$ depend on $a_{j-1}$ instead of on $j$. This models the tendency of neighbouring words to move together, which Model 2 cannot express. Its E step is the forward-backward algorithm, $\Theta(l^2 m)$ per pair.
- **IBM Model 3.** [Model 3](/ibm-model-3/) adds fertilities and turns $a$ around into a distortion $d(j \mid i, l, m)$. Its E step does not factor, and Brown et al. use Model 2 to find the starting alignments for it.
- **Noisy channel.** Any of these models can serve as the translation model of the [noisy channel model of translation](/noisy-channel-translation-model/).

## Known results in the model

- **The sum factors (theorem).** The likelihood, the E step and the best alignment cost $\Theta(l m)$ per pair, for the same reason as in Model 1. The tests check the E step, the likelihood and the best alignment against all alignments on random small corpora, in exact rational arithmetic.
- **EM never decreases the likelihood (theorem).** The tests check this round by round.
- **The log likelihood is not concave (theorem, by example).** Take the single pair a b | x y without a null word. The diagonal solution ($t(a \mid x) = t(b \mid y) = 1$ and $a(1 \mid 1) = a(2 \mid 2) = 1$) and the crossed solution ($t(a \mid y) = t(b \mid x) = 1$ and $a(2 \mid 1) = a(1 \mid 2) = 1$) both give likelihood 1. Their midpoint, with every value $1/2$, gives $(1/2)^2 = 1/4$. A concave function cannot be lower at a midpoint than at both ends, so the log likelihood is not [concave](/convex-function/) in $(t, a)$ together. The product $a \cdot t$ in each term is the cause.
- **The start matters.** The midpoint above is a fixed point of EM. A small push of $t$ towards one solution sends EM to that solution: in the tests, a push of $\pm 0.05$ gives likelihood above 0.99 after 30 rounds, with the alignment a-x, b-y for one sign and a-y, b-x for the other. In practice $t$ starts from Model 1, whose likelihood is concave, and $a$ starts uniform (Brown et al. 1993).
- **Not deficient.** Every row of $a$ and of $t$ sums to 1, so for each length the model gives all its probability to real strings.
- **Measured quality.** Dyer et al. (2013, Table 2) report alignment error rates for Model 2, fast_align and Model 4 on French-English and Chinese-English test sets. Model 2 had the highest error rate of the three on both sets. These are measurements on their data.
