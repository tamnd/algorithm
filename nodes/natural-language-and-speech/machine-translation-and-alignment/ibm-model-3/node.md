IBM Model 3 is the third of the translation models of Brown et al. (1993). It adds two ideas to [IBM Model 2](/ibm-model-2/). Each target word $e_i$ has a *fertility* $\varphi_i$, the number of source words it generates, drawn from a table $n(\varphi \mid e_i)$. The positions of the generated words come from a *distortion* table $d(j \mid i, l, m)$. A null word $e_0$ adds source words that no target word explains. The model can express that one English word becomes two French words, which Models 1 and 2 can only do by chance. The price is that the sum over alignments no longer factors: [EM](/expectation-maximization/) needs a sum over all $(l + 1)^m$ alignments, and Brown et al. approximate it with a set of good alignments found by [hill climbing](/hill-climbing/). The model is also *deficient*: it gives some probability to outputs that are not strings.

## The machine

**Variables.** A pair is a source sentence $f = f_1 \ldots f_m$ and a target sentence $e = e_1 \ldots e_l$ with the null word $e_0$. An alignment is $a = a_1 \ldots a_m$ with $a_j \in \{0, \ldots, l\}$, in the direction $p(f \mid e)$ of Brown et al. The fertility of $e_i$ in $a$ is $\varphi_i = |\{j : a_j = i\}|$, and $\varphi_0$ is the number of source words aligned to the null word. The reverse direction is a different model with different alignments.

**Parameters.**

- $t(f \mid e)$, the word translation table, with a row for the null word;
- $n(\varphi \mid e)$, the fertility table, a distribution over $\varphi = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ for each target word;
- $d(j \mid i, l, m)$, the distortion table, a distribution over source positions $j = 1, \ldots, m$ for each $(i, l, m)$ with $i \ge 1$;
- $p_1$, with $p_0 = 1 - p_1$: after each word generated by a real target word, one null word is added with probability $p_1$.

**How it makes a sentence.** Given $e$:

1. for each $i = 1, \ldots, l$, draw $\varphi_i$ from $n(\cdot \mid e_i)$, and let $\varphi' = \varphi_1 + \cdots + \varphi_l$;
2. draw $\varphi_0$ from the binomial distribution with $\varphi'$ trials and success probability $p_1$, and set $m = \varphi' + \varphi_0$;
3. for each $i \ge 1$, draw $\varphi_i$ words from $t(\cdot \mid e_i)$, and draw $\varphi_0$ words from $t(\cdot \mid e_0)$;
4. for each word of each $e_i$ with $i \ge 1$, draw its position from $d(\cdot \mid i, l, m)$, independently;
5. put the null words into the $\varphi_0$ positions that are left, in one of the $\varphi_0!$ orders, each with the same probability.

Step 4 can put two words in the same position. Then the output is not a string, and the model has lost that probability.

**The probability of a pair and an alignment.** Summing over the orders inside each fertility group gives Equation 32 of Brown et al.:

$$p(f, a \mid e) = \binom{m - \varphi_0}{\varphi_0} p_0^{\,m - 2\varphi_0} p_1^{\,\varphi_0} \prod_{i=1}^{l} \varphi_i! \, n(\varphi_i \mid e_i) \prod_{j=1}^{m} t(f_j \mid e_{a_j}) \prod_{j : a_j \ne 0} d(j \mid a_j, l, m).$$

The factor $\varphi_i!$ counts the orders of the words of $e_i$ that give the same alignment. The binomial factor is 0 if $2 \varphi_0 > m$: there are at most as many null words as real ones.

**Training: the approximate E step.** The E step needs the posterior over all alignments, and no factoring is known. Brown et al. collect the counts over a set $S$ of alignments that hold most of the posterior mass.

```pseudo id=main
MODEL-3-E-STEP(f, e)
L1  a⁰ ← the best alignment of a simpler model (here: the best i for each j of t times d)
L2  a ← HILL-CLIMB(a⁰, no peg)
L3  S ← {a} ∪ N(a)
L4  for i ← 0 to l and j ← 1 to m do
L5      b ← HILL-CLIMB(a⁰ with a_j set to i, peg (i, j))
L6      S ← S ∪ {b} ∪ N(b)
L7  for each a' in S: w(a') ← p(f, a' | e)
L8  add w(a') / Σ_{a'' in S} w(a'') to the counts of t, n, d, p₀ and p₁ of every a' in S

HILL-CLIMB(a, peg)
L9  repeat
L10     b ← the best alignment in N(a) that keeps the peg, or a if none is better
L11     if b = a then return a
L12     a ← b
```

$N(a)$ is the *neighbourhood* of $a$: every alignment that differs from $a$ by one *move* (one $a_j$ changed) or one *swap* (the values of $a_j$ and $a_{j'}$ exchanged). A move changes two fertilities; a swap changes none. Line L5 is *pegging*: it fixes the link $(i, j)$ and climbs from there, so that for every link $S$ contains a good alignment that has it. The M step is the usual one: each table is normalized over its outcome, and $p_1 = c_1 / (c_0 + c_1)$, where $c_1$ is the expected number of null words and $c_0$ the expected value of $m - 2 \varphi_0$. Brown et al. start the climb from alignments of Model 2; the code starts from the best link for each source word under $t \cdot d$, which has the same role.

**Tie rule.** Line L10 replaces $a$ only on a strict increase, and among equal neighbours it keeps the first in the order moves, then swaps.

**Example.** Take $e = x$ with $n(2 \mid x) = 1$, no null words ($p_1 = 0$) and $d(1 \mid 1, 1, 2) = d(2 \mid 1, 1, 2) = 1/2$. The model always makes two words, and step 4 puts them in the same position with probability $1/2$. Summed over all alignments and all words, Equation 32 gives $1/2$, not 1: half of the probability is lost. In a larger case in the tests, with two target words, fertilities up to 2 and $p_1 = 1/4$, the lost part is $0.388$. The tests compare the sum of Equation 32 over every length and alignment with an independent enumeration of the five steps above, in exact rational arithmetic.

## What it counts

The model assigns $p(f \mid e) = \sum_a p(f, a \mid e)$, a sum over $(l + 1)^m$ alignments. One step is one arithmetic operation on real numbers, as on the [real RAM](/real-ram/).

| Task | Cost |
|---|---|
| $p(f, a \mid e)$ for one alignment, Equation 32 | $\Theta(l + m)$ |
| $p(f \mid e)$ or the exact E step, by enumeration | $\Theta((l + 1)^m (l + m))$ |
| neighbourhood $N(a)$ | $m l$ moves and at most $m (m - 1) / 2$ swaps |
| one climbing step of line L10 | $O(m (l + m))$ neighbours; with ratio updates each costs $O(1)$ |
| pegging, lines L4 to L6 | $(l + 1) m$ climbs |

No polynomial algorithm is known for the exact sum or for the best alignment. By the results of Udupa and Maji (2006) below, none exists for the best alignment unless P = NP, and none exists for the sum unless P = #P. The probability of one alignment of a long pair underflows in double precision; the code also gives $\ln p(f, a \mid e)$ as a sum of logarithms.

## Relation to other models

- **IBM Models 1 and 2.** [Model 1](/ibm-model-1/) and [Model 2](/ibm-model-2/) have no fertility. Their alignments are independent across $j$, so their sums factor. Brown et al. train Model 1, then Model 2, and use Model 2 to start Model 3.
- **IBM Models 4 and 5.** Model 4 replaces the distortion $d(j \mid i, l, m)$ with a distribution over the jump from the position of the previous group, which keeps groups of words together. Model 4 is still deficient. Model 5 places each word only in a vacant position, so it is not deficient (Brown et al. 1993, Sections 4.5 to 4.7).
- **HMM alignment model.** The [HMM alignment model](/hmm-alignment-model/) models jumps between neighbouring source words but has no fertility. Its E step is exact by forward-backward, so it is often used in place of Model 2 to start Models 3 and 4.
- **Search.** The approximate E step is [local search](/local-search/) on the alignments with the move and swap neighbourhood. It finds a local maximum, not always the best alignment.
- **Noisy channel.** Model 3 can serve as the translation model of the [noisy channel model of translation](/noisy-channel-translation-model/).

## Known results in the model

- **Equation 32 (theorem).** Summing the five steps over the $\varphi_i!$ orders that give the same alignment gives Equation 32 (Brown et al. 1993, Section 4.4). The tests check it against an enumeration of the steps.
- **Deficiency (theorem).** Models 3 and 4 give part of their probability to outputs with two words in one position, so $\sum_f p(f \mid e) < 1$ in general. Model 5 is not deficient (Brown et al. 1993, Section 4.5). The example above loses half of its probability.
- **The best alignment is hard (theorem).** Udupa and Maji (2006) prove that finding the Viterbi alignment of Models 3, 4 and 5 is [NP-hard](/np-hard/), and that computing the expectations of the E step and the conditional probability $p(f \mid e)$ is #P-complete. So the approximation of lines L1 to L8 is not a shortcut around an easy problem.
- **Hill climbing finds local maxima only (measurement).** In the tests, on 80 random pairs with $m \le 4$, $l \le 3$ and random parameters, hill climbing alone stopped below the best alignment 7 times. In all 80 cases the pegged set $S$ contained the best alignment. Pegging gives no such guarantee in general; this is a measurement on small random cases.
- **Approximate EM.** Because the counts come from $S$ and not from all alignments, the training is not EM in the exact sense, and the likelihood can decrease. With the exact E step, which the code also offers for small pairs, it is EM and the likelihood never decreases; the tests check this.
