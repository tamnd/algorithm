IBM Model 1 is the simplest of the five translation models of Brown et al. (1993). It gives the probability that a sentence $e = e_1 \ldots e_l$ of one language is translated as a sentence $f = f_1 \ldots f_m$ of another. Each source word $f_j$ picks a target position $a_j \in \{0, \ldots, l\}$ uniformly at random, where $e_0$ is the null word, and is then drawn from a word translation table $t(f_j \mid e_{a_j})$. The only parameters are the table $t$. The hidden choices $a = a_1 \ldots a_m$ are a [word alignment](/word-alignment/), and [EM](/expectation-maximization/) on a [parallel corpus](/parallel-corpus/) learns $t$ without any aligned data. The log likelihood is concave in $t$, so EM reaches a global maximum, but the maximizer need not be unique.

## The machine

**Variables.** A pair is a source sentence $f = f_1 \ldots f_m$ and a target sentence $e = e_1 \ldots e_l$, with the null word $e_0$ in front of $e$. An alignment is $a = a_1 \ldots a_m$ with $a_j \in \{0, \ldots, l\}$: source word $j$ is generated by target word $a_j$, and $a_j = 0$ means that it has no partner. This is the direction of Brown et al., who translate from French $f$ to English $e$ and model $p(f \mid e)$. The two directions give different alignments: in this direction every source word has exactly one link, but a target word can have many.

**Parameters.** For every target word $e$ (including the null word) and every source word $f$, the table holds $t(f \mid e) \ge 0$, with $\sum_f t(f \mid e) = 1$ for each $e$. A length distribution $\varepsilon = p(m \mid e)$ completes the model. It is a constant for EM, and the code leaves it out.

**How it makes a sentence.** Given $e$: draw the length $m$ with probability $\varepsilon$; for $j = 1, \ldots, m$ draw $a_j$ uniformly from $\{0, \ldots, l\}$, then draw $f_j$ from the row $t(\cdot \mid e_{a_j})$. So

$$p(f, a \mid e) = \frac{\varepsilon}{(l + 1)^m} \prod_{j=1}^{m} t(f_j \mid e_{a_j}).$$

**Standing assumptions.** The position $a_j$ does not depend on $j$, on $l$ beyond the uniform choice, or on the other positions. So the model ignores word order completely: if you permute the words of $f$ or of $e$, the probability of the pair does not change. Each $f_j$ depends only on the one word $e_{a_j}$.

**Training.** Given a corpus of pairs, EM alternates two steps. The E step finds the expected number of links between each source word and each target word, under the current $t$. The M step sets $t(f \mid e)$ to the expected count of $(f, e)$ divided by the expected count of $e$.

```pseudo id=main
IBM-MODEL-1-EM(corpus, rounds)
L1  t(f | e) ← 1 / |V_F| for every source word f and target word e
L2  repeat rounds times
L3      c(f, e) ← 0 for every pair of words
L4      for each pair (f, e) in the corpus and each j ← 1 to m do
L5          Z ← t(f_j | e_0) + t(f_j | e_1) + .. + t(f_j | e_l)
L6          for i ← 0 to l do c(f_j, e_i) ← c(f_j, e_i) + t(f_j | e_i) / Z
L7      for each target word e: t(f | e) ← c(f, e) / Σ_f' c(f', e)
L8  return t
```

Line L5 is the normalizer of the posterior of $a_j$. Line L6 adds that posterior to the counts, so each source word spreads exactly one unit of count over the $l + 1$ target words. Line L7 is the M step: it follows from the [Lagrange](/lagrange-multiplier/) condition for a maximum of the expected complete log likelihood with each row of $t$ summing to 1. A word pair that never occurs in the same sentence pair keeps a count of 0. If a row has no count at all, the code keeps the old row.

**Example.** Take the three pairs of Koehn (2010, Section 4.2): the house | das Haus, the book | das Buch, a book | ein Buch. Here the English words are generated from the German words, so the table is $t(\text{English} \mid \text{German})$. There is no null word, and $t$ starts uniform at $1/4$ over the 4 English words. The likelihood column is $\prod_j \frac{1}{l} \sum_i t(f_j \mid e_i)$ over the corpus, without $\varepsilon$.

| round | t(the \| das) | t(house \| Haus) | t(book \| Buch) | t(book \| ein) | likelihood |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1/4 | 1/4 | 1/4 | 1/4 | 1/4096 = 0.000244 |
| 1 | 1/2 | 1/2 | 1/2 | 1/2 | 81/16384 = 0.004944 |
| 2 | 7/11 | 4/7 | 7/11 | 3/7 | 0.006730 |
| 3 | 29435/39357 | 164/251 | 29435/39357 | 87/251 | 0.008697 |

In round 1, the word "the" occurs with "das" in two pairs and gets the full count 1 from them, while "house" and "book" get 1/2 each from "das", so $t(\text{the} \mid \text{das}) = 1 / (1 + 1/2 + 1/2) = 1/2$. In round 2 the other values of the "das" and "Buch" rows are 2/11 each, and $t(\text{a} \mid \text{ein}) = 4/7$. The likelihood after two rounds is $114511401 / 17014071844$ exactly. The table moves towards the word pairs the-das, house-Haus, book-Buch and a-ein, because "das" and "Buch" occur twice and explain "the" and "book" better than the words that occur once. The tests compute every value of this table in exact rational arithmetic.

## What it counts

The model assigns to a pair the probability

$$p(f \mid e) = \sum_a p(f, a \mid e) = \frac{\varepsilon}{(l + 1)^m} \prod_{j=1}^{m} \sum_{i=0}^{l} t(f_j \mid e_i).$$

The second form holds because the sum over the $(l + 1)^m$ alignments is a sum of products in which each factor depends on one $a_j$ only, so it factors by the distributive law. The same factoring gives the posterior of each link separately:

$$p(a_j = i \mid f, e) = \frac{t(f_j \mid e_i)}{\sum_{i'=0}^{l} t(f_j \mid e_{i'})},$$

and the positions $a_1, \ldots, a_m$ are independent given $f$ and $e$. The size of a pair is measured by $m$ and $l$, and one step is one arithmetic operation on real numbers, as on the [real RAM](/real-ram/).

| Task | Direct method | With the factoring |
|---|---|---|
| $p(f \mid e)$ | sum over $(l + 1)^m$ alignments, $\Theta(m (l + 1)^m)$ | product of $m$ sums, $\Theta(l m)$ |
| expected link counts (E step) | the same sum, once per link | lines L4 to L6, $\Theta(l m)$ |
| best alignment $\arg\max_a p(a \mid f, e)$ | maximum over $(l + 1)^m$ alignments | best $i$ for each $j$ alone, $\Theta(l m)$ |
| one EM round on a corpus | | $\Theta(\sum l m)$ over the pairs, plus the size of $t$ |

The product over $j$ underflows in double precision for long sentences: 200 words with a probability of $1/50$ each give $50^{-200}$, below the smallest double. The code therefore sums $\ln \sum_i t(f_j \mid e_i)$ for the log likelihood, which needs no [log-sum-exp](/log-sum-exp-trick/) because each inner sum has positive terms of moderate size.

## Relation to other models

- **Noisy channel.** Model 1 is the simplest translation model $p(f \mid e)$ of the [noisy channel model of translation](/noisy-channel-translation-model/). A language model $p(e)$ supplies the word order that Model 1 ignores.
- **IBM Model 2.** [Model 2](/ibm-model-2/) replaces the uniform $1/(l + 1)$ with an alignment distribution $a(i \mid j, l, m)$. Model 1 is the case of a uniform $a$. Model 2 is usually trained from the Model 1 table.
- **HMM alignment model.** The [HMM alignment model](/hmm-alignment-model/) makes $a_j$ depend on $a_{j-1}$ through the jump $a_j - a_{j-1}$. With all jumps equally likely, it is Model 1 again.
- **fast_align.** [fast_align](/fast-align/) is a Model 2 with one parameter $\lambda$ that pulls the links to the diagonal. With $\lambda = 0$ it is a Model 1 in which the null word has a fixed probability $p_0$.
- **IBM Model 3 and later.** [Model 3](/ibm-model-3/) adds a fertility for each target word. Its training starts from Models 1 and 2, because its own E step has no factoring.
- **Mixture models.** Each source word is drawn from an equal-weight mixture of the $l + 1$ rows $t(\cdot \mid e_i)$ of its sentence pair. EM for Model 1 is EM for the components of such mixtures, with the weights fixed and the components shared across the corpus, as in a [Gaussian mixture](/gaussian-mixture-model/) with discrete components and known weights.

## Known results in the model

- **The sum factors (theorem).** $\sum_a \prod_j t(f_j \mid e_{a_j}) = \prod_j \sum_i t(f_j \mid e_i)$, by the distributive law (Brown et al. 1993, Appendix B.3). So the likelihood, the E step and the best alignment each cost $\Theta(l m)$ per pair instead of a sum or maximum over $(l + 1)^m$ alignments. The tests check the E step and the likelihood against the sum over all alignments.
- **EM never decreases the likelihood (theorem).** This holds for every EM algorithm, and the tests check it round by round on random corpora.
- **The log likelihood is concave (theorem).** Up to the constant $\varepsilon$ terms, the log likelihood is $\sum_{\text{pairs}} \sum_j \ln \sum_i t(f_j \mid e_i) - m \ln(l + 1)$. Each inner sum is linear in $t$, the logarithm of a positive linear function is [concave](/convex-function/), and a sum of concave functions is concave. The set of tables whose rows sum to 1 is convex. So every local maximum is a global maximum, and the set of maximizers is convex. Brown et al. (1993, Appendix B) use this to argue that EM reaches the global maximum from any start with all $t$ positive.
- **The maximizer need not be unique.** The log likelihood is not strictly concave, so many tables can reach the same maximum, and they can imply different alignments (Toutanova and Galley 2011). This corrects the claim of Brown et al. that the maximum is unique. For the single pair a b | x y without a null word, every table with $t(a \mid x) = t(b \mid y) = p$ and $t(b \mid x) = t(a \mid y) = 1 - p$ gives the maximum likelihood $1/4$ and is a fixed point of EM. With $p = 4/5$ the best alignment is a-x, b-y, and with $p = 1/5$ it is a-y, b-x. So the alignment EM returns depends on the start.
- **Not deficient.** Model 1 gives all its probability to real strings: for each length $m$, $\sum_f p(f \mid e) = \varepsilon$. Models 3 and 4 do not have this property.
- **Order free.** The probability of a pair does not change under a permutation of the words of either sentence. The model therefore cannot tell a crossed alignment from a monotone one. Model 2, the HMM model and fast_align add this information.
