---
title: The Eisner algorithm returns a best projective dependency tree, and it terminates
proves: [correctness, termination]
status: full
---

The positions are $0, 1, \ldots, n$, with the root at 0, and $s(h, d)$ is the score of the arc $h \to d$. For $0 \le i \le j \le n$, a **right tree** on $[i, j]$ is a set of arcs that makes a projective tree on the positions $i, \ldots, j$ with root $i$; a **left tree** on $[i, j]$ is the same with root $j$, and it is allowed only when $i > 0$ or $i = j$, since no arc may enter position 0. A right tree on $[i, j]$ with $i < j$ is **incomplete** if it contains the arc $i \to j$, and a left tree is incomplete if it contains $j \to i$. The score of a set of arcs is the sum of their scores. Let $\mathcal{C}^{\to}[i, j]$, $\mathcal{C}^{\leftarrow}[i, j]$, $\mathcal{I}^{\to}[i, j]$ and $\mathcal{I}^{\leftarrow}[i, j]$ be the four sets of trees. The claim is that the tables `CR`, `CL`, `IR` and `IL` computed by lines L1 to L8 hold the largest score in the matching set, or "none" when the set is empty.

**Lemma 1 (yields are intervals).** In a projective tree on an interval $[i, j]$, the subtree of every position is an interval.

*Proof.* Let $k$ be a position and let $a < b < c$ with $a$ and $c$ in the subtree of $k$. Suppose $b$ is not in it. The path from $a$ up to $k$ and the path from $c$ up to $k$ stay in the subtree, so there is an arc of the tree whose endpoints lie on different sides of $b$ and both in the subtree of $k$: walk from $a$ to $c$ through $k$, and take the first arc that steps over $b$. The head of that arc is in the subtree of $k$, and by projectivity it dominates $b$, so $b$ is in the subtree of $k$, a contradiction. $\square$

**Lemma 2 (incomplete spans).** For $i < j$, a set $T$ is in $\mathcal{I}^{\to}[i, j]$ if and only if $T = A \cup B \cup \{i \to j\}$ for some $r$ with $i \le r < j$, $A \in \mathcal{C}^{\to}[i, r]$ and $B \in \mathcal{C}^{\leftarrow}[r + 1, j]$. The split $r$ and the parts are unique. The same holds for $\mathcal{I}^{\leftarrow}[i, j]$, $i > 0$, with the arc $j \to i$.

*Proof.* Let $T \in \mathcal{I}^{\to}[i, j]$. By Lemma 1 the subtree of $j$ is an interval; it contains $j$, does not contain $i$, and lies in $[i, j]$, so it is $[r + 1, j]$ for a unique $r$ with $i \le r < j$. The arcs inside it form a tree $B$ rooted at $j$ on $[r + 1, j]$. Removing $j$'s subtree and the arc $i \to j$ leaves a tree $A$ rooted at $i$ on $[i, r]$. Both are projective, because a subset of the arcs of a projective tree that forms a tree on an interval keeps every arc projective: the words between the ends of an arc are dominated by its head in $T$, and they lie in the same interval, so the chain to that head stays inside the part. Conversely, let $A$ and $B$ be as stated. Then $A \cup B \cup \{i \to j\}$ is a tree on $[i, j]$ rooted at $i$: every position has one head and the chain from each position reaches $i$. The arcs of $A$ and $B$ are still projective. The arc $i \to j$ is projective because every position strictly between $i$ and $j$ is in $A$ or in $B$ and so is dominated by $i$. Uniqueness: $r + 1$ is the left end of the subtree of $j$, which $T$ determines. The left case is the mirror image; the condition $i > 0$ keeps the arc $j \to i$ from entering the root. $\square$

**Lemma 3 (complete spans).** For $i < j$, a set $T$ is in $\mathcal{C}^{\to}[i, j]$ if and only if $T = A \cup B$ for some $r$ with $i < r \le j$, $A \in \mathcal{I}^{\to}[i, r]$ and $B \in \mathcal{C}^{\to}[r, j]$, with $r$ and the parts unique. For $\mathcal{C}^{\leftarrow}[i, j]$, the split is $i \le r < j$, $A \in \mathcal{C}^{\leftarrow}[i, r]$ and $B \in \mathcal{I}^{\leftarrow}[r, j]$.

*Proof.* Let $T \in \mathcal{C}^{\to}[i, j]$. The position $j$ is below $i$; let $r$ be the child of $i$ on the chain from $j$ to $i$. By Lemma 1 the subtree of $r$ is an interval containing $r$ and $j$, and since $i$ is not in it, it is $[l, j]$ for some $i < l \le r$; it ends at $j$ because $j$ is the right end of the whole span. The positions of the subtree of $r$ that are $\ge r$ form, with their arcs, a tree $B$ on $[r, j]$ rooted at $r$; this is a set of positions closed under taking heads up to $r$. Indeed, if a position $p > r$ of the subtree had a head $q < r$, the arc $q \to p$ would pass over $r$, so by projectivity $q$ would dominate $r$, while $q$ is itself below $r$; that is impossible. The other arcs form a tree $A$ on $[i, r]$ rooted at $i$ that contains $i \to r$. Both are projective as in Lemma 2, so $A \in \mathcal{I}^{\to}[i, r]$ and $B \in \mathcal{C}^{\to}[r, j]$. Conversely, the union of such $A$ and $B$ is a tree on $[i, j]$ rooted at $i$, and its arcs are projective: an arc of $B$ spans only positions of $[r, j]$, which it dominates inside $B$, and an arc of $A$ spans only positions of $[i, r]$. Uniqueness: $r$ is the child of $i$ whose subtree contains $j$. The left case is the mirror image. $\square$

**Theorem 4 (the tables).** After lines L1 to L8, for all $0 \le i \le j \le n$, each table entry holds the largest score of its set of trees, or "none" when the set is empty; with the back pointers, each entry that is not "none" names a tree of that score.

*Proof.* By induction on $k = j - i$. For $k = 0$, the only tree on $[i, i]$ is the empty set of arcs, with score 0, which is line L1. Let $k \ge 1$ and assume the claim for all shorter spans. Line L4 runs after every span of length less than $k$ has its final values, because the loop of line L2 goes through the lengths in increasing order and the spans used in lines L4, L7 and L8 are strictly shorter, with two exceptions: $I^{\leftarrow}[i, j]$ in line L7 (the term $r = i$) and $I^{\to}[i, j]$ in line L8 (the term $r = j$). Both are entries of the same span computed in lines L5 and L6 just before. The complete spans $C^{\leftarrow}[i, i]$ and $C^{\to}[j, j]$ that go with them have length 0. By Lemma 2, the score of a tree in $\mathcal{I}^{\to}[i, j]$ is $\mathrm{score}(A) + \mathrm{score}(B) + s(i, j)$ for its unique parts, and every choice of parts gives a tree. So the largest score is $\max_r (\max_A \mathrm{score}(A) + \max_B \mathrm{score}(B)) + s(i, j)$, because the parts can be chosen independently for each $r$. By the induction hypothesis the inner maxima are $C^{\to}[i, r]$ and $C^{\leftarrow}[r + 1, j]$, which is line L6. Line L5 is the same for the left case, and it is skipped for $i = 0$, where the set is empty. Lemma 3 gives lines L7 and L8 in the same way. A split whose part is "none" contributes no tree, which is why `max` skips such terms. The back pointer stores a maximizing $r$, and the tree built from it and from the trees of the parts has the stored score. $\square$

**Corollary 5 (the answer).** $C^{\to}[0, n]$ is the largest score of a projective dependency tree of the sentence, and line L9 returns such a tree.

*Proof.* A projective dependency tree over the words $1, \ldots, n$ rooted at 0 is exactly an element of $\mathcal{C}^{\to}[0, n]$. The set is not empty (attach every word to the root), so the entry is a number. Theorem 4 gives the rest. Reading the tree follows the back pointers from $C^{\to}[0, n]$; each step replaces a span by strictly shorter spans and adds at most one arc, and Lemmas 2 and 3 show that the arcs added form the tree whose score is the table value. $\square$

**One child of the root.** In the single-root version, line L4 uses only $r = 0$ when $i = 0$. Call a right tree on $[0, j]$ **single** if position 0 has exactly one child. A tree in $\mathcal{I}^{\to}[0, j]$ is single exactly when its part $A \in \mathcal{C}^{\to}[0, r]$ has no arcs, that is, $r = 0$. A tree in $\mathcal{C}^{\to}[0, j]$, $j > 0$, splits by Lemma 3 into $A \in \mathcal{I}^{\to}[0, r]$ and $B$, and the root's children are those in $A$; so it is single exactly when $A$ is single. Line L8 for $i = 0$ reads only the restricted entries of `IR`, so by the same induction $C^{\to}[0, n]$ is the best score of a projective tree whose root has exactly one child, and such a tree exists (a chain $0 \to 1 \to \cdots \to n$).

**Termination.** Every loop has fixed bounds, every maximum is over at most $n$ terms, and the reading of the tree replaces each span by strictly shorter spans, so it stops. Each incomplete span it visits adds one arc, and each complete span of positive length it visits leads to one incomplete span, so it visits $O(n)$ spans. The tests check the result against a search over all $(n + 1)^{n - 1}$ trees for small $n$, in both versions. $\square$
