A normal form for a class of objects is a restricted set of representations, together with a conversion that maps every object to a representation in that set with the same meaning. Conjunctive normal form for Boolean formulas, Chomsky normal form for grammars, row echelon form for matrices and expanded polynomials are examples. A normal form is a *canonical form* when, in addition, objects with the same meaning get the same representation, so that equality of meaning becomes equality of text: [reduced row echelon form](/reduced-row-echelon-form/) and fractions in lowest terms are canonical, but [conjunctive normal form](/conjunctive-normal-form/) is not. Converting to a normal form is the representation change of [transform-and-conquer](/transform-and-conquer/), and converting to a canonical form is [canonicalization](/canonicalization/).

## In plain words

Algorithms are easier to write when their input has a fixed shape. A formula can mix "and", "or" and "not" in any nesting. If you first rewrite it as a list of clauses, each clause an "or" of simple terms, a solver needs to handle only that one shape.

A normal form is such a fixed shape that every object can be rewritten into without a change of meaning. It does not always give one answer: the same formula can have many different lists of clauses. When the shape is so strict that each meaning has exactly one way to be written, the form is called canonical, and two objects mean the same thing exactly when they are written the same.

Rewriting into a normal form can make the object much bigger.

## The idea

Let $X$ be a set of representations (formulas, matrices, grammars) and let $\equiv$ be an [equivalence relation](/equivalence-relation/) on $X$ that says "has the same meaning" (the same Boolean function, the same solution set, the same language). A *normal form* is a subset $N \subseteq X$ together with a computable map $\mathrm{nf} : X \to N$ such that

$$\mathrm{nf}(x) \equiv x \quad \text{for all } x \in X.$$

The normal form is *canonical* when, in addition,

$$x \equiv y \implies \mathrm{nf}(x) = \mathrm{nf}(y) \quad \text{for all } x, y \in X.$$

With both conditions, $x \equiv y \iff \mathrm{nf}(x) = \mathrm{nf}(y)$: if $\mathrm{nf}(x) = \mathrm{nf}(y)$, then $x \equiv \mathrm{nf}(x) = \mathrm{nf}(y) \equiv y$. The proof that both directions matter is on the [canonicalization](/canonicalization/) page. A weaker relation is also useful: $\mathrm{nf}(x)$ may be only *equisatisfiable* with $x$ (one has a model if and only if the other has one), which is enough for a satisfiability solver but not for an equivalence test.

In term rewriting, a [normal form](/normal-form/) of a term is a term to which no rule applies. It is a normal form in the sense above when the rules preserve meaning, and it is canonical for the equivalence generated by the rules when the rewriting system is confluent and terminating.

Three questions decide whether a normal form is useful:

1. **Size.** How large can $\mathrm{nf}(x)$ be compared with $x$? Conversion to CNF by the distributive law can be exponential.
2. **Time.** How fast is $\mathrm{nf}$ to compute? Reduced row echelon form takes $O(n^3)$ arithmetic operations for an $n \times n$ matrix by [Gauss-Jordan elimination](/gauss-jordan-elimination/).
3. **Uniqueness.** Is the form canonical? Only then does equality of forms decide equivalence.

## Examples

### CNF is a normal form but not a canonical one

A formula is in conjunctive normal form when it is an AND of clauses, and each clause is an OR of literals. Every Boolean formula has an equivalent CNF: push the negations down to the variables with De Morgan's laws, then distribute OR over AND. CNF is not canonical: $(x \lor y) \land (x \lor \lnot y)$ and $x$ are two different CNFs of the same function. DNF, an OR of ANDs of literals, has the same status. The *full* DNF, an OR of one complete term for each satisfying assignment, with the variables and terms in a fixed order, is canonical, but it has as many terms as the function has models.

### The size of CNF: an exponential lower bound

Let

$$f_n = (x_1 \land y_1) \lor (x_2 \land y_2) \lor \cdots \lor (x_n \land y_n).$$

The distributive law gives the CNF with one clause $(z_1 \lor z_2 \lor \cdots \lor z_n)$ for each choice of $z_i \in \{x_i, y_i\}$: $2^n$ clauses with $n$ literals each.

**Claim.** Every CNF equivalent to $f_n$ has at least $2^n$ clauses.

*Proof.* Drop the clauses that contain a variable and its negation; they are always true. Let $C$ be one of the remaining clauses. Since $f_n$ implies $C$, $C$ is true wherever $f_n$ is true. First, for each $i$, $C$ contains a literal on $x_i$ or on $y_i$: otherwise set $x_i = y_i = 1$ and every variable that occurs in $C$ so that its literal is false. Then $f_n = 1$ and $C = 0$, a contradiction. Now, for each choice vector $s \in \{x, y\}^n$, let $a_s$ be the assignment that sets the variable $s_i$ of pair $i$ to $0$ and the other variable of pair $i$ to $1$. Every term $x_i \land y_i$ is false at $a_s$, so $f_n(a_s) = 0$, and some clause $C$ must be false at $a_s$. Suppose one clause $C$ is false at $a_s$ and at $a_t$ with $s \ne t$, and let $s_i \ne t_i$. Then each of $x_i$ and $y_i$ is $0$ in one of $a_s, a_t$ and $1$ in the other. $C$ contains a literal on $x_i$ or $y_i$, and such a literal is true at one of $a_s$ and $a_t$, a contradiction. So the $2^n$ assignments $a_s$ need $2^n$ different clauses. $\square$

### Tseitin's transformation: linear size, equisatisfiable

The [Tseitin transformation](/tseitin-transformation/) adds a new variable $t_i$ for the subformula $x_i \land y_i$, with three clauses that force $t_i \leftrightarrow (x_i \land y_i)$, and one clause that says some term is true:

$$\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n} \bigl[(\lnot t_i \lor x_i) \land (\lnot t_i \lor y_i) \land (t_i \lor \lnot x_i \lor \lnot y_i)\bigr] \;\land\; (t_1 \lor \cdots \lor t_n).$$

This has $3n + 1$ clauses and $3n$ variables. It is not equivalent to $f_n$, since it has more variables, but every model of $f_n$ extends to exactly one model of it ($t_i$ is forced), and every model of it restricts to a model of $f_n$. So the two are equisatisfiable, and they even have the same number of models. For $n = 3$: the distributive CNF has $8$ clauses, the Tseitin CNF has $10$ clauses over $9$ variables, and both formulas have $37$ models (of $f_3$ over its $6$ variables, $64 - 3^3 = 37$ assignments make some term true). For $n = 10$ the counts are $1024$ clauses against $31$, and for $n = 20$ they are $1{,}048{,}576$ against $61$. In general, a formula with $m$ binary connectives gets $O(m)$ clauses. CLRS (Section 34.4) uses the same idea to reduce formula satisfiability to [3-SAT](/3-sat/).

### Reduced row echelon form is canonical

A matrix is in [reduced row echelon form](/reduced-row-echelon-form/) (RREF) when each nonzero row starts with a $1$ (its pivot), each pivot is to the right of the pivot of the row above, each pivot is the only nonzero entry of its column, and the zero rows are at the bottom. Two matrices are row-equivalent when one comes from the other by invertible row operations; they then have the same solution sets as systems of equations. The augmented matrices

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 & 0 & 2 \\ 3 & 6 & 1 & 6 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 6 & 1 & 6 \\ 6 & 12 & 2 & 12 \\ 1 & 2 & -1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$$

look different. $B$ has the rows $r_1 + r_2$, $2 r_3$ and $r_2 - r_1$ of $A$, and the matrix of these row operations has determinant $-4 \ne 0$, so $A$ and $B$ are row-equivalent. Both reduce to

$$R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$

which says $x + 2y = 1$ and $z = 3$, with $y$ free.

*Why RREF is unique.* Row operations keep every linear relation among the columns, since $Mv = 0 \iff EMv = 0$ for an invertible $E$. In $R$, column $j$ is a pivot column exactly when it is not a linear combination of the columns before it, and a non-pivot column $j$ is $\sum_k R_{kj} e_k$, a combination of the pivot columns before it with the coefficients in its entries. Both facts are statements about linear relations among the columns, so they hold in the original matrix too. The pivot columns are linearly independent, so these coefficients are unique. So the pivot positions and all other entries of $R$ are fixed by the original matrix, and any two row-equivalent matrices have the same RREF. Plain row echelon form, without the conditions on the pivots, is a normal form but not a canonical one.

### Fractions and polynomials

A fraction $a/b$ is in lowest terms when $\gcd(a, b) = 1$ and $b > 0$. This form is canonical for rational numbers: $42/(-56)$ and $-9/12$ both become $-3/4$ ([reducing fractions](/reducing-fractions/)). A [polynomial](/polynomial/) in expanded form, with like terms combined, no zero coefficients, and [monomials](/monomial/) in a fixed order, is canonical for polynomials with exact coefficients. For example, $(x + 1)^2 - (x - 1)^2$ expands to $x^2 + 2x + 1 - x^2 + 2x - 1 = 4x$, so it is the same polynomial as $4x$. The expanded form can be much larger than the input: $(x_1 + y_1)(x_2 + y_2) \cdots (x_n + y_n)$ has $2^n$ monomials.

### Chomsky normal form is not canonical

A context-free grammar is in [Chomsky normal form](/chomsky-normal-form/) when each rule has the form $A \to BC$ or $A \to a$, with $S \to \varepsilon$ allowed for the start variable. Every context-free language has such a grammar (Sipser, Section 2.1), and the [CYK algorithm](/cyk-algorithm/) needs it. The form is not canonical: many grammars in Chomsky normal form generate the same language. No canonical form for context-free grammars can be computed, because [equivalence of context-free grammars is undecidable](/cfg-equivalence-undecidable/).

## Where it is used

- **Satisfiability.** SAT solvers read CNF, usually made by the [Tseitin transformation](/tseitin-transformation/). [3-SAT](/3-sat/) is CNF with three literals in each clause.
- **Linear algebra.** [Gaussian elimination](/gaussian-elimination/) brings a system to row echelon form; [Gauss-Jordan elimination](/gauss-jordan-elimination/) to the canonical RREF. [Hermite normal form](/hermite-normal-form/) and [Smith normal form](/smith-normal-form/) are canonical forms for integer matrices.
- **Parsing.** The [CYK algorithm](/cyk-algorithm/) works on grammars in [Chomsky normal form](/chomsky-normal-form/), made by [Chomsky normal form conversion](/chomsky-normal-form-conversion/).
- **Boolean function manipulation.** [Reduced ordered BDDs](/reduced-ordered-bdd/) are canonical for a fixed variable order (Bryant 1986), so [equivalence checking](/boolean-equivalence-checking/) of two circuits becomes a comparison of two pointers.
- **Computer algebra.** Expanded polynomials and fractions in lowest terms let a system test equality by comparing forms ([canonical form](/canonical-form/)).
- **Grouping and deduplication.** Any canonical form turns grouping by meaning into grouping by equal keys ([canonicalization](/canonicalization/)).
