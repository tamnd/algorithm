Butterfly subdivision refines a closed triangle [polygon mesh](/polygon-mesh/) by splitting each triangle into four, like [Loop subdivision](/loop-subdivision/), but it keeps the old vertices where they are. Each new edge point is a weighted sum of eight points around the edge, in the shape of a butterfly. The scheme is interpolating, so the limit surface passes through every control point. On a regular mesh the rule reproduces cubic polynomials and the limit is $C^1$, and the modified rule of Zorin, Schröder and Sweldens makes it $C^1$ at the other vertices too.

## In plain words

Start with a coarse cage of triangles. Keep every corner where it is. On each edge, put a new point: start at the middle of the edge, push it a little toward the two corners across the edge, and pull it a little away from four corners further out. Join the points so that each triangle becomes four.

Do it again, and again. The old corners never move, so the surface goes through all of them. The pushes and pulls bend each new point so that the surface is smooth and not a set of flat pieces. It is the surface version of the [four-point scheme](/four-point-scheme/) for curves.

## The problem

The input is a triangle mesh $M = (V, E, F)$ with $V$ vertices, $E$ edges and $F$ triangles. The mesh is a closed oriented 2-manifold: each edge is in exactly two triangles, with opposite directions, and the triangles around each vertex form one cycle. The output is the mesh after one step, also a closed oriented 2-manifold, with $V + E$ vertices and $4F$ triangles, in which the first $V$ vertices are the old ones. The limit of repeated steps is the [subdivision surface](/subdivision-surface/) of $M$, and it passes through all vertices of $M$.

The model is the [real RAM](/real-ram/), and the unit of work is one addition or scaling of a point. With rational coordinates and a rational $w$ the result is exact.

## The algorithm

For an edge $ab$, let $(a, b, c)$ and $(b, a, d)$ be its two triangles. Write $\operatorname{opp}(x, y)$ for the third vertex of the triangle that has the directed edge $x \to y$. The four wing vertices are $\operatorname{opp}(c, b)$ and $\operatorname{opp}(a, c)$ across the other edges of $(a, b, c)$, and $\operatorname{opp}(d, a)$ and $\operatorname{opp}(b, d)$ across the other edges of $(b, a, d)$. The tension $w$ is $\frac{1}{16}$ by default.

```pseudo id=main
BUTTERFLY(M = (V, E, F), w = 1/16)
L1  for each edge ab with the triangles (a, b, c), (b, a, d) and the wings g_1, g_2, g_3, g_4: edge point e_ab ← 1/2 (a + b) + 2w (c + d) − w (g_1 + g_2 + g_3 + g_4)
L2  for each vertex v: v' ← v
L3  for each triangle (a, b, c): add (a', e_ab, e_ca), (b', e_bc, e_ab), (c', e_ca, e_bc) and (e_ab, e_bc, e_ca)
L4  return the new points and the new triangles
```

With $w = \frac{1}{16}$ the weights are $\frac12$ for the two ends, $\frac18$ for the two opposite vertices and $-\frac{1}{16}$ for the four wings. With $w = 0$ the edge point is the midpoint, and the limit is the flat mesh $M$.

## Example

Take the octahedron with the vertices $p_0 = (1, 0, 0)$, $p_1 = (-1, 0, 0)$, $p_2 = (0, 1, 0)$, $p_3 = (0, -1, 0)$, $p_4 = (0, 0, 1)$ and $p_5 = (0, 0, -1)$, and the 8 triangles $(0,2,4)$, $(2,1,4)$, $(1,3,4)$, $(3,0,4)$, $(2,0,5)$, $(1,2,5)$, $(3,1,5)$ and $(0,3,5)$. It has $V = 6$, $E = 12$ and $F = 8$, it is a closed 2-manifold with $\chi = 6 - 12 + 8 = 2$, and every vertex has valence 4.

| Step | Item | Value |
|---|---|---|
| L1 | triangles of the edge $p_0 p_2$ | $(0, 2, 4)$ and $(2, 0, 5)$, so $c = p_4$ and $d = p_5$ |
| L1 | wings | $\operatorname{opp}(4, 2) = p_1$ from $(2,1,4)$, $\operatorname{opp}(0, 4) = p_3$ from $(3,0,4)$, $\operatorname{opp}(5, 0) = p_3$ from $(0,3,5)$, $\operatorname{opp}(2, 5) = p_1$ from $(1,2,5)$ |
| L1 | edge point | $\frac12 (1, 1, 0) + \frac18 (0, 0, 0) - \frac{1}{16}(2 p_1 + 2 p_3) = (\frac58, \frac58, 0)$ |
| L2 | old vertices | $p_0, \dots, p_5$ stay |

On this small mesh each wing vertex is used twice, and the weights add up. The midpoint of the edge is at distance $\frac{\sqrt2}{2} \approx 0.71$ from the center, and the edge point is at $\frac{5\sqrt2}{8} \approx 0.88$, so the new points bulge out toward the unit sphere through the old vertices. The new mesh has $V' = 6 + 12 = 18$ vertices, $E' = 2 \cdot 12 + 3 \cdot 8 = 48$ edges and $F' = 32$ triangles, so $\chi = 18 - 48 + 32 = 2$. A second step gives $V = 66$, $E = 192$ and $F = 128$.

## Correctness

The proof file shows four facts. First, the topology of a step is the split of [Loop subdivision](/loop-subdivision/), so the new mesh is a closed oriented 2-manifold with $V' = V + E$, $E' = 2E + 3F$ and $F' = 4F$, and the [Euler characteristic](/euler-formula/) is kept. Second, every old vertex stays at its place in all later meshes, so the limit, where it exists, passes through them. Third, the wings exist on every closed mesh, and the weights sum to 1 for each $w$. Fourth, on the regular mesh of the integer grid, the stencil gives the value at the midpoint of the edge for every polynomial of degree at most 3 exactly when $w = \frac{1}{16}$. The proof checks the monomials of degree 2 at one edge and uses a lattice map that takes each edge direction to the next one. A quartic such as $x^2 y^2$ is not reproduced.

The limit on a regular mesh is $C^1$ for $w = \frac{1}{16}$. At an extraordinary vertex, a vertex of valence other than 6, the original scheme is not $C^1$ for some valences (check). The proof of both facts uses the eigenvalues of the subdivision matrix, and the proof file does not repeat it.

## Complexity

- **One step, $\Theta(V + E + F)$ time.** Each edge point costs 8 additions, and the old vertices are copied. The lookup of the triangle at a directed edge uses a hash map, which is expected $O(1)$ per edge.
- **Modified rule.** An edge with an end of valence $n \ne 6$ costs $O(n)$, and the sum over all edges is $O(\sum_v n_v^2)$, which is $O(E)$ when the valences are bounded.
- **Space, $\Theta(V + E + F)$.** The output has $V + E$ points and $4F$ triangles.
- **$k$ steps.** Each step multiplies the number of triangles by 4, so $k$ steps make $4^k F$ triangles.

## Properties

- **Interpolating.** The limit passes through all control points and through all points of all later meshes.
- **Primal, with the Loop split.** The old vertices stay as vertices, and each triangle becomes four.
- **Not in the convex hull.** The wing weights are negative, so a new point can be outside the convex hull of its stencil, and the surface can overshoot.
- **Local and affine.** Each edge point depends on eight points near the edge, and the weights sum to 1, so a step commutes with affine maps.
- **Cubic reproduction.** On a regular mesh with $w = \frac{1}{16}$, points sampled from a cubic stay on that cubic, which gives a higher order of approximation than the midpoint rule.
- **Smoothness.** $C^1$ on the regular part, and not $C^2$. With the original rule, the limit is not $C^1$ at some extraordinary vertices (check). With the modified rule it is $C^1$ at all of them (check).

## Variants and improvements

- **Modified butterfly (Zorin, Schröder and Sweldens 1996).** If one end $v$ of the edge has valence $n \ne 6$, the edge point is $\frac34 v + \sum_j s_j u_j$ over the neighbors $u_0, \dots, u_{n-1}$ of $v$ in cyclic order, with $u_0$ the other end. For $n \ge 5$, $s_j = \frac{1}{n} \left( \frac14 + \cos \frac{2 \pi j}{n} + \frac12 \cos \frac{4 \pi j}{n} \right)$. For $n = 3$ the weights are $\frac{5}{12}, -\frac{1}{12}, -\frac{1}{12}$, and for $n = 4$ they are $\frac38, 0, -\frac18, 0$. If both ends have valence other than 6, take the average of the two stencils. If both have valence 6, use the butterfly rule. In all cases $\sum_j s_j = \frac14$, so the weights sum to 1. The implementation has it as `modified=True`.
- **The ten point stencil.** Zorin, Schröder and Sweldens also give a stencil with two more points and a second tension. The rule above is the case where the second tension is 0.
- **Tension.** A smaller $w$ makes a flatter surface, and $w = 0$ gives the flat mesh. Dyn, Levin and Gregory studied the range of $w$ for a smooth limit (check).
- **Boundaries.** On a boundary edge, the [four-point scheme](/four-point-scheme/) $\frac{9}{16}(a + b) - \frac{1}{16}(a^- + b^+)$ along the boundary is the usual rule.
- **Other schemes.** [Loop](/loop-subdivision/) uses the same split with positive weights and gives $C^2$ but does not interpolate. The [$\sqrt{3}$ scheme](/sqrt3-subdivision/) has an interpolating variant too. [Catmull-Clark](/catmull-clark-subdivision/) is the usual scheme for quads.

## When to use it

Use it when the surface must pass through the given points, for example to refine a scanned mesh without moving the measured points, or to make a smooth mesh from a coarse one in a fixed number of steps for display. Use the modified rule, since the original rule has artifacts near vertices of valence 3 and of high valence. When the surface need not interpolate, Loop subdivision gives a smoother and fairer surface.

## Pitfalls

- **Wrong wings.** The wings are across the other two edges of each triangle at the edge, not the next vertices around $a$ or $b$. The rule $\operatorname{opp}(c, b)$, $\operatorname{opp}(a, c)$, $\operatorname{opp}(d, a)$, $\operatorname{opp}(b, d)$ follows the orientation and gets them right.
- **Repeated wings on small meshes.** On a small mesh two wings can be the same vertex, or a wing can be $c$ or $d$. Add the weights, and do not drop the copies.
- **Ripples.** The negative weights make small waves near sharp features and near vertices of high valence. The modified rule removes the worst of them.
- **Old points in place of new ones.** Line L1 uses only the old points. Since L2 keeps them, writing the edge points into a separate array is enough.
- **Open meshes.** On an edge near a boundary some wings are missing. Without boundary rules the step is not defined, and the implementation rejects such meshes.
- **Inconsistent orientation.** If two triangles list a shared edge in the same direction, $\operatorname{opp}$ finds the wrong triangles. The implementation checks that each directed edge is in one triangle only.

## Implementations

The Python file in this folder has `butterfly_subdivision(vertices, faces, w, modified)`, which follows the pseudocode, `butterfly_stencil`, which gives the eight point weights of an edge, `zss_weights(n)` and `modified_stencil` for the modified rule, `ring`, which lists the neighbors of a vertex in cyclic order, `half_edges(faces)`, which builds the map from directed edges to the third vertex and checks that the mesh is a closed oriented 2-manifold, and `euler_characteristic(faces)`. The tests check the octahedron example, interpolation and the counts $V', E', F'$ and the Euler characteristic for one and two steps on a tetrahedron, an octahedron, two bipyramids and two tori, the exact weights $\frac12, \frac18, -\frac{1}{16}$ on a regular torus, the weights $s_j$ against a hand computation, the modified rule on the octahedron and on a bipyramid, random positions against the stencil written out by hand, and on a $9 \times 9$ torus the exact reproduction of random cubics on the inner edges, with a quartic and with $w = 0$ as cases that fail.

## History

Nira Dyn, David Levin and John Gregory introduced the scheme in 1990 as an interpolating scheme for triangle meshes, after the four-point scheme for curves that Dyn, Levin and Gregory had studied a few years before. Dyn, Levin and Micchelli showed the smoothness on regular meshes. In 1996 Zorin, Schröder and Sweldens showed that the original rule is not smooth at some extraordinary vertices and gave the modified rule, which became the standard interpolating scheme for triangle meshes.
