---
title: Marching squares outputs closed contours that match the bilinear interpolant in each cell
proves: [correctness]
status: full
---

We use the names of the pseudocode and of the problem statement. A sample is inside when its value is less than $c$. We assume that no sample equals $c$; then each crossing parameter of line L13 satisfies $0 < t < 1$, so each crossing point is in the open interior of its grid edge.

**Claim.** (1) The endpoints of the output segments are exactly the points on the grid edges where the linear interpolant of the two end values equals $c$. (2) Each segment has the inside corners of its cell on its left. (3) Each crossing point on an edge inside the grid is the end point of exactly one output segment and the start point of exactly one; a crossing point on the border of the grid is an endpoint of exactly one segment. (4) In each cell, two corners are on the same side of the cell's segments if and only if they lie in the same connected component of $\{b < c\}$ or of $\{b \ge c\}$ in the cell, where $b$ is the bilinear interpolant of the cell. (5) If every border sample is outside, the segments form disjoint simple closed polygons.

**Step 1: the edges of one cell.** On an edge from $p_u$ to $p_v$, the bilinear interpolant restricted to the edge is the linear function $f_u + t (f_v - f_u)$, because one of $x$, $y$ is constant there. If both ends are on the same side of $c$, this function does not take the value $c$ in the open edge; if they are on different sides, it takes it at exactly one point, $t = (c - f_u)/(f_v - f_u)$, which is line L13. Call an edge active if its two ends are on different sides.

**Step 2: the table.** Going around the cell, the sign changes from one corner to the next an even number of times, so a cell has 0, 2 or 4 active edges. A direct check of the 16 cases in the table of the page shows: each active edge appears in exactly one pair of the chosen entry and no other edge appears. With step 1, this gives (1), because every active grid edge belongs to at least one cell and every endpoint is a crossing point of an active edge. For orientation, take a segment from the crossing $P$ on edge $a$ to the crossing $Q$ on edge $b$, and let $u$ be an end of $a$. Then $u - P$ is a nonzero vector along $a$, and $Q$ is not on the line of $a$, so the sign of $\det(Q - P, u - P)$ depends only on the side of the line of $a$ that $Q$ is on and on the direction from $P$ to $u$. Neither changes when $P$ and $Q$ move inside their open edges. So it is enough to check the 14 nonempty entries once with $P$ and $Q$ at the edge midpoints: in each of them, the ends of $a$ and $b$ that are on the left of $P \to Q$ are exactly the inside ones. This gives (2).

**Step 3: shared edges.** Let $e$ be an active edge inside the grid, between cells $A$ and $B$, with inside end $u$, outside end $v$ and crossing $P$. By step 2 each of $A$ and $B$ has exactly one segment with endpoint $P$. Let $\sigma_A$ be the one in $A$, with other endpoint $Q_A$. Because $0 < t < 1$ on every edge, $Q_A$ is not on the line $\ell$ through $e$: the other edges of $A$ meet $\ell$ only at the corners or not at all. So $Q_A$ is strictly on the side of $\ell$ where $A$ lies, and in the same way $Q_B$ is strictly on the side of $B$. Suppose both segments start at $P$. By (2), $u$ is on the left of $P \to Q_A$ and on the left of $P \to Q_B$. The sign of $\det(Q - P, u - P)$ says on which side of $\ell$ the point $Q$ lies, because $u - P$ is a nonzero vector along $\ell$. So $Q_A$ and $Q_B$ are on the same side of $\ell$, a contradiction. Both ending at $P$ gives the same contradiction with the signs reversed. So one segment starts at $P$ and the other ends there. A border edge belongs to one cell only, so its crossing is an endpoint of one segment. This proves (3).

**Step 4: the saddle cases.** In cases other than 5 and 10, the inside corners are consecutive around the cell, and so are the outside corners. The table has one segment, which separates the inside corners from the outside corners. Each corner set is joined inside its own set of $\{b < c\}$ or $\{b \ge c\}$ by the cell edges between them, on which $b$ is linear with both ends on the same side; so (4) holds. Now take case 5, with $f_0, f_2 < c \le f_1, f_3$; case 10 is the same after a rotation of the cell. Let $d = f_0 + f_2 - f_1 - f_3$, which is negative here. Expanding $b$ gives

$$b(x, y) = d (x - x_0)(y - y_0) + s, \qquad x_0 = \frac{f_0 - f_3}{d},\quad y_0 = \frac{f_0 - f_1}{d},\quad s = \frac{f_0 f_2 - f_1 f_3}{d},$$

which one checks by comparing the values at the four corners: for example at $(0, 0)$, $d x_0 y_0 + s = ((f_0 - f_3)(f_0 - f_1) + f_0 f_2 - f_1 f_3)/d = f_0 (f_0 + f_2 - f_1 - f_3)/d = f_0$. In case 5, $f_0 - f_3 < 0$ and $f_2 - f_1 < 0$, so $x_0 = (f_0 - f_3)/((f_0 - f_3) + (f_2 - f_1))$ is in $(0, 1)$, and $y_0$ is in $(0, 1)$ in the same way. So the saddle point $(x_0, y_0)$ is inside the cell, and $b = s$ on the two lines $x = x_0$ and $y = y_0$.

If $s < c$, the path from corner 0 along the bottom edge to $(x_0, 0)$, up the line $x = x_0$ to $(x_0, 1)$ and along the top edge to corner 2 stays in $\{b < c\}$: on the two edge parts $b$ is linear between two values less than $c$ (the values $f_0$ or $f_2$ and $s$), and on the middle part $b = s$. So corners 0 and 2 are joined. Corners 1 and 3 are in opposite open quadrants around $(x_0, y_0)$, and every path from one to the other in the cell meets the line $x = x_0$ or $y = y_0$, where $b = s < c$; so they are not joined in $\{b \ge c\}$. If $s \ge c$, the same two arguments with the roles exchanged show that 1 and 3 are joined in $\{b \ge c\}$ and that 0 and 2 are not joined in $\{b < c\}$. Line L8 takes the joined entry exactly when $s < c$. The joined entry has segments $(0, 1)$ and $(2, 3)$, which cut off corners 1 and 3 one at a time and leave corners 0 and 2 on one side; the separated entry has $(0, 3)$ and $(2, 1)$, which cut off corners 0 and 2. So the corners on one side of the segments are those joined in the interpolant, and (4) holds.

**Step 5: closed polygons.** If every border sample is outside, no border edge is active, so by (3) every endpoint has exactly one segment arriving and exactly one leaving. A finite directed graph in which every vertex has in-degree 1 and out-degree 1 is a disjoint union of directed cycles, so the segments form closed polygons. Two segments of different cells meet only on the shared boundary of the cells, which by (1) means at a common crossing point. Two segments of one cell occur only in a saddle case, and they are disjoint. In the notation of step 4, the level set $b = c$ is $(x - x_0)(y - y_0) = (c - s)/d$. If $s > c$, the right side is positive, the curve and so the four crossings lie in the open lower left and upper right quadrants around $(x_0, y_0)$, and the separated entry joins the two crossings of one quadrant; each segment lies in its convex quadrant. If $s < c$, the same holds for the upper left and lower right quadrants and the joined entry. If $s = c$, the crossings are on the two lines through $(x_0, y_0)$, and each separated segment lies in one closed quadrant and does not pass through $(x_0, y_0)$. In each case the two segments lie in two quadrants that share no point other than $(x_0, y_0)$, so they are disjoint. A crossing point is the endpoint of exactly two segments by (3). So the polygons are simple and pairwise disjoint, which proves (5).

**Termination.** The loops run over the $(w - 1)(h - 1)$ cells once, and each cell does a bounded amount of work. $\blacksquare$
