---
title: MSAA stores at each sample the shade of the nearest owning triangle, calls the shader once per touched pixel and triangle, equals supersampling for flat colors, and the top-left rule gives each sample of a mesh to one triangle
proves: [correctness]
status: full
---

Fix an image of $W \times H$ pixels and a pattern of $s$ offsets $o_1, \dots, o_s \in [0, 1)^2$. Sample $m$ of pixel $(i, j)$ is the point $p_{i,j,m} = (i, j) + o_m$, and the pixel center is $c_{i,j} = (i + 1/2, j + 1/2)$. The triangles $t = 1, \dots, T$ are processed in the given order. For a triangle of positive area with vertices ordered so that its signed area is positive, each edge from $a$ to $b$ has the edge function $E(p) = (b - a) \times (p - a)$, an affine function of $p$ with the constant gradient $g = (-(b_y - a_y),\ b_x - a_x) \ne 0$, and the interior of the triangle is the set where all three edge functions are positive. The triangle *owns* $p$ when, for each edge, $E(p) > 0$, or $E(p) = 0$ and the edge is a left edge ($b_y - a_y < 0$) or a top edge ($b_y = a_y$ and $b_x > a_x$). The depth $z_t(p)$ is the affine interpolation of the vertex depths. Zero-area triangles own no point.

**Lemma 1 (the depth test at one sample).** After the triangles $1, \dots, t$ are processed, $\mathrm{depth}[i, j, m] = \min \{ z_s(p_{i,j,m}) : s \le t,\ s \text{ owns } p_{i,j,m} \}$, or $\infty$ if no such $s$ exists, and $\mathrm{color}[i, j, m]$ is $\mathrm{SHADE}(s^*, c_{i,j})$ for the first $s^*$ in order that attains this minimum, or the background.

*Proof.* By induction on $t$. Line L1 sets every depth to $\infty$ and every color to the background, which is the claim for $t = 0$. Take triangle $t$. Its bounding box contains every point it owns, so lines L3 and L4 visit every pixel with a sample owned by $t$, and line L4 computes the set of owned samples with their depths. If $t$ owns $p_{i,j,m}$, then lines L8 and L9 replace the stored pair exactly when $z_t(p_{i,j,m})$ is strictly less than the stored minimum. The new minimum is then $z_t$ and $t$ is its first attainer. If $z_t$ equals the stored minimum, the earlier attainer stays first. If $t$ does not own the sample, nothing changes. In every case the claim holds for $t$. The value written at line L9 is the shade $c$ computed at line L6 for the pixel center. $\square$

**Lemma 2 (shader calls).** The shader runs exactly once for each pair of a triangle $t$ and a pixel $(i, j)$ such that $t$ owns at least one sample of $(i, j)$, at the point $c_{i,j}$, and at no other time.

*Proof.* Each pair (triangle, pixel in its bounding box) is visited once by lines L2 and L3. Line L5 skips the pair when no sample is owned, and otherwise line L6 calls the shader once at $c_{i,j}$. Pixels outside the bounding box have no owned sample. $\square$

**Theorem 1 (flat colors).** If $\mathrm{SHADE}(t, x, y) = k_t$ does not depend on the position, then every sample color equals the color that supersampling with the same sample points, the same ownership rule and a per-sample depth test computes, and the resolved pixel $P[i, j] = \frac1s \sum_m \mathrm{color}[i, j, m]$ is the supersampled pixel. So the error bounds of supersampling against the exact box filter hold for MSAA at geometric edges.

*Proof.* By Lemma 1, sample $m$ holds $k_{s^*}$ for the nearest owning triangle, first in order on ties, or the background. Supersampling with a per-sample depth test computes the same nearest owner at the same point and stores its color, which is $k_{s^*}$. Line L10 takes the same mean over the $s$ samples. $\square$

When the shade varies over a triangle, MSAA differs from supersampling only in where the shade is evaluated: at $c_{i,j}$ for all owned samples of the pixel, in place of at each sample. Visibility and coverage are the same.

**Theorem 2 (the top-left rule).** Let triangles of positive area tile a region $R$: their union is $R$ and their interiors are disjoint. Then each point $p$ in the interior of $R$ is owned by exactly one of them. So a sample on an edge or a vertex shared by triangles of one mesh is written once, and coverage of the mesh adds up exactly.

*Proof.* For one edge, $E(p + d) = E(p) + g \cdot d$, since $E$ is affine. Put $q_\varepsilon = p + (\varepsilon, \varepsilon^2)$. Then $E(q_\varepsilon) = E(p) + g_x \varepsilon + g_y \varepsilon^2$, and for all small enough $\varepsilon > 0$ its sign is the sign of the first nonzero term of $(E(p), g_x, g_y)$; one exists because $g \ne 0$. With $g_x = -(b_y - a_y)$ and $g_y = b_x - a_x$, this sign is positive exactly when $E(p) > 0$, or $E(p) = 0$ and $b_y - a_y < 0$, or $E(p) = 0$, $b_y = a_y$ and $b_x > a_x$, which is the ownership rule. So for small $\varepsilon$, a triangle owns $p$ if and only if $q_\varepsilon$ is in its interior. Now choose $\varepsilon$ small enough for this to hold for all the finitely many triangles, for $q_\varepsilon$ to lie in the interior of $R$, and for $q_\varepsilon$ to avoid every line that carries an edge. The last is possible because the parabola $\varepsilon \mapsto q_\varepsilon$ meets a line in at most two points. Then $q_\varepsilon$ lies in some triangle, since it is in $R$, and it is on no edge, so it lies in the interior of that triangle, and by disjointness in the interior of no other. Hence exactly one triangle owns $p$. $\blacksquare$
